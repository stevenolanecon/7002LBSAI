Causal analysis and experiments

So far we’ve described associations and stopped short of calling them effects. This week: when we’re allowed to make that claim.

Does working from home change who quits?

Business question: you’re deciding whether to let staff work from home. Would it actually change how many people quit — or is it just a perk that changes nothing?

We have real data: a company that ran this as an actual experiment on 249 of its own employees.

The naive answer

Naive answer: compare the quit rate of employees who already work from home to those who don’t.

Why it’s not that simple: people who already work from home chose to. They might have longer commutes, different family situations or longer tenure, and any of these could explain a lower quit rate on its own. It’s the same problem as omitted variable bias in Week 6.

What we need

  • A formal definition of what “the effect of X” actually means
  • A way to know, not just hope, that two groups are comparable
  • A way to collect data where that comparison works: an experiment

The potential outcomes framework

A causal question

  • A causal question needs an intervention we can at least imagine, and variation in it across subjects. “Does training managers raise revenue?” works; without an intervention we can only describe the data
  • The treatment: the causal variable, usually an intervention – a price change, an ad, a policy
  • The subjects: who receives it. Some are treated, some untreated; both groups matter equally
  • The outcome: what might be affected. A precise causal question names both \(x\) and \(y\)

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 19

Two states of the world

Every subject has two potential outcomes:

\[y_i^1 \text{ — what happens if treated} \qquad y_i^0 \text{ — what happens if not}\]

Before the intervention, both exist in principle for every subject, whether or not they end up treated.

What assignment reveals

  • Once a subject is assigned — treated or not — one potential outcome becomes real and observable
  • The other one never happens, so it can’t be in any dataset
  • Treated subject: observed outcome = their treated outcome \(y_i^1\)
  • Untreated subject: observed outcome = their untreated outcome \(y_i^0\)

The outcome we don’t see is the counterfactual: what would have happened if this subject’s treatment had been the other way round.

The individual treatment effect

\[te_i = y_i^1 - y_i^0\]

We can never measure this. We only ever see one of the two potential outcomes for each subject, so the other half of the difference is always missing.

A worked example

A shop emails some customers a discount code. The outcome: did the customer buy something that week (1) or not (0)? Suppose, just for once, we could see both potential outcomes for four customers.

Customer Buys if emailed (y1) Buys if not (y0) te What the email did
Asha 1 0 +1 persuaded her to buy
Ben 1 1 0 nothing: he'd have bought anyway
Chloe 0 1 −1 put her off: she unsubscribed instead
Dev 0 0 0 nothing: he doesn't buy either way

In real data each customer is either emailed or not, so one of the two middle columns is always missing. If Asha was emailed, we see her 1 but never her 0, so we can’t tell whether she’s an Asha or a Ben.

What we’d actually have

A real dataset never has the y¹/y⁰ columns above — only one outcome per customer, plus whether they were emailed:

Customer Emailed? Bought
Asha Yes 1
Ben Yes 1
Chloe No 1
Dev No 0

Three columns, not five. No te, no counterfactual, nothing marked “missing” — just who got the email and what happened. This is the table a shop would actually have, and every causal question in this session starts from something that looks like it.

Heterogeneous treatment effects

  • Individual effects vary: by subgroup, by circumstance, by person
  • Men and women, small markets and large ones, new employees and long-serving ones
  • Effects that differ across subjects are the normal case

We can’t recover the full distribution of \(te_i\). But we can recover its average.

The average treatment effect

\[ATE = E[y_i^1] - E[y_i^0]\]

  • The average, across everyone, of the individual effects — even though we can’t see any individual one
  • Multiply by population size to get the total effect of rolling something out

Averages of differences = differences of averages

\[ATE = E[te_i] = E[y_i^1 - y_i^0] = E[y_i^1] - E[y_i^0]\]

We can’t average \(te_i\), because we never see it. But we can average the treated group’s outcomes and the untreated group’s outcomes separately, and the difference between those two averages is the same thing.

ATE can hide heterogeneity

If \(ATE = 0\), did the intervention genuinely do nothing?

Not necessarily. Two versions of the discount email, six customers each:

Individual effect te ATE
Email A 0 0 0 0 0 0 0
Email B +1 +1 +1 −1 −1 −1 0

Email A does nothing to anyone. Email B wins over three bargain hunters and puts off three customers who now think the brand is cheap. Both have an ATE of zero. The average can’t tell them apart, but the business would want to know: send Email B to the bargain hunters only.

Ceteris paribus, formalised

  • “All else equal” has been our working principle since Week 4. Potential outcomes make it precise: the only difference between \(y_i^1\) and \(y_i^0\) is the intervention
  • Week 6’s regression \(y^E = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 z\) conditions on \(z\): it compares observations with the same \(z\) but different \(x\). That’s ceteris paribus for the variables we include

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 19

Can regression actually deliver it?

Can we condition on every potential confounder in a regression?

Probably not. We can only include what’s in the data, and ceteris paribus means holding everything fixed, measured or not. However many controls we add, that gap stays. So the real problem is how to get a good estimate of the ATE, and that’s the rest of today.

Checking the identity against real data

Back to our four customers. Using the full table — both potential outcomes, for everyone — the identity gives:

\[ATE = E[y_i^1] - E[y_i^0] = 0.5 - 0.5 = \mathbf{0}\]

But real data never gives us that table. It gives us the one we’d actually have: emailed customers (Asha, Ben) averaged 1.0 bought; unemailed customers (Chloe, Dev) averaged 0.5. The difference between those two groups is \(\mathbf{0.5}\).

The true effect is zero. The comparison we can actually run says 0.5. Why are these different?

Something about who got emailed must be related to whether they’d have bought anyway. Averaging the two groups doesn’t fix that on its own — we need a way to reason about which comparisons are safe, and which aren’t.

\(C\) sits downstream of both: \(X\) causes it and \(Y\) causes it. Two arrowheads meet at \(C\), which is why it’s called a collider.

The rule: don’t control for it, and don’t pick your sample using it. Even if \(X\) and \(Y\) are completely unrelated, holding \(C\) fixed makes them look related.

Where does the variation in x come from?

Whatever we’re measuring, x varies across observations for some reason. That reason matters as much as the relationship itself.

  • An endogenous source of variation: whatever makes x vary is also related to y, directly
  • An exogenous source of variation: whatever makes x vary is independent of y

Some of the variation in x is useless to us — it’s really a confounder in disguise. Some is exactly what we need. The rest of this session is about telling them apart, and about one way to guarantee we only get the second kind.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 19

An exogenous and an endogenous source

Two things could be driving the variation in x. \(Z_1\) only affects x. \(Z_2\) affects x and y directly.

\(Z_1\) (green) is exogenous: a clean source of variation in x, unrelated to y except through x. \(Z_2\) (red) is endogenous: it’s a confounder in disguise, and any variation in x that comes from it will bias our estimate of x’s effect on y.

Random assignment is a way of guaranteeing only \(Z_1\)-type sources exist. When we assign x ourselves, nothing about the subject — their own characteristics, their own choices — can be the reason x varies, so every remaining source of variation in x is exogenous by construction.

Random assignment — the solution

Controlled assignment

  • In a controlled experiment, the value of x isn’t something subjects choose — the experimenter assigns it
  • That rules out self-selection: the people who end up working from home aren’t the ones who’d have quit anyway, or stayed anyway, for reasons related to their own circumstances
  • It also rules out reverse causality: y (whether someone later quits) can’t have caused x (their assignment), since assignment happened first

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 20

Why random assignment works

  • Randomisation is one particular assignment rule, and it’s independent of every potential confounder by design
  • That’s what the DAG a moment ago was really saying: randomise, and only exogenous variation in x is left. The treated and untreated groups are, on average, identical except for treatment
  • Under random assignment, the simple difference in observed averages is a good estimate of the ATE
  • No test can prove assignment was random. We believe it because of how the assignment was done, and we can check that the groups look alike on what we measured

Complications

Imperfect compliance

In this experiment, fewer than 20% of the treatment group had to go back to the office because their personal circumstances changed.

  • Assignment (what a subject is told to do) and treatment (what they actually do) don’t always match
  • Comparing people by assignment, whatever they actually did, gives the intent-to-treat (ITT) effect: “if we rolled this out with this level of compliance, what would happen?” Often the number a manager needs
  • With small non-compliance, ITT is a good stand-in for the effect of the treatment itself, just a bit smaller. With substantial non-compliance it isn’t, and the effect on those who comply (the LATE) is what we’d need instead

Being observed changes behaviour

  • Hawthorne effect: both treated and untreated subjects change their behaviour simply because they know they’re being watched — not because of the treatment itself
  • John Henry effect: the control group works harder than usual, specifically because they know they’re the comparison — the opposite problem, and just as real
  • Spillovers: one subject’s treatment affects another’s outcome, which spoils the comparison. Good design keeps treatment and control apart where possible

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 20

Internal and external validity

  • Internal validity here: assignment was random, and non-compliance was small and concentrated in the treatment group only — Gabor judges internal validity as strong for this study
  • External validity: the firm changed its policy because of this result. Would the same effect hold at another firm, in another job or another country? That’s a judgement call; the data can’t answer it

Summary

  • Potential outcomes define what “an effect” means. We never see the individual effect, but we can estimate the average
  • DAGs make our assumptions explicit: control for confounders, not for mediators or colliders
  • Random assignment makes the simple group comparison trustworthy. With observational data we can only try to get close
  • Experiments still need care: enough subjects, checked balance, and a clear account of non-compliance
