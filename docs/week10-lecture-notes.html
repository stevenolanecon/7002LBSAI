Prediction

Part One

A Different Question

Nine weeks of this module have all asked a version of the same question: what is the relationship? Does finger length predict reaction time? Does a gender pay gap survive adding education? Does working from home change the chance someone quits? Every one of those is a question about a coefficient – about β, and whether you can trust it. This week asks something that sounds similar and is actually quite different: given a case you have never seen, what is your best guess?

Estimation is about solving β̄ problems. Prediction is about solving ŷ problems. The whole of this module so far has cared about the right-hand side of the equation – which variables belong, how they combine, whether a particular coefficient is statistically different from zero. Prediction cares about the left-hand side: an estimate of y itself, for a case where y is not yet known, and much less about the coefficients that got it there.

From the workshop

“We've been concerned about the right-hand side of the equation, how we best estimate those coefficients that explain some kind of relationship between variables. Prediction is about solving ŷ problems. We are now interested in estimating that left-hand side variable – how can we best calibrate our models to make sure that we're estimating our ŷ as close to what will actually come about in the real world as possible?”

A β̄ question: if we change x, how much does y change, and can we trust that number? That has been every week so far, and last week’s causal question – does working from home actually change the quit rate – was the hardest version of it, because getting β̄ right meant worrying about confounders, mediators, and whether assignment was really as good as random. A ŷ question is different in kind: for a case we have not seen, how close does our prediction get? Not whether a relationship is real – whether a guess is good.

A recent paper puts the same point in one line: “estimation is about solving β̄ problems, while prediction is about solving ŷ problems.” Almost everything this week is that one distinction worked out in detail – in the choice of loss function, in what counts as a good model, in why a model built to explain is not automatically a model built to predict.

The business question

The case study is deliberately mundane: you want to sell your Toyota Camry through an online ad, and you need to put a price in the listing. This is not a hypothetical. You have real data on hundreds of other Camrys already advertised – their age, their mileage, their condition, their asking price – and somewhere in that data is an answer to the question you actually have, which is what number to type into the box.

From the workshop

“We want to sell a Toyota Camry – apparently they're quite popular – through an online ad. We want to know what price to put in the listing. But we're not just going to stick our finger in the air and guess. We've got access to data on hundreds of other Camrys already advertised, with lots of information about the characteristics of each one – age, mileage, condition, and crucially, their asking price.”

The naive answer writes itself: run a regression of price on everything you have, read off the coefficients, plug in your own car’s numbers, and out comes a price. It feels obviously correct, and that feeling does not survive long. Which features go in? How many? A model with more variables in it will always fit the data in front of you a little better – that is just arithmetic, the same fact that made R² useless for model choice back in Week 6 – but a better fit to the data you already have is not the same thing as a better price for the car you are about to sell.

The question underneath the question. You are not actually trying to build a model that explains the 281 Camrys in your dataset. You are trying to build one that gets the price right for a car that is not in that dataset – yours. If those two things were always the same, prediction would just be another word for fitting. They are not always the same, and the gap between them is what this chapter is about.

Three things close that gap, and none of them is a statistical technique yet – they are all groundwork. A way to measure how wrong a prediction is, and how much that should matter. A way to tell whether a model that fits well is actually good, or just overfit. And a rule for choosing between candidate models that does not simply reward adding more variables. The rest of this chapter builds those three things, in order.

Part Two

Two Datasets, Not One

The vocabulary shift that makes the rest of this chapter make sense is a distinction between two datasets that are easy to conflate, because in practice you only ever get to look closely at one of them.

  • Original dataWhat you actually have: 281 Camrys with known prices, used to build and test the model
  • Live dataWhat the model gets applied to once it exists – your own car, or the next ad posted tomorrow. You know its x’s, but not yet its y – finding that out is the entire point of predicting it
  • Target variabley, what you want to predict – price, here
  • Predictor variablesx, what you use to predict it – age, mileage, condition, and so on

    • A model is only useful to the extent that the relationship between x and y in the live data matches what it learned from the original data. That sounds almost too obvious to need saying, but nothing about fitting a regression well on the data you have guarantees that the pattern it found is the pattern that holds for the car you are about to sell. Usually it is – same market, same kind of car, same week – but not always.

    From the workshop

    “This is driving a lot of the techniques and statistics we'll be looking at today – this mismatch, or potential mismatch, between the data we have and the data we're actually interested in. We know for instance it's age, its mileage, its condition. The one thing we don't have in the live data to begin with – what is the one thing we don't have? Price is the thing we want to estimate, and we don't have it. Which obviously stands to reason, or else this would be a completely redundant exercise.”

    Behind all of this sits a true model you will never actually see. Call it \(f\): the real, exact function relating every x to the true y, for every car that exists or could exist. You do not get \(f\). You get an estimate of it, \(\hat f\), built from 281 rows of original data – and from that estimate, for any new x, a predicted value:

    \( \hat y_j = \hat f(x_j) \)

    the predicted value for target observation j – we know its x’s, not yet its y

    Everything from here is really about that hat. You never know \(f\) with certainty, the same way you never knew the true \(\beta\) in any regression this module has run. What you can do is get \(\hat f\) closer, and know when it has stopped being trustworthy.

    Part Three

    What “Wrong” Actually Means

    Before any model gets chosen, there needs to be a precise way to say how wrong one particular prediction was. The gap between what the model said and what actually happened has a name – the prediction error – and it comes with an annoying property: you cannot know it until the real outcome happens, and by then it is usually too late to be useful. You sell the car. Only then do you find out whether $6,000 was close.

    \( e_j = \hat y_j - y_j \)

    the prediction error for observation j

    Two separate things can be said about one of these errors. There is its direction: positive if you overpredicted, negative if you underpredicted, and whether that matters at all depends entirely on the decision at hand – a theme this chapter comes back to properly in Part Four. And there is its size: larger in absolute terms the further the prediction sits from the truth, regardless of which direction it missed in.

    From the workshop

    “It's basically an after-the-fact kind of thing – it's the single number that tells you how wrong a specific projection was, once you find out. But if you think about it, if you want to predict something, waiting until the actual data realises in the real world to know whether your model is good or not is a kind of thankless task, because you're basically waiting. The whole job of prediction is predicting something before it happens.”

    Three sources, stacked together

    One error term, \(e_j\), is actually hiding three different things, and only two of them are within an analyst’s power to improve.

  • Estimation errorYou never know the true β, only an estimate of it. This shrinks with a larger sample – more data helps
  • Model errorYou may not have the right model – wrong functional form, a missing interaction, the wrong variables entirely. Better domain knowledge and better technique help
  • Irreducible errorThe part no model could ever capture, even knowing the truth exactly – genuine randomness, or a variable that is simply never measured. No amount of cleverness touches this one
    • From the workshop

    “It's irreducible, genuine error – no part of the model could ever capture it, even with the truth known exactly. This could simply be a data issue: there are some variables that are unmeasurable that we can't capture. There's nothing we can do. So we're always going to get a little bit of prediction error, even if we can completely reduce our estimation error and our model error.”

    That three-way split matters later, because it is what a prediction interval does and does not cover. A point prediction is a single number – the price you actually type into the ad. An interval prediction wraps uncertainty around it, and the standard 95% version covers estimation error and irreducible error – but not model error. It assumes, implicitly, that the model itself is the right one. If it is not – if the true relationship has a shape this model cannot represent at all – the interval can be confidently, precisely wrong.

    From the workshop

    “Prediction intervals are really good, but they cover the estimation error and the irreducible error, but not the model error – because they're based on the assumption that our model is correct. I think for communicating a prediction – especially an example like this, where you're talking about price – it's actually more legible to a non-statistical mind. Anyone that hasn't even done a stats class would look at 'somewhere between 8,000 and 12,000 pounds, and I'm 95% confident about that' and go, okay, that makes sense.”

    Part Four

    Turning Errors Into a Decision

    Knowing an error happened is not the same as knowing how much it should matter. That translation – from a raw number to a judgement about how bad it is – is the job of a loss function, and two questions decide its shape: is an error in one direction as bad as the same-sized error in the other direction (symmetric, or not), and does an error twice as large cost more than twice as much (convex, or not)?

    Figure · Interactive

    Three shapes a loss function can take

    Asymmetry (penalty on overprediction)
    The symmetric, linear line is a weather forecaster’s temperature guess: being off by 4° costs exactly twice being off by 2°, either direction. The symmetric, convex curve is squared loss – OLS’s own shape, and the default for the rest of this chapter. Drag the slider and the third curve turns asymmetric: a hospital forecasting tomorrow’s bed demand, where running short costs more than a few empty beds ever would.

    The workshop spent real time on the asymmetric case before setting it aside for the rest of the day. The two worked examples expose something the symmetric default quietly hides: a loss function is not a neutral piece of mathematics. It is a stand-in for a business judgement about which kind of mistake actually costs more.

    Worked out loud, in the room. Selling your own car: a price set too low sells fast but leaves real money on the table – it is “pure lost money,” nothing recoverable. A price set too high mostly costs time, and perhaps a later markdown. Both cost something, and reasonable people in the room disagreed about which cost more – one argument ran that waiting also has an opportunity cost, since your own time is worth something too. The class landed, tentatively, on treating underpricing as the larger cost, but there was no single right answer – only a defensible one, decided before any model gets fit, not after.

    A bank deciding on a loan read more clearly. Reject a borrower who would have repaid, and the bank loses the interest it would have earned – real, but recoverable, “odd profit” forgone. Approve a borrower who defaults, and the bank can lose the entire loan outright. “Your default risk is going to be bigger than your opportunity cost – you'd have an asymmetric loss function to ensure, against giving a loan, that you're minimising the loss from bad debtors.” A bank's loss function should weight the two mistakes very differently – the same trade-off, bias against variance, that reappears when LASSO is introduced in the online session.

    Neither case is symmetric by default, and neither is squared-error by default either. Squared loss is the convenient, common choice – not the automatically correct one. The rest of this chapter uses it because it is exactly what OLS is already built to minimise, which makes the two fit together cleanly – but the shape of a loss function is itself a decision, made before the statistics start, not a fact about the world the statistics reveal.

    \( MSE = \dfrac{1}{K}\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\hat y_k - y_k)^2 \qquad\qquad RMSE = \sqrt{MSE} \)

    mean squared error, and its square root back in the original units

    Squared loss is symmetric and convex – a miss twice as large costs four times as much, not just twice – and because OLS already chooses its coefficients to make the in-sample MSE as small as possible, squared loss is quite literally the loss OLS was built for. RMSE is the more usable number day to day, since it is back in dollars rather than dollars-squared.

    Part Five

    Bias, Variance, and the Marksman

    MSE looks like one number, but it decomposes cleanly into two separate ideas, and almost every method introduced for the rest of this module – LASSO included – is really just a different way of trading one of these for the other.

    Write \(\bar{\hat y}\) for the average prediction across all K observations, and \(\bar e = \bar{\hat y} - \bar y\) for the average of the individual errors. Then:

    \( MSE = \underbrace{\bar e^{\,2}}_{\text{Bias}^2} \;+\; \underbrace{\dfrac{1}{K}\displaystyle\sum_k (e_k - \bar e)^2}_{\text{Prediction variance}} \)

    the bias–variance decomposition of MSE

  • BiasThe average prediction error across every observation – zero for an unbiased predictor
  • VarianceHow much each individual error varies around that average – not how much y itself varies

    • OLS is unbiased by construction – that was the “U” in BLUE, back in Week 4’s mnemonic. What is new this week is that unbiasedness is not automatically the thing you want most. A method that trades away a little of that guarantee, in exchange for a lot less variance, can come out ahead on MSE – and that is exactly the deal LASSO makes, later in this chapter.

    Numerically, on Model 3’s own residuals, the identity holds exactly, not approximately: bias² plus variance comes to – which, to the display precision used here, is indistinguishable from MSE computed the direct way. Same quantity, split into two pieces that ask different questions.

    The classic picture

    The standard way to make bias and variance concrete is to imagine four marksmen aiming at the same target. The bullseye is the true value; each dart is one prediction, from one model, fit on one sample.

    Figure

    Four combinations of bias and variance

    Low bias, low variance: every dart near the bullseye. Low bias, high variance: scattered, but centred on the bullseye – correct on average, useless on any one shot. High bias, low variance: consistently off-centre, but tightly grouped. High bias, high variance: the one you do not want to stand near.

    A shooter who is biased but consistent – bottom left – can still beat one who is unbiased but scattered – top right. Small, repeated misses add up to a smaller MSE than big, random ones.

    From the workshop

    “Think about it as a marksman trying to hit a target. Here we have low bias and low variance – always hitting the bullseye, and really close together. Here, low bias but high variance: on average the shots cancel out and land near the bullseye, but it's not really much use to us, because the shooter is only hitting it on average – widely varying where any one shot actually lands. And here, high bias, low variance: the shooter can't hit the target, but they're consistent – off by the same amount every time. You could argue that's actually a better model. This trade-off of bias and variance is at the heart of lots of machine learning and model building.”

    Part Six

    Meet the Data: 281 Camrys

    The workshop’s dataset is Békés and Kézdi’s own used-cars case study: Toyota Camry listings, scraped from an online marketplace and already cleaned to a comparable set – gas-fuelled, automatic, non-truck, a sensible price and mileage range. The target is `price`, in US dollars. The predictors are `age`, `odometer` (in tens of thousands of miles), trim (LE, XLE, SE), a set of condition dummies, and whether the seller was a dealer or a private individual.

    Five candidate models get built up by hand, each one a superset of the last:

    \( \begin{aligned} M_1:\ & price^E = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,age + \beta_2\,age^2 \\ M_2:\ & M_1 + \beta_3\,odometer \\ M_3:\ & M_2 + \beta_4\,odometer^2 + \beta_5\,LE + \beta_6\,cond_{exc} + \beta_7\,cond_{good} + \beta_8\,dealer \\ M_4:\ & M_3 + \beta_9\,XLE + \beta_{10}\,SE + \beta_{11}\,cond_{new} + \beta_{12}\,cylind6 \\ M_5:\ & M_4 + \big(LE + XLE + SE + cond_{new} + cond_{exc} + cond_{good} + cylind6 + odometer + dealer\big)\times age \end{aligned} \)

    five nested models, each adding mileage, trim, condition, dealer status, or interactions with age

    Fitting all five on the real data and reading the in-sample root mean squared error straight off each one’s residuals – the formula from Part Four, applied directly – gives a first real answer:

    Every model fit on all 281 cars. N vars counts distinct predictors added at each step; N coef counts actual coefficients once dummies and interactions are expanded.

    RMSE falls at every step, but not by the same amount each time. Adding odometer (M1 to M2) buys the single biggest improvement, cutting RMSE by about $. Adding trim, condition and dealer status (M2 to M3) buys a further $. The remaining trims and a six-cylinder dummy (M3 to M4) buy only $ more, and the full set of interactions with age (M4 to M5) – nine more coefficients, the largest jump in complexity of the four steps – buys just $, the smallest gain. Every addition helps. The size of the help shrinks each time, even as the model keeps growing.

    Does a shrinking-but-still-positive gain mean the bigger model is automatically the better one to use?

    From the workshop

    “Model three's root mean squared error is noticeably lower. Does it mean it's a better model to actually use? What would we need to answer that? This is just telling us about the data we have – we need to be more sophisticated than that, because essentially we're estimating a model on data and then using that same data to tell us whether the model is good. We're basically putting our thumbs on the scale.”

    A lower in-sample number proves a bigger model fits this dataset better. It proves nothing yet about whether it will fit a car that is not in this dataset, which is the only question that was ever actually being asked.

    Part Seven

    The Trap That Looks Like Success

    Comparing two models on the same data can go wrong in two different ways, and only one of them is dangerous. Underfitting is the easy one: Model A fits worse than Model B everywhere, in the data you have and in any data you don’t – the fix is simply to build a better model, and you will usually notice. Overfitting is the one that does real damage precisely because it is invisible until it is too late: Model A fits better in the data you have, and worse wherever the model actually gets used. By construction, in-sample RMSE cannot tell these two apart, because it is measured on exactly the data the model was fit to – the same structural flaw that made plain R² useless for model selection back in Week 6.

    From the workshop

    “It's really dangerous, because it looks like you've got a better model, and it's too late to tell – and then you're using your model, you're coming out with wild predictions, and your reputation is at risk. It happens because the model is too complex for the data: too many variables, too many interactions, too flexible a functional form. You're fitting patterns that are specific to the sample you have, but not the general pattern behind it – or specific to the world of the original data, but not the world the live data comes from.”

    Since there is no live data yet to check against, the fix is to simulate having some: hold back a slice of the original data on purpose, fit the model without ever showing it that slice, and then see how well the model predicts the part it never saw. Do that a few times and average the results, and you have a different number – the cross-validated RMSE – which is not vulnerable to the same trick, because it is never evaluated on data the model was allowed to learn from.

    Figure

    The complexity curve, on these 281 cars

    In-sample RMSE falls all the way to Model 5 – it cannot do anything else, since adding a coefficient can never make the fit to the same data worse. Cross-validated RMSE, measured on held-back folds each time, bottoms out around Models 3–4 and rises again at Model 5. That rise, and nowhere else, is overfitting made visible.

    In-sample fit always wants you to go bigger, because mathematically it cannot do anything else. Cross-validated fit eventually disagrees, and when it does, it is telling you something in-sample fit structurally cannot.

    Part Eight

    Penalising Complexity Without Splitting the Data

    There are two different ways to stop in-sample fit rewarding complexity for its own sake. One is to actually hold data back, which is Parts Nine and Ten. The other is indirect: keep fitting on all the data, but use a fit measure that already has a penalty for the number of parameters baked in. That second route is the BIC – the Bayesian Information Criterion – and its close cousin the AIC.

    \( AIC = -2\ln(\text{likelihood}) + 2k \qquad\qquad BIC = -2\ln(\text{likelihood}) + \ln(N)\,k \)

    both built from the model’s log-likelihood, plus a penalty proportional to the number of parameters, k

    Both criteria start from the same number – the model’s log-likelihood – and both add a penalty for every extra parameter. The only real difference is how heavy that penalty is: AIC charges a flat 2 per parameter, BIC charges \(\ln N\) per parameter, which is the heavier of the two once the sample passes about 7 observations – true of almost every dataset this module touches. Neither needs a held-out sample, which is exactly why BIC earns its keep when data is scarce: no splitting required, and quick, and rarely far wrong.

    A quiet assumption. AIC and BIC do not inherently need normal residuals – they work with whatever likelihood the fitted model actually has, which is why Week 8’s logit and probit models could report an AIC/BIC built on a Bernoulli likelihood with no normality in sight. But the specific formula above, the one statsmodels prints for an OLS fit, comes from assuming the residuals are normal: that is what turns “likelihood” into something computable directly from the residual sum of squares, with no further machinery. Using BIC this way is not free – this assumption is the actual price paid for not having to hold data back.

    Reading the table real BIC values produce for these five Camry models settles the question Part Six left open:

    From the workshop

    “It's a photo finish between these two – model four and model three. And actually our BIC has gone up for model five, because it's punishing that complexity. What you buy, in terms of reducing prediction bias, is not enough to overcome the cost of adding those parameters in. If we were just looking at root mean squared error, it would have pointed to model five – whereas BIC agrees with the cross-validation we've done. Rule of thumb: when two models predict about equally well, pick the simpler one.”

    Part Nine

    Holding Data Back on Purpose

    The direct route to the same goal is more literal: split the 281 cars into a training set, usually around 80%, and a test set, the remaining 20%. Fit every candidate model on the training rows only. Evaluate each one’s RMSE on the test rows – data no model was allowed to see while fitting. Unlike BIC, this needs no distributional assumption at all; the cost is that some of the 281 cars are sacrificed from estimation: estimation error, named back in Part Three, goes up a little in exchange for a clean test.

    train, test = train_test_split(df, test_size=0.2, random_state=1)
model3_train = smf.ols('price ~ age + agesq + odometer + odometersq'
                        ' + LE + cond_excellent + cond_good + dealer', data=train).fit()
pred_test = model3_train.predict(test)
rmse_test = np.sqrt(np.mean((test['price'] - pred_test) ** 2))

    Run exactly that code once and Model 3’s test RMSE comes out around $. Run the identical code five times, changing nothing except the random seed that decides which 20% of cars land in the test set, and the number moves around a surprising amount:

    From the workshop

    “So at the moment we've got the same model, same data, same code, but we have RMSE swinging. So a single split is partly luck, and we need to average that luck away.”

    A single 80/20 split answers the overfitting question honestly, but it also introduces a brand-new source of noise – which particular cars happened to land in the 20% – that has nothing to do with which model is actually better.

    Part Ten

    Averaging the Luck Away

    The fix is to stop choosing just one split, and instead do the split several times, systematically, so that every row gets a fair turn as test data without ever being tested on the same fit that trained on it. Divide the data into \(k\) roughly equal folds – five is the usual rule of thumb. For each fold in turn: train on the other \(k-1\) folds, test on this one, record the RMSE. Average the \(k\) numbers, and the result is the cross-validated RMSE – the quantity Part Seven’s complexity curve was already built from.

    Figure

    Five folds, five models, one average per column

    Each row is one of five folds; the red segment is that fold’s turn as the test set, the blue segments are what the model trains on. The same five folds are reused for every candidate model, so each column gets its own fair, independent average – not five separately-split comparisons.
    kf = KFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=1)

rmses = []
for train_idx, test_idx in kf.split(df):
    train, test = df.iloc[train_idx], df.iloc[test_idx]
    model = smf.ols(formula, data=train).fit()
    pred = model.predict(test)
    rmses.append(np.sqrt(np.mean((test['price'] - pred) ** 2)))

cv_rmse = np.mean(rmses)

    Running that loop for real, on all five models, with the same five folds reused throughout, gives the full picture that the single chart in Part Seven was summarising:

    Model 3 still beats Model 1 – the advantage from Part Six survives being tested on data it never trained on, which is exactly the reassurance this whole apparatus exists to provide. BIC and cross-validated RMSE now agree with each other too: Models 3 and 4 are effectively tied, and Model 5’s lower in-sample number has not survived contact with either honest check.

    From the workshop

    “We've gone the long way round to get the same answer, but we're more confident of it now, because we've done the right process – we've tested the model on data it wasn't trained on. Any predictive model has to be done this way. It's almost a cardinal sin in model-building: you do not test your model on the same data it was trained on. It didn't matter in this toy example here – but it could matter.”

    BIC vs. cross-validated RMSE, side by side. BIC needs no sample splitting, so it is not a problem in small samples – quick, and rarely far wrong. Cross-validated RMSE makes no distributional assumption at all, at the cost of sacrificing some data to testing and needing enough rows to make five folds meaningful in the first place. Here, on 281 real cars, they agree. They will not always.
    Part Eleven

    Back to the Original Question

    Model 3 is the chosen model – simpler than Model 4, tied with it on every honest measure. One more step remains before it can actually price a car, and it is a step easy to get wrong by skipping it. Cross-validation and BIC both needed some version of held-back data to make their comparison fair. Now that the comparison is over and a winner is chosen, there is no reason left to hold anything back.

    new_car = pd.DataFrame({
    "age": [10], "agesq": [100], "odometer": [12], "odometersq": [144],
    "LE": [1], "cond_excellent": [1], "cond_good": [0], "dealer": [0],
})

pred = model3.get_prediction(new_car).summary_frame(alpha=0.20)
point_pred = pred["mean"].iloc[0]
pi_lower   = pred["obs_ci_lower"].iloc[0]
pi_upper   = pred["obs_ci_upper"].iloc[0]
    From the workshop

    “Once I've made the decision, once I've looked at it and I'm going to go with model three over model four – I could go with model four – what you do then is go back with the full data set and re-estimate your model. You've done your diagnostic work, you've judged your model with various splits and tested it, but you've only done that on, at any one time, 80% of your data set. You want to use the full data set. More data will mean tighter coefficients, more precision.”

    `model3`, from this point on, means the version refit on all 281 cars – not the version trained on any one fold or any one 80% split. For the specific car this chapter has carried throughout – ten years old, 120,000 miles, LE trim, excellent condition, sold privately – that gives a real number:

    An 80% prediction interval: `alpha=0.20` is the chance the interval misses, the same convention `get_prediction()` used back in Week 5, so \(1-\alpha\) is the chance it is covered.

    Even with five models tried, cross-validated, and the winner refit on every available row, the interval is wide, and not a rounding artefact. That width is not a flaw in the method. It is Part Three’s three sources of error, made visible all at once: estimation error from a sample of only 281 cars, irreducible error from everything about a used car’s price that no feature in this dataset could ever capture, and – invisibly, since the interval cannot see it – whatever model error survives in Model 3’s chosen functional form. Telling whoever relies on this number that it is wide, before they rely on it, is not an optional courtesy.

    The assumption underneath everything

    One assumption has been quietly doing all the work since Part Two: stationarity, the idea that the relationship between x and y in the live data is the same relationship the model learned from the original data. If the used-car market shifts – a fuel-price shock, a new model year, a different city – the fitted relationship may simply not transfer, and no amount of BIC or cross-validation performed on the original data can ever detect that, because both diagnostics only ever look backward.

    From the workshop

    “No amount of statistical wizardry – looking at BIC, looking at cross-validation – can detect this. It has to be reasoned about, not computed. This is where you, being business analysts, comes into play – having a kind of domain knowledge, being able to argue for why looking at this data is appropriate for the live question. This is, to some extent, the stuff that protects your jobs from the coming of the robots.”

    The whole chapter, in one line. Prediction is a different kind of problem from the estimation this module spent its first nine weeks on, and the same line already quoted in Part One puts the whole split in one sentence: “Estimation is about solving β̄ problems, while prediction is about solving ŷ problems.” Everything in this chapter – the loss function, the bias–variance trade-off, BIC, cross-validation, the holdout discipline the online session builds on next – is really just that one sentence, worked out in enough technical detail to actually price a car.
    Part Twelve

    Many Candidates, Not Six

    The workshop built its toolbox the slow way: six predictors, chosen by hand, on 281 cars. The online session keeps every one of those tools and asks what happens once “a few variables, chosen by hand” stops being realistic – once a real dataset hands you dozens of candidates, and choosing by hand starts to feel less like judgement and more like guessing.

    The case study for the rest of this chapter is a different real dataset entirely: Airbnb listings in Hackney, London, from a March 2017 snapshot – Békés and Kézdi’s own public case study, with price in pounds (the textbook’s own write-up labels it dollars; the listings in this data are in pounds). Around 70 predictor candidates come with it: capacity, room type, property type, cancellation policy, review scores, and fifty individual amenity dummies – is there a TV, a lift, is the flat pet-friendly.

    Before a single predictor gets chosen, there is a decision about the target variable itself that is easy to walk past without noticing it was a decision at all: should the model predict `price`, or `log(price)`?

    From the online session

    “Price is usually closer to a linear relationship with its predictors when expressed in log terms than in levels – and that's why we often model the log price. Log differences are approximate percentage differences, and percentage differences in price are often more stable across very different kinds of listings than pound differences are. But choosing a log isn't necessarily free, because when we want to predict the price, we don't want to communicate the prediction in log points – most normal people on the street do not know what you're talking about. So converting it back takes a little bit of care.”

    A model fitted on `np.log(price)` predicts log price – and the naive fix, exponentiating the prediction straight back, systematically underpredicts the actual average price, because the exponential of an average is not the average of the exponentials. Week 5 covered the correction properly; it has not gone away just because the model got bigger.

    The checklist

    Once the target is settled, feature engineering is the much bigger job: deciding the list of candidate predictors, and the functional form each one enters in. Almost none of this is new material on its own – it is every decision Weeks 4 through 9 already made, just now facing dozens of candidates instead of a handful.

  • Missing valuesRandom, or systematic? Impute with a flag column rather than silently dropping the row
  • Categorical valuesBinary, or a small set of dummies – nothing new since Week 6
  • TextMine it for signal where it is informative, or leave it out
  • Functional formPolynomials, splines, logs – the same choices as Weeks 4 and 5, now applied to many variables at once
  • InteractionsWorth considering, expensive to search for exhaustively
    • From the online session

    “Building your model this way is effort-intensive, time-consuming – but it can be the part with the biggest payoff, when you get a model that's much closer to the real world, and it draws on two things at once: your domain knowledge, what's actually driving price for a short-term letting, and the statistics, which choices the data itself supports.”

    Part Thirteen

    Six Models, Built the Slow Way First

    Before any algorithm gets near the data, the online session repeats the workshop’s own exercise at a larger scale: six models, each a conscious, defensible addition to the last, built entirely by hand and judged by exactly the diagnostics Parts Eight through Ten already established – cross-validated RMSE, and BIC.

    One split happens before any of that model-building starts, and it stays untouched until the very last diagnostic of this chapter: 20% of the 4,393 listings – of them – are set aside as a holdout set, leaving as the work set that every model, every fold, and every LASSO fit in the rest of this chapter actually uses. Part Seventeen explains why that particular discipline matters enough to do first, before building anything.

    All six models, fit on the work set only. Training RMSE keeps falling all the way to M6 – it cannot do anything else – but CV RMSE and BIC tell two different stories about where to stop.

    From the online session

    “Cross-validation says that if we keep on going, maybe if we had an even more complex model, we'd see the limits of having so many predictor variables. But BIC is definitely indicating there's a cost in terms of individual model fit. There might be a trade-off here – model five might be fine, the difference in cross-validated mean squared error isn't that much. What has happened here has still been done by hand: each one of these models was decided by hand using domain knowledge, and then we used statistics to decide between them.”

    The two criteria disagree this time, in a way the workshop’s five Camry models never quite forced: CV RMSE keeps improving all the way to M6’s 65 coefficients, while BIC bottoms out at M4 and rises again for M6, penalising the jump from roughly 15 coefficients to 65 more than cross-validation ever does. Neither number is wrong. They are simply measuring slightly different things. Picking a model is as much an art as a science, even once every tool in this chapter has been applied correctly.

    Everything that has happened in this part – all six models, the comparison between them – was still done by hand: a human decided which variable to add at each of the six steps. What comes next changes that.

    Part Fourteen

    Letting the Data Choose

    With around 70 genuine candidate predictors, trying every possible combination by hand stops being merely tedious and becomes mathematically impossible. Each candidate is either in the model or out of it, so \(p\) candidates give \(2^p\) possible models – with 70 candidates, that is more than \(10^{21}\), a number with 21 zeros after it, before a single interaction or functional-form choice is even considered.

    From the online session

    “By hand: domain knowledge drives the choice, it's interpretable, but it's slow and it can be impossible with many interactions. By algorithm: let the data suggest which variables are in their place. Just to reiterate – even if the algorithm does the final selection, there's going to be an element of the groundwork still done by hand. You can use your domain knowledge to pick the key variables first, comb through candidates, cut the ones with poor coverage or no plausible relevance. The role of the analyst is not obsolete by any means – you could argue it's even more important.”

    LASSO – the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator – is the algorithm introduced to do the selecting. It is built to produce good predictions while avoiding exactly the overfitting trap Part Seven described, and it does two things at once, inside a single fitting step rather than two separate stages:

  • ShrinkageEvery coefficient is pulled towards zero, to a greater or lesser extent – this is what reduces variance
  • SelectionFor some coefficients, that pull is absolute: they are pulled all the way to exactly zero, dropping the variable from the model entirely

    • Mechanically, LASSO adds one new term to the familiar OLS minimisation:

    \( \min_{\beta} \left\{ \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(y_i - \beta_0 - \sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_j x_{ij}\Big)^2 \;+\; \lambda\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}|\beta_j| \right\} \)

    ordinary least squares, plus a penalty on the sum of the absolute coefficients

    The first term is pure OLS: fit the data as closely as possible. The second is new – a penalty sized to the sum of the absolute values of the coefficients, so a large positive coefficient and a large negative one are penalised equally. \(\lambda\) sets how hard that penalty pulls: at \(\lambda = 0\) the whole thing collapses back to plain OLS; the larger \(\lambda\) gets, the harder it pulls, and the more variables get pushed out entirely. \(\lambda\) itself is not chosen by hand – it is chosen by cross-validation, trying many candidate values and keeping whichever gives the best cross-validated RMSE, the exact same diagnostic Part Ten built from scratch.

    LASSO and BIC are solving the same problem differently. Both exist to balance fit against complexity, but BIC scores a model after it is fitted – so several separately-built candidates can be compared and the best in-sample fit picked out. LASSO never produces a score to compare candidates with at all. It alters the coefficients during fitting, so the complexity penalty is already baked into the single model it hands back – there is no second model to compare it against.
    Part Fifteen

    Why Some Coefficients Hit Exactly Zero

    Every coefficient in a LASSO fit faces the same trade, pulling in opposite directions. A larger \(\beta_j\) may fit the data a little better. It also costs more penalty, every time, regardless of whether \(\beta_j\) was already large or already tiny – and that last clause is the one that actually explains why coefficients land on exactly zero rather than just shrinking partway.

    Figure

    The penalty's slope never changes

    The penalty is a straight line through zero. Its slope – the saving from nudging β a little closer to zero – is exactly the same whether β is large or already tiny. That constant pull never lets up as β shrinks, so the moment a variable’s contribution to fit drops below it, the best move is to push all the way to zero, not partway.

    Contrast that against ordinary least squares, where the equivalent penalty (if there were one) would be proportional to \(\beta^2\) – a curve whose slope flattens out as \(\beta\) approaches zero, so the incentive to keep pushing weakens exactly when it would matter most. LASSO’s penalty has no such flattening. That geometric fact, more than any intuition about “turning off” unhelpful variables, is the actual mechanical reason exact zeros appear at all.

    Run on the full 64-variable version of M6 – every amenity dummy included. LASSO needs its features standardised first (the penalty sums raw coefficient magnitudes, so a variable measured in hundreds would be penalised far more than one measured in single digits for no good reason):

    from sklearn.linear_model import LassoCV
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X_scaled = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)
lasso = LassoCV(cv=5).fit(X_scaled, y)

    Figure

    Coefficients on standardised variables – pounds per night, per standard deviation of that predictor. Size and room type dominate, but several individual amenities survive the penalty too.
    From the online session

    “Property type of house has no prediction for an Airbnb price here. Weird, accessible, wireless internet – actually not driving things at all. That's what LASSO has done: it's literally selected which features are important and chucked out those that aren't. There is a cost here, as we explored way back in Week 4 with the BLUE mnemonic – OLS is unbiased, the best linear unbiased estimator. LASSO isn't, because of the penalty term. But that's a trade-off: it makes for having lower variance.”

    Going further. There is no closed-form solution for a LASSO fit the way there is for OLS – no formula you could evaluate by hand the way Week 4 solved for β̄ directly from covariance and variance. An algorithm solves for every β and λ together, iteratively, which is also why fitting LASSO on 70 variables is a job for a computer in a way that fitting six variables by OLS never was.

    Part Sixteen

    Why LASSO Gives No p-values

    LASSO hands back coefficients and no standard errors, no p-values, none of the classical inference machinery every regression this module has run so far has come with automatically. That is not a missing feature. It is unavoidable, and the reason is precise, not hand-wavy.

    From the online session

    “Classical standard errors and p-values are built on the assumption that the model was fixed on paper before any data arrived. That's not the case with LASSO – the model comes from the data. If we were to reapply standard errors and p-values after LASSO, that can understate the real uncertainty. It's what's called post-selection inference. It doesn't go away – you could take the model you've estimated, put it into OLS, re-estimate, and that will give you different betas and standard errors. But the variables going in were still chosen by looking at this data first.”

    The counterfactual a classical standard-error formula actually relies on is this: if this exact study were rerun on a fresh sample, would the same model come back? For OLS, where the variable list is fixed in advance by the analyst, only the coefficients would move – that is the only kind of wobble the formula was ever built to describe. For LASSO, the variable list itself would likely come back different too, because which coefficients survive depends on this particular sample’s own noise. A formula built to describe only the first kind of wobble has nothing to say about the second kind – and the second kind is not hypothetical.

    Figure

    The selection step, made visible

    Refitting LASSO on 30 different bootstrap resamples of the exact same listings – same rows, resampled with replacement each time. Every one of the 64 candidate variables got selected at least once somewhere across the 30 resamples; only were selected every single time. The rest – of them – came and went, including a few of the very variables the original single fit leaned on most heavily.

    From the online session

    “Some of these candidate variables are always selected – those that are really important, like private room and accommodates, are always selected, which kind of makes sense. But not all of them are. Each time, the model that LASSO points to is similar – not identical. That's not a problem, because the role of LASSO is predicting an outcome. We judge the model on held-out root mean squared error, not on any one coefficient's p-value. That bias–variance trade-off is exactly the right trade when the goal is predicting new cases, not estimating one specific effect.”

    Nothing here says LASSO is unreasonable to use – quite the opposite. It is precisely reasonable for the job it is actually being asked to do. For a causal question, Week 9’s own apparatus still applies unchanged: choose controls by reasoning about a diagram, a DAG, not by letting an algorithm pick them for you. LASSO is a prediction tool. It was never built to be an inference tool, and the fact that it does not behave like one is not a flaw – it is the honest consequence of what it is actually optimising for.

    Part Seventeen

    The One Honest Final Check

    Every diagnostic used so far in this chapter – BIC, cross-validated RMSE, LASSO’s own internal cross-validation – has used the work set repeatedly. That repetition is fine for choosing between models; it is not fine for reporting how well the chosen model will actually do, because by the time a model has been picked using a particular slice of data several times over, that slice has stopped being a clean test of anything. The fix, sitting underneath the whole of this chapter from the very first line of code, is the holdout set: a slice carved off before any model building begins, and touched exactly once, right at the end.

    Figure

    The full workflow, original data to live prediction

    After Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 14: “Illustration of the uses of the original data and the live data.” Everything this chapter has done so far – model selection, LASSO, the bootstrap check – lives entirely inside the work set, on the left. The holdout set, and live data beyond it, are untouched until this final step.
    1. Split the original data into a work set and a holdout set – before any model gets built
    2. Inside the work set only: training/test folds, k-fold CV, model selection, LASSO’s own fitting
    3. Re-estimate the winning model on the whole work set
    4. Apply it to the holdout set – untouched until now, for either finalist
    5. Evaluate – this is the honest estimate of how the model will do on genuinely new data

    Step 3 is not the same action for every method. For LASSO, there is nothing left to do: `LassoCV(cv=5).fit(Xs, y)` was already called on the entire work set in Part Fourteen – its own internal five-fold cross-validation only ever existed to choose λ, and never withheld data from the final fit. For the hand-built route, M1 through M6, step 3 is a real, separate action: comparing six models by cross-validated RMSE fits each one on 80%-sized folds, never on the whole work set at once, so whichever model wins that comparison still has to be refit on every row of the work set before it is a finished model – the identical point Part Eleven made for the workshop’s own Model 3.

    Two finalists go forward into the holdout set: M6, re-estimated on the whole work set, and LASSO, which needed no re-estimation at all. Neither has seen a single one of the holdout listings until this exact moment.

    Figure

    Predicted vs. actual, on the holdout set

    From the online session

    “You're not refitting, you're not peeking, you're checking one more thing first. You're taking the model through the process you've estimated, and then taking it on to data that's never been seen before, and seeing how well it predicts things. Live data is different from the holdout set – the holdout set still has a real price to check against. A new listing coming in tomorrow has no answer to check it against at all, because it doesn't exist yet. By the time a prediction reaches live data, every chance to evaluate it has already happened.”

    The holdout set is not just a nice-to-have extra check – it is the closest thing this whole exercise has to a rehearsal for the one evaluation that will never actually be possible once the model goes live. Everything before it is negotiable: which model, which features, which loss function. The holdout discipline is not.

    Summary, in one sentence each. Prediction with many candidates needs the same discipline as prediction with a few – just automated where hand-picking becomes infeasible. Choosing the target itself, level or log, is a real decision, and converting a logged prediction back takes more than undoing the log. LASSO trades a little bias for a lot less variance, and sets weak coefficients to exactly zero – which is exactly why it gives no valid p-values, and exactly why that is fine for prediction and not for inference. A holdout set, touched only once, is the one honest final check.
