Multiple Regression

Part One

One x at a Time Was Never the Whole Story

We are halfway through the module. For three chapters we have explained one outcome, y, with one explanatory variable, x. That was the right place to start, but it is rarely how the world works. Unless a business is very simple, its outcomes are driven by many things at once, and a model that only ever looks at one of them is going to miss most of the story.

Back in Week 1 we didn't only collect reaction times and finger lengths. We also asked which device each person used and whether they'd had caffeine in the last two hours. Last chapter, finger length on its own barely moved the needle. So HR now has a set of separate regressions – reaction time on finger length, reaction time on device – and someone asks the obvious question. Which one actually matters?

The naive answer is to run each regression separately, compare the slopes, and tell a story. That fails as soon as the explanatory variables are related to each other. Suppose one device tends to attract people with a particular hand size. People with small hands may drift towards a laptop as phones get bigger. Then the finger-length regression is quietly picking up some of the device effect, and the device regression is picking up some of the finger-length effect. Neither regression can tell us which variable is doing the work, because neither one asks both questions at once.

From the workshop

“What is the interaction in the real world between finger length, the amount of caffeine you've had, whether you're using a laptop or a phone? These all happen at once, so we don't want separate models to explain each one. We want one regression model – essentially one equation – that explains everything, which we can then estimate.”

Remember the data generating process: the real-world mechanism that produced the numbers in front of us. Every model imposes our idea of that mechanism on the data. If the real process involves several variables at once and our model includes only one, the process doesn't go away. Our model just fails to capture it, and the one coefficient we do estimate silently absorbs the effects we left out. What we need is a regression with more than one x. That is multiple regression, and it is the subject of this chapter.

Part Two

Why Add a Second x?

Békés and Kézdi give three reasons to move beyond a single explanatory variable.

  • Broader patternsPlain exploratory work. Adding variables lets us describe the data more fully and see how several things relate to y together.
  • Comparing like with likeHolding one variable fixed while looking at another gets us closer to comparing observations that are similar in every respect but one. This is the big one.
  • Better predictionMore of the variation in y gets explained, so predictions for new observations can be more accurate.

The second reason has a Latin name you'll meet constantly: ceteris paribus, “all else equal”. When we want to know the impact of finger length on reaction time, what we really want is the difference in reaction time between two people who are identical in every way except their finger length. Adding variables to a regression is how we control for the other things that differ between people, so that the comparison we make gets closer to all else equal.

Closer, but not all the way. Controlling for every possible driver of y is very hard. Some drivers we can't measure, some we measure badly, and some we don't even know exist. We'll come back to this in Part Nineteen. For now, the point is that a multiple regression is a better model, and a more honest one, without automatically being a causal one.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 10

Part Three

Reading a Regression with Two x Variables

In Chapter 4 the model was \(y^E = \alpha + \beta x\). The intercept \(\alpha\) is the average of y when x is zero, and the slope \(\beta\) is the average difference in y for a one-unit difference in x. With two explanatory variables the model becomes

$$y^E = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2$$

Different textbooks label the intercept \(\alpha\) or \(\beta_0\). It makes no difference: there is no x next to it, so it is still the intercept, the average of y when every x is zero. What has changed is the meaning of the slopes.

  • \(\beta_1\)The average difference in y between observations that differ by one unit in \(x_1\) but have the same \(x_2\).
  • \(\beta_2\)The same idea the other way round: the average difference in y for one unit more \(x_2\), with \(x_1\) the same.

That extra clause – “with the other variables the same” – is the whole difference. In practice, every interpretation you have learned so far carries over, and you add “holding the other variables fixed” (or “controlling for” them) at the end. The units don't change either. If y is reaction time in milliseconds and \(x_1\) is finger length in millimetres, \(\beta_1\) is still in milliseconds per millimetre.

What can change is the number itself. In a simple regression, every influence of \(x_2\) that happens to line up with \(x_1\) gets folded into the one slope. In the multiple regression, \(x_2\) has its own coefficient, so that influence is pulled out of \(\beta_1\). If \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are related, the two versions of the \(x_1\) slope will differ. The next part shows exactly by how much.

All of this extends to as many variables as you like, \(y^E = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \beta_3 x_3 + \dots\). Each slope is the average difference in y for one unit more of its own x, with all the other x variables the same.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 10.1–10.2

Part Four

Omitted Variable Bias, Step by Step

In Chapter 4 we said OLS is the best linear unbiased estimator. The word “unbiased” came with a condition: the model has to be right. If a variable that belongs in the model has been left out, you can meet every other assumption, find a slope that is precise and statistically significant, and still be wrong. The slope is biased – on average it sits away from the true value in the population. This is omitted variable bias, and it is worth seeing exactly where it comes from.

Two models of the same world

Suppose the true model has two explanatory variables, but we only fit a regression with the first one. Write the two side by side:

$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{Simple (what we fit):}&\quad y^E = \alpha + \beta\, x_1\\[6pt] \textbf{Multiple (the truth):}&\quad y^E = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 \end{aligned}$$

We care about the effect of \(x_1\). The question is how far \(\beta\), the slope we estimate when \(x_2\) is missing, is from \(\beta_1\), the slope we would get if \(x_2\) were included. The answer depends on how \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are related, so we need a way to describe that relationship.

The intermediate step: regress x₂ on x₁

Nothing stops us running a regression between two explanatory variables. Regress \(x_2\) on \(x_1\):

$$x_2^E = \gamma + \delta\, x_1$$

The symbols are new but the form is familiar. \(\gamma\) (gamma) is the intercept, and \(\delta\) (delta) is the slope: on average, how much higher \(x_2\) is for observations with one unit more \(x_1\). If \(\delta\) is zero, the two explanatory variables are unrelated. If it is large, knowing \(x_1\) tells you a lot about \(x_2\).

From the workshop

“Just think about the format here. We've got a left-hand-side variable and a right-hand-side variable with a parameter in front of it, and another parameter with no variable attached to it. This is just a regression model, but between \(x_2\) and \(x_1\). They're both right-hand-side variables, but if there's a relationship between the two, we can model that with a regression as well.”

Substitute it into the full model

Now the algebra. Start from the true model:

$$y^E = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2\, x_2 \tag{1}$$

We are going to write the whole thing in terms of \(x_1\) alone, which is all the simple regression can see. Replace \(x_2\) with what \(x_1\) tells us about it, \(\gamma + \delta x_1\):

$$y^E = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2\,(\gamma + \delta x_1) \tag{2}$$

Multiply out the bracket. \(\beta_2\) multiplies both terms inside it:

$$y^E = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2\gamma + \beta_2\delta\, x_1 \tag{3}$$

Gather the terms with no \(x_1\) in them at the front, and the terms that multiply \(x_1\) together:

$$y^E = \underbrace{(\beta_0 + \beta_2\gamma)}_{\text{a constant}} \;+\; \underbrace{(\beta_1 + \beta_2\delta)}_{\text{a slope on } x_1}\, x_1 \tag{4}$$

Look at what this is. It has the shape of the simple model: a constant plus a slope times \(x_1\). Nothing in the first bracket varies with \(x_1\), so it plays the role of \(\alpha\). The second bracket is the slope on \(x_1\), so it plays the role of \(\beta\). Match them up:

$$\alpha = \beta_0 + \beta_2\gamma \qquad\qquad \beta = \beta_1 + \delta\beta_2 \tag{5}$$

The second equation is the one that matters. Rearrange it:

$$\beta - \beta_1 = \delta\,\beta_2 \tag{6}$$

The left-hand side is the gap between the slope we estimated and the slope we wanted. That gap is the omitted variable bias. The right-hand side says it is the product of two things: how strongly the omitted variable moves with \(x_1\) (\(\delta\)), and how strongly the omitted variable affects y (\(\beta_2\)). When we run the simple regression, we think we have found \(\beta_1\). What we have actually found is \(\beta_1 + \delta\beta_2\): the true effect of \(x_1\) plus a share of \(x_2\)'s effect, borrowed through the correlation between them.

From the workshop

“If I use the simple model, I'm going out confidently talking about this estimated parameter as if it's the other one, because I haven't taken account of the other variable. My estimate is biased. It's incorrect. I'm going out into the world talking confidently about something I shouldn't be.”

Two details are worth knowing. First, equation (6) is not an approximation. For OLS estimates on the same data it holds exactly: \(\hat\beta - \hat\beta_1 = \hat\delta\hat\beta_2\) to the last decimal place, as you'll see in Part Five. Second, the intercept absorbs the omitted variable too, through \(\beta_2\gamma\). We rarely care about intercepts, which is why the slope gets all the attention.

Why it happens, without the algebra

Suppose \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are positively correlated. Observations with higher \(x_1\) then tend to have higher \(x_2\) as well. The simple regression ignores \(x_2\) and compares observations with different \(x_1\) – but those observations also differ in \(x_2\). Any difference in y between them might be due to \(x_1\), or to \(x_2\), and the simple regression has no way of telling them apart. It hands all of it to \(x_1\). The multiple regression compares observations with different \(x_1\) and the same \(x_2\), so \(x_2\) can't be responsible for the difference.

Which way does the bias go?

Because the bias is a product, its sign follows the signs of \(\delta\) and \(\beta_2\):

Direction of omitted variable bias by sign of delta and beta two
\(\beta_2 > 0\) (omitted variable raises y)\(\beta_2 < 0\) (omitted variable lowers y)
\(\delta > 0\) (x₁ and x₂ move together)Positive bias: \(\beta\) too highNegative bias: \(\beta\) too low
\(\delta < 0\) (they move in opposite directions)Negative bias: \(\beta\) too lowPositive bias: \(\beta\) too high

This is useful even when you don't have the omitted variable. If you can argue about the likely signs of \(\delta\) and \(\beta_2\) from what you know about the business, you can say which way your estimate is probably wrong.

When there is no bias

The bias is exactly zero in two cases:

  • \(\delta = 0\)The omitted variable is unrelated to \(x_1\). Then leaving it out costs nothing in terms of bias. Including it can still help, because it explains some of y and makes the estimates more precise.
  • \(\beta_2 = 0\)The omitted variable has no effect on y once \(x_1\) is in the model. Then it didn't belong in the model in the first place.

Neither condition can be assumed. Both are empirical questions. In practice, you check them by adding the variable and seeing whether the coefficient you care about moves, and whether the new variable has a sizeable coefficient of its own.

From the workshop

“If you've got two completely independent variables, you might want to include one because it has explanatory power in your model. But if it's independent, you're not running the risk of omitted variable bias. A lot of the time, though, you will be.”

A first example from the textbook

Békés and Kézdi use monthly data on a beer brand. Regress the month-to-month change in log quantity sold on the change in our own log price, and the slope is \(\hat\beta = -0.5\): sales fall by about 0.5% when our price rises by 1%. Now add the change in competitors' average log price. Our own price slope becomes \(\hat\beta_1 = -3\) and the competitor slope \(\hat\beta_2 = 3\). The simple regression was out by \(-0.5 - (-3) = +2.5\). The auxiliary regression of competitor price on our price gives \(\hat\delta = 0.83\), and \(0.83 \times 3 = 2.5\). The two calculations agree. The bias is positive because our price and the competitors' price move together (\(\delta > 0\)) and higher competitor prices mean more sales for us (\(\beta_2 > 0\)). Customers are much more price-sensitive than the simple regression suggested.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 10.3

Part Five

Watching the Bias Happen

The best way to believe a formula is to build a world where you know the answer and check that the formula finds it. We can do that with a simulation: we write down the data generating process ourselves, generate data from it, and then fit regressions as if we didn't know the truth.

The structural model of the simulation

Here is the world we create. There are 300 observations (think of them as months), and three variables, each built from the ones before it plus random noise.

$$\begin{aligned} \text{competitor price}_i &= \varepsilon_i, & \varepsilon_i &\sim N(0,\;2^2)\\[4pt] \text{our price}_i &= 0.8\times\text{competitor price}_i + u_i, & u_i &\sim N(0,\;1.2^2)\\[4pt] \text{sales}_i &= -3\times\text{our price}_i + 3\times\text{competitor price}_i + v_i, & v_i &\sim N(0,\;2.5^2) \end{aligned}$$

Read it from the top. The competitor sets its price first: a random number with mean zero and standard deviation 2 (these are changes in log prices, so zero means “no change”). We follow them. Our price is 0.8 times theirs, plus our own random adjustment with standard deviation 1.2. Sales then depend on both prices: each unit rise in our price lowers sales by 3, each unit rise in theirs raises our sales by 3, and \(v_i\) is everything else that moves sales, with standard deviation 2.5. The three noise terms \(\varepsilon\), \(u\) and \(v\) are drawn independently of each other.

Map this onto Part Four. The third line is the true model, with \(x_1\) our price, \(x_2\) the competitor's price, \(\beta_0 = 0\), \(\beta_1 = -3\) and \(\beta_2 = 3\). The second line is where the two prices become correlated, and it fixes \(\delta\). Be careful here: \(\delta\) is the slope of competitor price on our price, the reverse of the way the second line is written. In the population,

$$\delta = \frac{Cov[\text{comp},\,\text{our}]}{Var[\text{our}]} = \frac{0.8\times 2^2}{0.8^2\times 2^2 + 1.2^2} = \frac{3.2}{2.56+1.44} = \frac{3.2}{4.0} = 0.8$$

So with these particular standard deviations \(\delta\) happens to equal 0.8 as well, because our price and the competitor's price end up with the same variance (4). Change either standard deviation and the two numbers part company. With \(\delta = 0.8\), equation (6) tells us in advance what the simple regression should find:

$$\beta = \beta_1 + \delta\beta_2 = -3 + 0.8\times 3 = -0.6$$

The truth is \(-3\). A simple regression of sales on our own price should report something close to \(-0.6\): it says customers barely react to our price, when in fact they react strongly.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
competitor_price = rng.normal(0, 2.0, 300)
our_price = 0.8 * competitor_price + rng.normal(0, 1.2, 300)
sales = -3*our_price + 3*competitor_price + rng.normal(0, 2.5, 300)
sim = pd.DataFrame({'sales': sales, 'our_price': our_price,
                    'competitor_price': competitor_price})

simple = smf.ols('sales ~ our_price', data=sim).fit()
multi  = smf.ols('sales ~ our_price + competitor_price', data=sim).fit()
aux    = smf.ols('competitor_price ~ our_price', data=sim).fit()

The rng.normal(mean, sd, size) calls draw the three noise terms. Each line of code is one line of the structural model.

One sample of 300 is not the population, so the numbers don't hit \(-0.6\), \(-3\), 3 and 0.8 exactly. But notice that the last two rows of the table agree to every decimal place in both columns. The gap between the simple and multiple slopes is exactly \(\hat\delta\hat\beta_2\). The intercepts obey their own version too: \(\hat\alpha = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_2\hat\gamma\), here .

The right-hand column re-runs the whole simulation with the 0.8 replaced by 0, so the two prices are unrelated. Now \(\hat\delta\) is close to zero, the bias almost disappears, and the simple regression lands close to the truth on its own. Including competitor price still helps – it explains a lot of the variation in sales – but leaving it out no longer biases our price's slope.

“Our model, if we're including both, should be around minus three and around three, which they are. I created the variables – I'm in charge of what the real model is. This is the incorrect model, because I'm not accounting for the relationship between competitor price and our price, which I've imposed. And so this one is biased.”

Part Six

Conditioning, Controlling and Confounding

Multiple regression comes with its own vocabulary, and you'll use it constantly.

  • Conditioning on, controlling forWhen \(x_2\) is in the regression, we measure differences in y by \(x_1\) conditional on \(x_2\), or controlling for \(x_2\). Both mean the same thing: the comparison holds \(x_2\) fixed.
  • Omitted variableA variable that belongs in the model but has been left out.
  • ConfounderAn omitted variable that is correlated with both \(x_1\) and y. The slope on \(x_1\) is then confounded – another way of saying it is biased by the omission.

In the simulation, competitor price is a confounder. It moves with our price, and it moves sales. Leave it out and our price's slope is confounded.

However many variables we condition on, a regression on observational data is still a comparison of conditional means. It is not an experiment. So the language rule from Chapter 4 still stands, with one addition: say “is associated with”, not “affects” or “increases”, and say what was held fixed. “A £1 higher price is associated with about 3,000 fewer sales on average, holding the competitor's price fixed” is honest. “Raising our price by £1 cuts sales by 3,000” is a causal claim the data can't make on their own.

From the workshop

“A one pound change in our price is associated with an average decline in sales of about 3,200. Get that tattooed on your arm: on average.”

This is awkward with prices and sales, because most of us carry a mental model in which price obviously drives sales. That model may well be right. But until the data come from a controlled experiment (Chapter 9), the precise statement is about association.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 10.4

What would a big move have told us? If the coefficient had jumped from 18.6 to, say, 200, device or caffeine would have to be strongly related to finger length and to reaction time – a confounder at work. We added two variables at once, so we couldn't say which. The natural next step would be to add them one at a time and watch which one moves the coefficient. Almost no movement tells us the opposite: whatever confounding there is, it doesn't run through device or caffeine.

The formula with two omitted variables

Equation (6) extends naturally. With several omitted variables, the bias is the sum of one \(\delta\times\beta\) term for each of them:

$$\beta - \beta_1 = \delta_{\text{device}}\,\beta_{\text{device}} + \delta_{\text{caffeine}}\,\beta_{\text{caffeine}}$$

Each \(\delta\) comes from regressing that omitted variable (coded 0/1) on finger length, and each \(\beta\) is its coefficient in the full model. On our data:

The two terms pull in opposite directions and are both small, because finger length barely predicts either device or caffeine (the two \(\delta\)s are tiny). That is exactly why the coefficient barely moves.

Part Eight

Multicollinearity: A Different Problem

Omitted variable bias is about what is missing from a model. Multicollinearity is about what is already in it. It happens when two or more explanatory variables in the same regression are strongly correlated with each other.

Multicollinearity does not bias anything. The estimated coefficients are still right on average. What it does is inflate the standard errors, so each coefficient is estimated less precisely, the confidence intervals widen, and it gets harder to say anything with confidence.

The intuition is about variation. OLS learns the separate effect of \(x_1\) by comparing observations that differ in \(x_1\) but are similar in \(x_2\). If the two variables move together almost all the time, there are hardly any such observations: whenever \(x_1\) is high, \(x_2\) is high too. The data contain very little information about what happens when one changes and the other doesn't, and that is exactly the comparison the regression needs.

From the workshop

“If you've got two variables, \(x_1\) and \(x_2\), and they're moving together almost every time, there's barely any data where one is high and one is low. We're talking about a lack of variation. And one thing OLS is hungry for is variation in your right-hand-side variables.”

Part Nine

Where the Standard Error Comes From

In Chapter 5 the standard error of a simple-regression slope was \(SE(\hat\beta) = \dfrac{Std[e]}{\sqrt{n}\,Std[x]}\). In a multiple regression one new term appears:

$$SE(\hat\beta_1) = \frac{Std[e]}{\sqrt{n}\;\cdot\;Std[x_1]\;\cdot\;\sqrt{1-R_1^2}}$$

Take it piece by piece.

  • \(Std[e]\)The standard deviation of the residuals. A better-fitting model has smaller residuals and a smaller SE. Same as before.
  • \(\sqrt{n}\)More observations, smaller SE. Same as before.
  • \(Std[x_1]\)More spread in \(x_1\), smaller SE. Same as before.
  • \(R_1^2\)New. The R² from regressing \(x_1\) on all the other explanatory variables. It measures how much of \(x_1\) the other variables already explain.

If the other variables explain none of \(x_1\), \(R_1^2 = 0\), the new term is \(\sqrt{1}=1\), and the formula collapses back to the simple-regression one. As \(R_1^2\) rises towards 1, \(\sqrt{1-R_1^2}\) shrinks towards zero, and since it sits in the denominator, the SE blows up. That is multicollinearity in one line of maths: the more \(x_1\) is predictable from the other x variables, the less independent variation it has left, and the less precisely its own slope can be estimated. The formula is symmetric – the same applies to every coefficient, each with its own \(R_k^2\).

Checking it on our own regression

Take the finger-length coefficient in the model with device and caffeine. The pieces are:

Perfect and imperfect collinearity

There are two degrees of the problem.

  • Perfect collinearityOne explanatory variable is an exact linear function of the others, say \(x_1 = 2x_2\). Then \(R_1^2 = 1\), the denominator is zero, and the coefficients cannot be calculated at all. There is no way to separate the two effects, because the data never show one move without the other. Software will refuse, or drop one of the variables for you. Check which one it dropped: it may be the one you care about.
  • MulticollinearityStrong but imperfect correlation. Everything can be calculated, but the standard errors may be large.

This is the fifth Gauss–Markov condition from Chapter 4 – no explanatory variable is an exact linear combination of the others – finally doing some work. Notice that it rules out only perfect collinearity. OLS stays unbiased at any level of imperfect correlation; it just gets less precise. You'll meet perfect collinearity again in Part Fourteen as the “dummy variable trap”.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 10.5–10.6

Part Ten

Measuring It: The Variance Inflation Factor

Software summarises the new term as the variance inflation factor, one number per explanatory variable:

$$VIF_k = \frac{1}{1-R_k^2}$$

It is the same \(R_k^2\) as in the SE formula, flipped into a single number. The name comes from the variance, which is the SE squared: a VIF of 4 means the variance of \(\hat\beta_k\) is four times what it would be if \(x_k\) were unrelated to the other variables, so the SE is twice as large. A VIF of 1 means no inflation. A common rule of thumb treats a VIF above 10 as a real problem (some use a stricter 5).

In Python, reuse the fitted model. It already holds the design matrix (the columns of x values) and their names:

from statsmodels.stats.outliers_influence import variance_inflation_factor

model = smf.ols('mean_rt ~ finger_mm + device + caffeine', data=student_summary).fit()
X, names = model.model.exog, model.model.exog_names
for k in range(1, len(names)):          # 0 is the intercept
    print(names[k], round(variance_inflation_factor(X, k), 2))



All three are close to 1. Finger length, device and caffeine barely overlap in what they explain, so adding device and caffeine made the finger-length estimate no less precise. The trouble with our model was never multicollinearity. It was a weak signal and 24 observations.



The VIF trap



A high VIF does not mean “drop this variable”. If the variable is a genuine confounder, dropping it to fix a precision problem brings back the bias problem from Part Four, and that is usually the worse of the two. The honest response to a high VIF is usually to report it, keep the variable if your argument needs it, and say plainly that the estimate is imprecise. The VIF is a diagnostic, not a decision rule.

“It's not, it's over ten, I drop it. It's, it's over ten, I report it, and I explain that this could be a limitation of my model.”

  • Drop one of a pairIf two variables are near-duplicates, keep one – as long as that doesn't create omitted variable bias.
    • 
  
  • Combine themAverage them, or standardise each as a z-score (value minus its mean, divided by its standard deviation) and average the z-scores. This is the latent-variable idea from Chapter 3.
    • 
  
  • With many variablesPairwise correlations aren't enough, because one variable can be a combination of several others. Check \(R_k^2\) (or the VIF) for each variable regressed on all the others.
    • 




Multicollinearity is mostly a small-sample problem – hundreds to a few thousand observations. With much more data, the extra precision usually swamps it.

Part Twelve


Testing Several Coefficients at Once: The F-Test



One coefficient at a time, as before



Every coefficient in a multiple regression gets its own confidence interval and its own t-test, exactly as in Chapter 5:



$$CI(\hat\beta_k)_{95\%} = \hat\beta_k \pm 2\,SE(\hat\beta_k) \qquad\qquad t=\frac{\hat\beta_k}{SE(\hat\beta_k)}$$



The only change is where \(SE(\hat\beta_k)\) comes from: the formula in Part Nine, with its \(\sqrt{1-R_k^2}\) term. In the simulation from Part Five, the 95% confidence interval for our price's coefficient runs from . Zero is nowhere near it, so the coefficient is significantly different from zero at 5% – and the true value, \(-3\), sits inside it.



When the question is about a group of coefficients



Sometimes the question isn't about one coefficient but about several together. Do the location variables matter at all? Is a curve needed, or would a straight line do? Are all the education dummies jointly zero? This is a joint hypothesis: a null hypothesis that makes a statement about more than one coefficient. For two coefficients:



$$H_0:\ \beta_1 = 0 \ \text{ and }\ \beta_2 = 0 \qquad\qquad H_A:\ \text{at least one of them is not zero}$$



Running two separate t-tests doesn't answer this. Each coefficient can be individually indistinguishable from zero while the two together clearly matter – this happens a lot with correlated variables, where each one's SE is inflated. The F-test tests the group as a whole.



What the F-statistic measures



The idea is a comparison of two models. The unrestricted model is the one you fitted, with all the variables. The restricted model is the same model with the null hypothesis imposed, i.e. with the tested variables dropped. If those variables really don't matter, dropping them should cost very little fit. If they do matter, R² should fall noticeably. The F-statistic measures that fall, scaled so it can be compared with a known distribution:



$$F = \frac{\left(R^2_{U} - R^2_{R}\right)/\,q}{\left(1 - R^2_{U}\right)/\,(n-k-1)}$$



    
  
  • \(R^2_U,\ R^2_R\)R² of the unrestricted and the restricted model.
    • 
  
  • \(q\)The number of restrictions – how many coefficients the null sets to zero.
    • 
  
  • \(n-k-1\)The number of observations minus the number of estimated coefficients in the unrestricted model (k slopes plus the intercept).
    • 




The top of the fraction is the gain in fit per tested variable. The bottom is the fit still unexplained, per remaining degree of freedom. A big F means the tested variables explain far more than you'd expect from chance. If the null is true, F follows an F-distribution with \(q\) and \(n-k-1\) degrees of freedom, and its p-value is the probability of an F at least this large when all the tested coefficients are really zero. The logic is exactly the t-test logic from Chapter 2, with a different reference distribution.

The curve is the distribution of F if the null hypothesis is true. The shaded area to the right of the dashed line is the 5% rejection region. The red line is the F-statistic from the data.





One practical note. With robust standard errors, statsmodels computes the F-statistic in a slightly different way (a robust Wald test) rather than from the two R²s, so its number won't exactly match the formula above. The formula is the one to understand; the robust version is the one to report.



The global F-test



The most common F-test asks whether all the slope coefficients are zero at once. Every regression summary reports it by default, as F-statistic and Prob (F-statistic) in the top block. Here's ours:



model = smf.ols('mean_rt ~ finger_mm + device + caffeine', data=student_summary).fit(cov_type='HC1')
print(model.summary())









Békés and Kézdi's advice on the global F-test is blunt: don't lean on it. For the question “does this model explain anything?”, R² tells you nearly the same thing more simply. You still need to know what it means, because it's on every output you'll ever read.



A significant F-test: the hotels



Our class data can't reject anything. Better data can. Regress log hotel price on distance, stars and rating:



model = smf.ols('ln_price ~ distance + stars + rating', data=hotels).fit(cov_type='HC1')
print(model.summary())









Testing a chosen group of coefficients



The global F-test covers every slope. To test a subset, set up the null hypothesis yourself with f_test, after fitting the model:



model.f_test('stars = 0, rating = 0')

Part Thirteen


Worked Example: Do the Hotel Prices Need a Bend?



This example puts the F-test to work on a real modelling decision. In Chapter 4 we fitted a straight line of hotel price on distance from the city centre. In Chapter 5 we saw that a straight line doesn't fit well: prices fall steeply over the first mile or so and then flatten out. One way to let a line bend is a piecewise linear spline. The question here is whether the bends are really needed, or whether the straight line would do.



What a spline is



A spline cuts the x-axis at points called knots and fits a straight line in each interval. The lines join at the knots, so there are no jumps, but each segment can have its own slope. Here we put knots at 1 mile and 4 miles, the same choice Békés and Kézdi make, which gives three segments: the first mile, 1–4 miles, and beyond 4 miles.



A spline is built from ordinary variables, so it's just a multiple regression. For each knot, create a new variable that measures how far beyond the knot a hotel is, and is zero for hotels inside it:



$$\text{beyond}_1 = \max(\text{distance} - 1,\ 0) \qquad\qquad \text{beyond}_4 = \max(\text{distance} - 4,\ 0)$$




  Example values of the spline variables for three hotels
  
Hotel atdistancebeyond_1beyond_4

  
    
0.5 miles0.500

    
2.5 miles2.51.50

    
6 miles652

  




hotels['beyond_1'] = np.maximum(hotels['distance'] - 1, 0)
hotels['beyond_4'] = np.maximum(hotels['distance'] - 4, 0)

model = smf.ols('price ~ distance + beyond_1 + beyond_4', data=hotels).fit(cov_type='HC1')
print(model.summary())






Reading the coefficients



The model is



$$\text{price}^E = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{distance} + \beta_2\,\text{beyond}_1 + \beta_3\,\text{beyond}_4$$



Read it segment by segment. Inside the first mile, both beyond variables are zero, so only \(\beta_1\) is at work: it is the slope in the first mile. Between 1 and 4 miles, each extra mile adds one to both distance and beyond_1, so the slope there is \(\beta_1 + \beta_2\). Beyond 4 miles, an extra mile adds one to all three variables, so the slope is \(\beta_1 + \beta_2 + \beta_3\). The coefficient on each beyond variable is therefore the change in slope at that knot, not the slope itself. This is the most common mistake in reading spline output.

Setting up the test



If prices fell at the same rate everywhere, the slope would never change, so \(\beta_2\) and \(\beta_3\) would both be zero. The question “do we need the bends?” is therefore the joint hypothesis



$$H_0:\ \beta_2 = 0 \ \text{ and }\ \beta_3 = 0 \qquad (\text{a straight line is enough})$$



Under the null, the model collapses to price ~ distance, the straight line from Chapter 4. That is the restricted model; the spline is the unrestricted one. There are \(q = 2\) restrictions.



The F-statistic by hand












model.f_test('beyond_1 = 0, beyond_4 = 0')   # robust, since the model used HC1






And the second knot on its own?



The joint test says the bends matter. It doesn't say both of them do. Test the second knot alone:



model.f_test('beyond_4 = 0')






This is a good example of statistical significance without much practical significance. The first bend is the story: prices fall steeply in the centre and then level off. The second knot passes a test, but it describes the handful of hotels on the fringe of the city, and a modeller could reasonably leave it out for simplicity. In Chapter 10 we'll see formal tools for this trade-off between fit and simplicity.




  From the workshop
  
“What it's doing here is an F-test on two coefficients within your model. The model will tell you if they're individually significant. If you're interested in whether they're jointly significant, you have to do post-estimation tests like this.”

Part Fourteen


Categorical Variables, Properly



So far our explanatory variables have been continuous (price, finger length) or binary (laptop or phone, caffeine or not). Many variables have several categories instead: region, product line, job role, education level. These go into a regression as a set of binary variables, called dummy variables.



k categories, k − 1 dummies



Take a variable with three values: low, medium and high. Create a dummy for medium (1 if medium, 0 otherwise) and a dummy for high. There is no dummy for low:



$$y^E = \beta_0 + \beta_1\, x_{\text{medium}} + \beta_2\, x_{\text{high}}$$



    
  
  • \(\beta_0\)The average of y in the left-out category, low. For a low observation both dummies are zero, so only the intercept is left.
    • 
  
  • \(\beta_1\)The difference in average y between medium and low. The average for medium itself is \(\beta_0 + \beta_1\).
    • 
  
  • \(\beta_2\)The difference in average y between high and low.
    • 




The left-out category is the reference category. Every dummy coefficient is a difference from it. In general, a variable with k categories enters as k − 1 dummies. A variable with 122 categories enters as 121 dummies plus one reference.



Why leave one out? If you included all k dummies, they would add up to 1 for every observation – exactly the column of 1s behind the intercept. That is perfect collinearity (Part Nine), so the model can't be estimated. This is the dummy variable trap. statsmodels avoids it for you by leaving one category out automatically.



This works the same way whether the categories have a natural order or not. An ordinal variable has one (strongly disagree, disagree, neutral, agree, strongly agree; low, medium, high). A nominal variable doesn't (country, product type, cat, dog, horse). Both enter as dummies against a reference.




  From the workshop
  
“Cat, dog, horse, potato – they don't have an order. But you'd still have a reference category against them. Your reference categories are always important.”





Choosing the reference category



    
  
  • Substantive guidePick the category you naturally want everything else compared to: the home market, the base product, the lowest tier.
    • 
  
  • Statistical guidePick a category with plenty of observations. Every coefficient is a comparison with the reference, so a thin reference category gives every one of them a large standard error.
    • 




Start with the substantive reason, but check how many observations sit in the category you've picked.



Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 10.8

Part Sixteen


Interactions: When the Slope Differs by Group



The models so far let groups differ in their level but not in their slope. With female and age both in the model, women's wages sit a fixed amount below men's at every age: two parallel lines. But what if wages rise with age faster for one group than the other? What if the gender gap itself changes with age? Then the slope on age is different for men and women, and a model with parallel lines can't show it.



An interaction asks whether the relationship between y and \(x_1\) depends on a second variable. That second variable is sometimes called a moderator. Does customer rating relate to hotel price differently by star category? Does a drug's effect depend on the patient's age? Does financial health change how hard a recession hits a firm?



The structural model



Take a continuous \(x_1\) and a binary D (say, D = 1 for women). Add their product as a third variable:



$$y^E = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 D + \beta_3\,(x_1 \times D)$$



Work out what this says for each group. When D = 0, both terms with D drop out:



$$D=0:\quad y^E = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1$$



When D = 1, both come back in. Collect the terms:



$$D=1:\quad y^E = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 + \beta_3 x_1 = (\beta_0 + \beta_2) + (\beta_1 + \beta_3)\, x_1$$



    
  
  • \(\beta_1\)The slope for the D = 0 group only.
    • 
  
  • \(\beta_2\)The shift in the intercept for the D = 1 group: the gap between the groups at \(x_1 = 0\).
    • 
  
  • \(\beta_3\)The shift in the slope for the D = 1 group. This is the answer to the interaction question: the difference in slopes between the two groups.
    • 




Because \(\beta_3\) is just another coefficient, it comes with its own standard error, t-statistic, p-value and confidence interval. Testing \(H_0:\beta_3 = 0\) tests whether the slopes are the same in the two groups. If it's significant, they differ.

Set \(\beta_3\) to zero and the lines are parallel: no interaction, only a difference in level. Any non-zero \(\beta_3\) makes the lines converge or diverge.



Two useful facts from the textbook. First, an interacted model fitted on everyone gives exactly the same lines as two separate regressions, one per group. The separate regressions are easier to read; the pooled one lets you test directly whether the slopes differ. Second, the same structure works with two continuous variables: \(y^E = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \beta_3 x_1x_2\), where the slope on \(x_1\) is \(\beta_1 + \beta_3 x_2\) and so changes smoothly with \(x_2\).



In Python, use * between the two variables. statsmodels writes out the main effects and the product for you:



smf.ols('y ~ x * d', data=df).fit(cov_type='HC1')
# the same as: y ~ x + d + x:d

Prediction and the ŷ–y plot



Another reason to fit a multiple regression is to predict y for a new observation: \(\hat y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1x_1 + \hat\beta_2x_2 + \dots\). A good way to see how well a model predicts is the ŷ–y plot: predicted values on the horizontal axis, actual values on the vertical axis, and the 45-degree line. Points on the line are predicted exactly. Points below it are overpredicted (\(\hat y > y\)); points above it are underpredicted. Unlike R², it shows where the model struggles, not just how much.

R² can only go up when you add a variable, even one that is pure noise. It never falls. A higher R² is therefore no proof of a better model. Part Nineteen


Is It More Causal?



Conditioning on more variables gets us closer to comparing like with like, even in observational data. But getting closer is not the same as getting there.



Ceteris paribus means conditioning on every relevant confounder: every variable that affects both y and the variable we're interested in. A regression can only condition on what is in the dataset, measured the way it happens to be measured. Some confounders are measured with error. Some could be measured but weren't, because it was too expensive. And some can't be measured at all. In a wage regression, one of the biggest drivers of pay in economic theory is ability, and there is no reliable way to measure it. Conditioning on everything is impossible in general, so a multiple regression on observational data is hardly ever truly ceteris paribus. Treat ceteris paribus as a destination, not something any dataset gets you to.



The textbook's gender gap case shows both the progress and the limit. Restrict the sample to employees aged 40–60 and add more and more controls: age and education, then family circumstances, demographic background, job characteristics and union membership. The female coefficient falls from −0.224 to −0.151. Comparing a man and a woman who are the same in all those respects, the woman earns about 14% less on average. That's much closer to a like-for-like comparison than the raw gap. It still can't prove the remaining gap is discrimination: plenty of differences between people remain unmeasured.



Not every correlated variable belongs



There is a second limit, and it pushes in the opposite direction. You might think the safe thing is to add every variable that is correlated with both x and y. It isn't. Suppose you want to know how TV advertising affects sales, and you also have data on how many people viewed the adverts. Should you control for views?



No. Fewer views is part of why less advertising leads to fewer sales. Views aren't a confounder; they are a step in the causal chain from advertising to sales. Control for views, and you hold fixed the very channel through which advertising works. What's left is the effect of advertising on sales for the same number of views, which is close to nothing and isn't the question. A variable like this is a bad control.



Compare the season. Sales are seasonal, and so are advertising budgets. The season affects both advertising and sales, but it isn't caused by advertising. It's a confounder: leave it out and the advertising coefficient is biased. Control for it.

A simulation makes the point concrete. Build a world where the season drives both advertising and sales, advertising drives views, and views drive sales:



$$\begin{aligned}
\text{season}_i &\sim N(0,1)\\
\text{advertising}_i &= 0.8\times\text{season}_i + u_i, & u_i&\sim N(0,1)\\
\text{views}_i &= 2\times\text{advertising}_i + w_i, & w_i&\sim N(0,1)\\
\text{sales}_i &= 1.5\times\text{views}_i + 2\times\text{season}_i + v_i, & v_i&\sim N(0,2^2)
\end{aligned}$$



One more unit of advertising brings two more units of views, and each unit of views brings 1.5 more sales, so the true total effect of advertising on sales is \(2\times 1.5 = 3\). Now fit three regressions on 500 simulated weeks:

Coefficient on advertising with 95% confidence intervals. The dashed line is the true total effect, 3.








How do you tell the two apart? It isn't a statistical question; no test will do it for you. It takes knowledge of the business – domain knowledge. One useful check: could this variable be an outcome of the thing you're studying? Views could be the dependent variable in a regression on advertising. That's the warning sign. We'll draw these diagrams properly, and give them their proper name, in Chapter 9.




  From the workshop
  
“This is where you become a sophisticated analyst. You've got, say, 31 variables. You wouldn't just throw them all in and say, I've got as many variables as possible and my R-squared has gone up – it always goes up. You have to think about why every variable should be in there. That isn't necessarily a statistical question. It's your knowledge, your business knowledge.”





Throwing in every available variable has a name: a kitchen-sink regression. It risks bad controls, it invites overfitting, and it makes the model harder to explain. Aim for the simplest model that answers the question.

Summary and What's Next



    
  
  • Multiple regressionA linear model with several x variables. Each slope compares observations that differ in one variable while being the same in the others.
    • 
  
  • Omitted variable biasLeave out a variable that belongs, and the slope you estimate is \(\beta_1 + \delta\beta_2\) instead of \(\beta_1\). The bias is zero only if the omitted variable is unrelated to \(x_1\) or doesn't affect y.
    • 
  
  • MulticollinearityCorrelated explanatory variables inflate standard errors through \(\sqrt{1-R_k^2}\). Estimates stay unbiased. A high VIF is a reason to report, not automatically to drop.
    • 
  
  • F-testsTest several coefficients at once by comparing the fit with and without them.
    • 
  
  • Categorical variablesk categories enter as k − 1 dummies, each a difference from the reference category.
    • 
  
  • InteractionsLet the slope differ by group. \(\beta_3\) is the difference in slopes, with its own test.
    • 
  
  • CausalityMore controls get us closer to like-for-like, never all the way. Bad controls, which sit in the causal chain, should stay out.
    •

Whether an employee leaves, whether a customer buys, whether a patient is healthy. That needs models for binary outcomes – the linear probability model, logit and probit – and everything in this chapter carries straight over: controls, omitted variable bias, dummies and interactions all work the same way.
