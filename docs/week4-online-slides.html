Finding a good deal among hotels

This week’s regression tools – bin-scatter, LOWESS, OLS, residuals, R² – on one real pricing question.

The question

You’re booking a hotel in Vienna for a weekday night. You want somewhere central, but you don’t want to overpay.

  • y = price per night (€)
  • x = distance from the city centre (miles)

A good deal isn’t simply a cheap hotel – cheap hotels are often far out. It’s a hotel that’s cheap for its location: priced below what we’d expect, given how far it is from the centre.

That “what we’d expect” is exactly what a regression gives us.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Data Analysis for Business, Economics, and Policy, Ch. 7

The data: one night in Vienna

  • Prices scraped from a booking site for a weekday night in November 2017 – accommodation prices in Vienna
  • We keep the textbook’s sample: hotels (not apartments), actually in Vienna, 3 to 4 stars, and no price above €600 (treated as an error)

That leaves hotels.

Show the Python code

Reading the line

html`<ul>
<li><strong>Intercept ≈ €${stats.alpha.toFixed(0)}:</strong> the predicted price of a hotel right in the city centre (distance = 0)</li>
<li><strong>Slope ≈ ${stats.beta.toFixed(1)}:</strong> hotels <strong>1 mile further out</strong> are, on average, <strong>€${(-stats.beta).toFixed(0)} cheaper</strong> in our data</li>
</ul>`

Note the careful wording: “on average, in our data”. The slope is a comparison between hotels at different distances, not what would happen to one hotel’s price if it moved.

The line is an average slope: it misses the steep drop in the first mile and is too steep further out. Not a bad approximation overall – but next week’s online session asks whether a different shape would do better.

Residuals

Each hotel’s residual is its actual price minus the price the line predicts: \(e_i = y_i - \hat y_i\).

accessibleChart(Plot.plot({
  height: 280, width: 900, marginLeft: 50,
  x: { label: "Residual (€): actual minus predicted price" }, y: { label: "Hotels" },
  marks: [
    Plot.rectY(hotels, Plot.binX({ y: "count" }, { x: "residual", thresholds: 30, fill: "#457b9d", inset: 1 })),
    Plot.ruleX([0], { stroke: "#e63946", strokeWidth: 2, strokeDasharray: "4,3" }),
    Plot.ruleY([0])
  ]
}), "Histogram of the regression residuals: centred on zero, mostly between minus 50 and plus 50 euros, with a few large positive residuals.")
  • Negative residual: cheaper than its location predicts – a candidate good deal
  • Positive residual: more expensive than its location predicts
  • They average exactly zero – OLS guarantees it, as we saw in the workshop
Show the Python code 
codeBox(`hotels['predicted'] = model.fittedvalues
hotels['residual']  = model.resid           # = price - predicted`)

How good is the fit?

html`<p style="font-family:sans-serif;font-size:1.1em;text-align:center;margin-top:30px;">R² = <strong style="color:#e63946;font-size:1.4em;">${stats.r2.toFixed(2)}</strong></p>`
html`<ul>
<li>Distance explains about <strong>${(stats.r2*100).toFixed(0)}%</strong> of the variation in hotel prices; the other <strong>${(100 - stats.r2*100).toFixed(0)}%</strong> is left unexplained</li>
<li>For a single x variable in cross-section data, that's not unusual</li>
<li>And it's still the <strong>best-fitting line</strong> – no other straight line does better</li>
</ul>`

For finding deals, R² isn’t the point: we only need the line to give a sensible benchmark for each location. But 84% unexplained is a warning that plenty besides distance drives price.

Interpolation vs. extrapolation

The furthest hotel in our sample is miles from the centre.

Pause. Use the line to predict the price of a hotel 10 miles out. Would you trust the answer?

html`<p style="font-family:sans-serif;font-size:1.05em;">${stats.alpha.toFixed(2)} + (${stats.beta.toFixed(2)} × 10) = <strong style="color:#e63946;font-size:1.25em;">€${stats.pred10.toFixed(0)}</strong></p>`

A negative price. The line hits zero at about miles, well beyond our data. Predicting inside the range of x we observed is interpolation; outside it is extrapolation, and nothing in the data tells us the pattern carries on.

Is it causal?

The slope is . Does being further out cause a hotel to be cheaper?

  • Partly, plausibly: guests pay for location, so central rooms command a premium
  • But other things differ with distance too: central hotels may be newer, bigger, better rated or more often 4-star

So the slope mixes the effect of location with everything else that comes with it. For finding a deal that’s fine – we only want a benchmark. For answering “what if this hotel moved?” it isn’t, and Week 6 shows how to start holding other things fixed.

Bring to the Q&A

  1. Before booking one of the five “best deals”, what else would you want to know about it? Check its rating against the sample average.
  2. Which hotel has the largest positive residual? Is it really overpriced, or could something else explain it?
  3. The line predicts a negative price at 10 miles. Where exactly did it go wrong, and what would you do instead?
  4. R² is only 0.16. Does that make this regression useless for finding deals?
  5. Fit the same regression separately for 3-star and 4-star hotels. Does your list of best deals change?
