Causal analysis and experiments

Every regression this module has described association carefully, and stopped short of claiming an effect. This week: a formal framework for when you’re allowed to make that claim.

Does working from home change who quits?

Business question: you’re deciding whether to let staff work from home. Would it actually change how many people quit — or is it just a perk that changes nothing?

We have real data: a company that ran this as an actual experiment on 249 of its own employees.

The naive answer

Naive answer: compare the quit rate of employees who already work from home to those who don’t.

Why it’s not that simple: people who already work from home chose to. They might have longer commutes, different family situations, different tenure — any of which could explain a lower quit rate on its own. This is exactly the naive comparison Week 6 taught us to distrust.

What we need

  • A formal definition of what “the effect of X” actually means
  • A way to know, not just hope, that two groups are comparable
  • The tools to work with data collected this way — an experiment — properly

The potential outcomes framework

What makes a question causal

  • Causality requires two things: an intervention that’s at least conceptually possible, and variation in it across subjects
  • “Do vitamins have a beneficial health effect?” — intervention: taking vitamins or not
  • “Does better management yield greater revenue?” — intervention: replacing or training managers
  • “Does a major industrial investment affect nearby house prices?” — intervention: the investment itself
  • If neither an intervention nor variation is conceivable, the question isn’t answerable causally — it might still be worth describing, just not with this toolkit

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 19

Treatment, subjects, outcome

  • The causal variable — usually an intervention: a price change, an ad, a policy change. Also called the treatment
  • The subjects — who or what receives it. Some are treated, some are untreated; both groups matter equally
  • The outcome — what might be affected
  • A precise causal question names both: what’s \(Y\)? What’s \(X\)?

Two states of the world

Every subject has two potential outcomes:

\[y_i^1 \text{ — what happens if treated} \qquad y_i^0 \text{ — what happens if not}\]

Before the intervention happens, both exist — hypothetically, for every subject, whether or not they’ll actually end up treated.

What assignment reveals

  • Once a subject is assigned — treated or not — one potential outcome becomes real and observable
  • The other stops being knowable. It isn’t hidden somewhere in the data; it never gets realised at all
  • Treated subject: observed outcome = their treated outcome \(y_i^1\)
  • Untreated subject: observed outcome = their untreated outcome \(y_i^0\)

The unobserved one is the counterfactual — what would have happened in the universe where this subject’s treatment status was flipped. It never exists in any dataset, no matter how good.

The individual treatment effect

\[te_i = y_i^1 - y_i^0\]

This can never be measured. Not “hard to measure” — impossible, for anyone, ever, from data alone. We only ever see one of the two potential outcomes per subject. This is a missing-data problem with no fix.

A worked example

  • Outcome: did the subject convert (1) or not (0)?
  • \(te_i\) can only take three values: \(+1\) (converted because of treatment), \(-1\) (converted despite not being treated — treatment would have stopped it), or \(0\) (same outcome either way)
  • For any specific person, we will never know which of the three is true — only ever one of \(y_i^1, y_i^0\) is observed for them

Heterogeneous treatment effects

  • Individual effects vary, of course — by subgroup, by circumstance, by person
  • Men vs. women, small markets vs. large ones, new employees vs. long-tenured ones
  • The possibility that effects differ across subjects is the normal case, not an edge case worth ignoring

We can’t recover the full distribution of \(te_i\). But we can recover its average.

The average treatment effect

\[ATE = E[y_i^1] - E[y_i^0]\]

  • The average, across everyone, of the individual effects — even though we can’t see any individual one
  • Multiply by population size to get the total effect of rolling something out

Averages of differences = differences of averages

\[ATE = E[te_i] = E[y_i^1 - y_i^0] = E[y_i^1] - E[y_i^0]\]

This identity is what rescues the whole framework. We can’t average something we can’t observe (\(te_i\), person by person). But we can separately average the treated group’s outcomes and the untreated group’s outcomes — and their difference equals the same thing.

ATE can hide heterogeneity

If \(ATE = 0\), did the intervention genuinely do nothing?

Not necessarily. \(ATE = 0\) is consistent with every individual effect being exactly zero — or with the intervention helping half the subjects and hurting the other half by equal amounts. The average alone can’t tell these apart.

Subgroups and ATET

  • ATE can be calculated for any subgroup, not just the whole population
  • One especially useful subgroup: the treated group itself
  • \(ATET\) — average treatment effect on the treated — answers “was this decision worth it, for the people it was actually applied to?” rather than “what would it do to a randomly chosen person?”

Under random assignment, \(ATE = ATET\) — Section C explains why.

When X isn’t just on or off

  • Not every intervention is binary. Examples: prices, advertising budgets, dosages, days per week working from home
  • These are quantitative causal variables — the intervention doesn’t just happen or not, its size varies too

A series of individual effects, not one

  • With a quantitative \(x\), there’s no single \(te_i\) — the effect depends on how much \(x\) changes, and where you start from
  • Before we can even average, we need an extra step: define the effect per unit change in \(x\), for each subject
  • Everything else — heterogeneity, ATE, the same “average of differences = difference of averages” identity — carries over once that per-unit effect is defined

Our case study today is binary (WFH: yes or no) precisely because it keeps this first pass simple. The same logic extends to “how many days” — just with one extra step.

Ceteris paribus, formalised

  • “All else equal” has been this module’s working principle since Week 4. Potential outcomes is the same idea, made precise: the difference between \(y_i^1\) and \(y_i^0\) is the intervention and only the intervention — nothing else about the subject is allowed to differ
  • The question of what needs to stay fixed and what’s allowed to vary is exactly the causal question — there’s no way to answer it from the data alone

Ceteris paribus vs. multivariate regression

Recall Week 6’s regression:

\[y^E = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 z\]

  • In regression, we condition on \(z\): compare two observations with the same \(z\) but different \(x\)
  • That comparison is exactly what ceteris paribus means — holding \(z\) fixed, letting only \(x\) vary

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 19

Can regression actually deliver it?

Can we condition on every potential confounder in a regression?

Probably not. We can only include what we actually observed in the data — and ceteris paribus, strictly, means holding everything fixed, measured or not. This is the honest gap between the theoretical ideal and any real regression, no matter how many controls it has.

What to do?

  • \(te_i\) can never be calculated or averaged directly, and true ceteris paribus is a theoretical concept we can only approximate — how to get a good estimate of ATE is the central technical problem this module keeps returning to
  • Everything from here is about closing that gap as much as the data allows

\(M\) sits downstream of \(X\), on the path to \(Y\) — the mechanism through which the effect happens, not a separate cause of it.

The rule: do not control for it. Controlling for \(M\) removes part of the very effect you’re trying to measure — this is a bad control: \(M\) itself could be the dependent variable were we interested in the casual impact of \(X\) on \(M\).

Confounder or mediator — which is it here?

Back to working from home. Suppose it reduces commute stress, and commute stress affects whether someone quits. Is commute stress a confounder of the WFH → quit relationship, or a mediator?

A mediator — it sits downstream of the treatment, part of how WFH works, not a pre-existing difference between the groups. This is exactly why Section D’s regression compared quit rates by assignment alone, with no other controls: random assignment already handles the confounders, and there’s no mediator sitting between assignment and outcome to accidentally control away.

Random assignment — the solution

Why random assignment works

  • If treatment is assigned independently of potential outcomes — for a reason that has nothing to do with what would have happened either way — then the treated and untreated groups are, on average, identical except for treatment
  • Under random assignment, the simple difference in observed averages is a good estimate of the ATE
  • This is a theoretical guarantee, not something you can verify directly in any one dataset — there’s no test that proves assignment was random, only reasons to believe it

Experimenting versus conditioning

  • Experimental data: variation in \(x\) is controlled by the experimenter — deliberately made exogenous
  • Observational data: variation in \(x\) comes from wherever it comes from — some of it exogenous, some of it tangled up with \(y\) itself. The best we can do is condition on what we can measure, exactly as Weeks 4–8 have been doing
  • A controlled experiment is the most direct way to get exogenous variation in \(x\). Everything else is a second-best attempt at the same goal

This is why the rest of this session focuses on running experiments well — and why the online session comes back to what’s left when you can’t.

Designing and running the experiment

The experiment family

  • Field experiments — as close to the real decision environment as possible. Our case study today: a real firm, its own employees
  • Lab experiments — controlled but artificial; higher control, lower realism
  • A/B tests — a specific kind of field experiment, comparing versions of the same product (a page, an ad, an email). Easy to scale, large N

Most firms still don’t experiment, even though the potential is huge — internet firms are the biggest exception, running randomised tests on their own customers constantly.

Making it rigorous

How many subjects do you need?

  • Sample size depends on: significance level, the power you want (chance of detecting a real effect if it exists), and the minimum effect size worth detecting
  • Smaller effects need bigger samples — precision doesn’t come free
  • Gabor’s own worked example for this: fine-tuning social media advertising — a CEU business school choosing between two versions of an ad
from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportion_effectsize
from statsmodels.stats.power import NormalIndPower

effect_size = proportion_effectsize(0.01, 0.02)  # planning assumption: 1% CTR, want to detect a doubling
n_needed = NormalIndPower().solve_power(effect_size=abs(effect_size), alpha=0.05, power=0.8, ratio=1)
html`<p>Planning assumption: a <strong>${planning_ctr}%</strong> click-through rate. To detect a doubling of that rate at 80% power: <strong>${n_needed.toLocaleString()}</strong> visitors per arm.</p>`

Complications

Imperfect compliance

Gabor’s own account of this experiment: fewer than 20% of the treatment group had to be reassigned back to the office, because of changes in their personal circumstances.

  • Assignment (what a subject is told to do) and treatment (what they actually do) don’t always match
  • With small non-compliance like this, the effect we measured — comparing by assignment — is a reasonable stand-in for the effect of actual treatment
  • With substantial non-compliance, that stops being true, and the honest move is to say so rather than report a number that’s no longer trustworthy

ITT versus the treatment effect

  • What we measured above compares people by assignment, regardless of what they actually did — this is the intent-to-treat (ITT) effect
  • ITT answers a genuinely useful question on its own: “if we rolled this out and got this level of compliance, what would happen?” — often the more decision-relevant number anyway
  • The LATE (local average treatment effect) — the effect specifically on those who comply with their assignment — is what you’d need instead if non-compliance were large

Being observed changes behaviour

  • Hawthorne effect: both treated and untreated subjects change their behaviour simply because they know they’re being watched — not because of the treatment itself
  • John Henry effect: the control group works harder than usual, specifically because they know they’re the comparison — the opposite problem, and just as real
  • Spillovers: one subject’s treatment can affect another’s outcome directly — breaks the clean comparison entirely. Good design keeps treatment and control apart where possible

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 20

Internal and external validity

  • Internal validity here: assignment was random, and non-compliance was small and concentrated in the treatment group only — Gabor judges internal validity as strong for this study
  • External validity: this firm changed its actual management practice off the back of this result — real impact. But would the same effect hold at a different firm, a different job type, a different country? That’s a judgement call, not something the data itself can answer

So, does working from home change who quits?

Summary

  • Potential outcomes formalise what “an effect” even means — and confirm the individual effect is permanently unobservable, only the average is reachable
  • DAGs make the causal question, and the assumptions behind it, explicit
  • Random assignment is what makes the simple group comparison trustworthy — everything else this module has done with observational data is a second-best attempt at the same goal
  • Real experiments still need care: enough power, checked balance, and an honest reckoning with imperfect compliance
