Are bigger firms better managed?

Everything from the workshop — conditional probability, conditional means, scatter, correlation — on one real business dataset.

The question

Some firms are much better managed than others. Is firm size part of the story?

  • y = quality of management
  • x = firm size (number of employees)

Why it matters: if size itself causes better management, policies that help firms grow could also improve how they’re run. If not, that link is a mirage.

Today is the first step: describe the pattern of association. Whether it’s causal comes at the end.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Data Analysis for Business, Economics, and Policy, Ch. 4

The data: World Management Survey

  • Interviewers talk to senior managers and score 18 management practices — e.g. how performance is tracked, how targets are set
  • Each practice scored from 1 (worst practice) to 5 (best practice)
  • The management score = the simple average of the 18, so it also runs from 1 to 5

Our sample, exactly as in the textbook: Mexican firms, 2013, with 100–5,000 employees — firms.

Is it causal?

A correlation of describes an association, not an explanation. Three possible stories:

  • Size → management: bigger firms can afford professional managers, HR systems and consultants
  • Management → size: well-run firms win customers and grow
  • Something else drives both: industry, foreign ownership, competition. A multinational subsidiary is likely to be both big and well run.

Firm size wasn’t randomly assigned: this is observational data, so the variation in x comes from firms’ own histories and choices. Description can’t tell these stories apart. Weeks 6 and 9 give us tools that can start to.

From pattern to model choice

Describing a pattern is only half the job. The other half is saying what it means for the model you build next.

What we saw What it means for the model
The bin-scatter curves: a big rise from small to medium, then it flattens Don’t fit a straight line in raw employees. Use a curve, or log employees first (Weeks 4–5)
Employment is heavily right-skewed Consider logging it: the pattern was clearer on the log scale
The pattern could be driven by something else, e.g. industry Hold that variable fixed, or check the pattern within each group (Week 6)

In your portfolio, this is the step that turns exploratory analysis into justification: each pattern you find should point to a specific modelling choice, and you should say so explicitly.
