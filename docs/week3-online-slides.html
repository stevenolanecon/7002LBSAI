Why we log

The workshop used the log of reaction time to see a pattern more clearly. This session looks at why that works — and when it stops working.

Why transform at all?

A “% change” needs a baseline — a “before” to measure against. Time series has one built in: this year’s GDP vs. last year’s.

Cross-sectional data doesn’t. Student A’s finger length isn’t “before” Student B’s — there’s no natural reference point to build a percentage difference from.

The natural logarithm sidesteps the problem: on a log scale, the difference between any two values approximates their percentage difference — no designated baseline required. It has a second payoff too: frequent nonlinear patterns are often far better approximated once we look at relative differences instead of raw ones.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Data Analysis for Business, Economics, and Policy, Ch. 4

What ln(x) actually buys you

  • \(\ln(x)\) = the natural logarithm of \(x\) — \(x\) must be a positive number: \(\ln(0)\) and \(\ln(\text{negative})\) don’t exist, so a variable with zeros (e.g. income including the unemployed) can’t be logged as-is
  • The claim: differences in natural logs approximate relative (percentage) differences

\[\ln(x + \Delta x) - \ln(x) \approx \frac{\Delta x}{x}\]

Numerically: \(\ln(1.01) = 0.0099 \approx 0.01\) — a 1% increase. \(\ln(1.1) = 0.095 \approx 0.1\) — close to a 10% increase, already a little off.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 4

Where it comes from

From calculus: the derivative of \(\ln(x)\) is \(\dfrac{1}{x}\).

\[\frac{d\ln(x)}{dx} = \frac{1}{x} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \ln(x+\Delta x) - \ln(x) \approx \frac{1}{x}\Delta x = \frac{\Delta x}{x}\]

That’s the whole trick: a log difference is approximately a relative difference, for small changes. The approximation is a straight line touching the curve — it only stays close nearby.

Beyond the approximation: the exact conversion

For an exact percentage change instead of the approximation, invert the log:

\[\%\Delta x = e^{\,\ln(x_2) - \ln(x_1)} - 1\]

{
  const diffs = [0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0, -0.1, -0.3, -0.5, -1.0]
  const rows = diffs.map(d => ({ diff: d, approx: d, exact: Math.exp(d) - 1 }))

  return html`<table style="width:75%;margin:0 auto;border-collapse:collapse;font-family:sans-serif;font-size:0.95em;">
    <thead><tr style="background:#1a1a2e;color:white;">
      <th style="padding:10px 20px;text-align:right;">Log difference</th>
      <th style="padding:10px 20px;text-align:right;">Approximation (Δx / x)</th>
      <th style="padding:10px 20px;text-align:right;">Exact % change</th>
    </tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      ${rows.map((r,i) => `<tr style="${i%2===1?'background:#fafafa;':''}border-bottom:1px solid #eee;">
        <td style="padding:8px 20px;text-align:right;">${r.diff > 0 ? '+' : ''}${r.diff.toFixed(1)}</td>
        <td style="padding:8px 20px;text-align:right;color:#999;">${r.approx > 0 ? '+' : ''}${(r.approx*100).toFixed(0)}%</td>
        <td style="padding:8px 20px;text-align:right;font-weight:700;color:${Math.abs(r.exact - r.approx) > 0.05 ? '#e63946' : '#2d6a4f'};">${r.exact > 0 ? '+' : ''}${(r.exact*100).toFixed(0)}%</td>
      </tr>`).join('')}
    </tbody>
  </table>`
}

The approximation isn’t just imprecise for large differences — it’s also asymmetric. A log difference of +1.0 is a +172% change, not +100%. A log difference of −1.0 is only −63%, not −100%: log differences are bounded below by −1 no matter how close x gets to zero, but unbounded above. The scale itself is lopsided.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 4

Bringing it back to our own data

Back in the workshop, we scattered finger length against raw reaction time — no logging. Week 1 found reaction time is skewed, though: logging stretches out the crowded low end and compresses the long right tail, which can make a pattern easier to see. Let’s check both, side by side, on our own data.
