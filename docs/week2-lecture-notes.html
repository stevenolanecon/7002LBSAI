Inference: When Is a Difference Real?

Part One

Mind the gap

Chapter 1 ended with what felt like a tentative finding in our own data: phone users looked faster than laptop users, by something like 300 milliseconds. The gap is not in dispute – it is sitting right there in the numbers we collected. The question this chapter exists to answer is a different one entirely: if we ran the exact same exercise again next week, with the same twenty-four of us, would that gap look the same? Bigger? Smaller? Could it even flip direction?

Nobody in the room can answer that question by staring harder at the table above. Answering it properly is the whole business of statistical inference: using the one dataset we actually have to say something responsible about a world we didn't get to fully observe. That's what this chapter builds, piece by difficult piece.

Part Two

What Are We Allowed to Claim? (External Validity)

Suppose, for the sake of argument, that the device gap turns out to be real and not chance. What, exactly, would that actually entitle us to say? Only this: that the gap is real for this class, on the day it was collected, using this specific task. Nothing yet about phone and laptop users in general – twenty-four self-selecting business analytics master's students on one Friday afternoon are a long way from "everyone."

That gap – between what a dataset can actually show and what we'd like to claim from it – has a name: external validity. It asks whether the pattern sitting in our data is the same as the general pattern we actually care about. It is a different question from internal validity (did we run the experiment itself correctly – were we measuring what we thought we were measuring, the way Week 1's finger-length discussion raised).

Three places the threat usually hides

  • TimeWe have data from the past, or from one specific session. We usually care about the future.
  • SpaceOur data is one class, in one room, on one day. Would the same pattern hold somewhere else?
  • Sub-groupsOur data is this cohort. Would it hold for a different group of people?

In the class students worked in groups on exactly this question for our own data. The first group pointed out the time of day might have had an influence.

From the workshop

“You'd just done three hours of learning Python, then I came in and said, right, do this reaction-time experiment. If you'd done it at nine in the morning, fresh, I'd say more than likely those reaction times would be quicker. Most people in the universe haven't just sat through a three-hour Python class before they react to something.”

That's a real time threat, specific to our own data, not a generic textbook warning. The same groups raised a real space threat (the room itself, the specific laptops and phones on hand) and a real sub-groups threat (this is a notably young, tech-comfortable cohort – gaming experience in particular came up as something that could easily be pushing reaction times faster than a more representative population would show).

There's no tick-box version of this. You run an experiment, and you keep asking how what you measured here might fail to generalise, staying honest about the specific threats rather than pretending you've eliminated them. Every real dataset carries some of these threats somewhere. The job is knowing which ones apply to yours.
Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Data Analysis for Business, Economics, and Policy, Ch. 5
Part Three

One Sample Among Many

Our Week 1 data is one dataset – one of many that could have been collected. Run the exercise again next week and the numbers move, partly just by chance. Even more abstractly, imagine running history again – we may end with different numbers. Those many possible datasets are, conceptually, repeated samples from the same underlying data-generating process (DGP): the whole tangle of eyesight, dexterity, finger length, attention, and hundreds of other things that combine to produce a reaction time.

Because the data could have come out differently, any statistic computed from it – the mean, say – has its own distribution too. Today's mean differs from next week's mean, which differs from the week after's. That distribution of a statistic, across repeated samples, is called its sampling distribution. This is a different object from the distribution of the variable itself.

Two distributions, not one.
  • The distribution of the variable – Week 1's own histogram, 217 individual reaction times, each one a single tap.
  • The sampling distribution of the mean – what you'd get if you repeated the whole 24-student exercise many times and plotted each repetition's own mean, once per repetition.
The first is built from individual observations. The second is built from statistics, one per imagined repeat of the entire experiment. Mixing the two up is the most common way to get lost in what follows.
Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 5
Part Four

Building a Sampling Distribution by Hand

Before touching a computer, it's worth building one sampling distribution by hand, on a population small enough to see completely. Take five real students from our own Week 1 data and pretend – just for this exercise – that these five are the entire population we care about. Their true average, across all five, is the one number we actually want.

We could easily calculate it now but – to understand the concept of a sampling distribution – we are going to impose an artificial restriction: you're only allowed to sample two of the five at a time. We are also going to imagine that mean reaction time stays fixed (again, not very realistic – maybe this is a very small planet populated by five robots). We want to write out every possible pair, and compute each pair's own average.

Figure

Ten pairs, ten different averages

As you can see below, there are ten possible pairs from the population of five people. That is ten repeated samples and ten different averages, one from each sample – some close to the true mean of all five, some a long way off. That spread, from repeated samples of just two observations, is the sampling distribution in miniature. Scale this exact idea up to our example: repeated classes of twenty-four, sampled from a much larger population, and you have the real thing.

Part Five

Our Assumed Data-Generating Process

Going forward we are going to collapse last chapter's raw trials down to one average reaction time per student, and the 24 resulting numbers have their own shape – a mean, a spread, a distribution. Fit a Normal curve to that shape (two parameters, mean and standard deviation, exactly as Chapter 1 covered) and you get something you can simulate from.

Figure

The distribution we're assuming

You may notice this curve allows for negative reaction times – a side effect of fitting a Normal distribution whose standard deviation (888.6ms) is nearly as large as its own mean (1,070.1ms). Obviously, that can't happen in the real world, and we haven't allowed it in any of the simulated data used in this chapter – every simulated value is floor-clipped at a small positive minimum before use. The underlying statistical ideas we're exploring are unaffected either way.

This Normal(μ, σ) curve is our assumed data-generating process: an assumption we're choosing to make, because we have no way to observe the real one directly. We wouldn't normally treat the result of a one-off data-collection exercise like our reaction-time test this way – but doing so here helps us understand some genuinely powerful underlying statistics, which is exactly why it's worth the artificial setup. Everything else in this chapter follows from taking that assumption seriously enough to compute with it.

Part Six

A Thousand Simulated Classes

Here is the payoff of assuming a DGP: a computer can pretend to rerun last week's experiment. Draw 24 values from Normal(1070.1, 888.6) and average them, and you have one simulated class's mean – a single fresh draw from the sampling distribution, standing in for "what if we did this again next week or what if we re-ran history using the Time Stone?"

Show the Python behind the simulation 
import numpy as np

# mu_pop, sd_pop, n_students all come from our own Week 1 data
def simulate_means(n, reps):
    draws = np.random.normal(mu_pop, sd_pop, (reps, n))
    return draws.mean(axis=1)

sample_means = simulate_means(n_students, 1000)  # 1,000 simulated class means

This is the core idea, simplified for reading. The real deck's version adds a floor-clip and a small calibration step, because our class's standard deviation (888.6ms) is almost as large as its mean (1,070.1ms) – left unclipped, an occasional simulated reaction time would come out negative, which isn't physically possible. That correction doesn't change the concept above, just keeps every simulated value plausible.

Figure

One simulated replay, against our real class

Same n (number of observations) both times – our real class of 24, and one simulated class of 24 – so the two panels are directly comparable. The simulated class is different in its specific numbers, as it should be, but similar in its overall shape: that's the DGP assumption doing its job.

Do that once, and one simulated class is a curiosity. Do it a thousand times, and you can watch the sampling distribution build itself in front of you – which is exactly what the figure below does, from the real simulated data behind that task.

Figure

The sampling distribution, building up

Two things happen at once as the sample count climbs from 10 to 1,000:

  1. The shape smooths out. Ten samples gives a lumpy, uneven histogram – not much more than a bar chart. By a thousand, it's unmistakably bell-shaped.
  2. The mean of the means converges on the true mean. With only 10 samples, the average of those averages can sit well away from 1,070.1ms. By 1,000, it's landed close.
Two different numbers, both called "n" – don't conflate them. A question from the room, almost word for word: "why does the mean get closer to the truth the more we have?" It's worth being precise about what is growing.
  • Sample size (24 here) – how many students are averaged together inside one simulated class.
  • Number of samples (10, 100, 1,000 here) – how many separate simulated classes you've run, each with its own mean.
Growing the number of samples is what smooths the histogram above and pulls the mean-of-means toward the truth. Growing the sample size does something related but different – it's covered on its own in Part Eight, "The Standard Error."
Part Seven

Why This Works: Unbiasedness and the Central Limit Theorem

Let's get specific on the two effects we just discussed.

Unbiasedness

The mean of the sampling distribution equals the true population mean. Track the running average of the simulated class-means as more and more of them accumulate, and watch it settle toward the true 1,070.1ms.

Figure

Converging on the truth

This is the Law of Large Numbers: as the number of independent draws increases, the sample mean converges to the true mean. An estimator whose expected value equals the true parameter is called unbiased.

Asymptotic normality (the Central Limit Theorem)

The sampling distribution of the mean approaches Normal as sample size grows (that's basically what asymptotic means) – regardless of the shape of the underlying population. This is the genuinely surprising part. Our own class's reaction times are not Normal at all – Chapter 1's histogram from the classroom-generated data was sharply right-skewed, with a long tail. It doesn't matter. Simulate samples from a right-skewed distribution shaped like our own data (same mean, same spread), at three different sample sizes, and compare each histogram against the theoretical Normal curve the CLT predicts.

Figure

From lumpy to Normal, regardless of the starting shape

At n = 5 the histogram is genuinely uneven, and only loosely follows the dashed Normal curve. By n = 30 the two are hard to tell apart, even though every draw still comes from the same right-skewed population. This is the Central Limit Theorem, and it is one of the most useful facts in all of statistics: it's the reason the Normal distribution underpins so much of frequentist statistical testing, even when the raw data it's built from looks nothing like a bell curve.

Neither of these results needed the DGP to actually be Normal – only that it has a finite mean and variance, and that enough independent draws get averaged together. That's a far weaker requirement than it might feel like on first encounter, which is exactly why it shows up everywhere.
Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 5
Part Eight

The Standard Error

Everything in Parts Six and Seven assumed a known population – Normal(1070.1, 888.6) – so we could watch a thousand simulated samples land around the true value. In reality, we have one dataset: the reaction-time data collected last week. We still have no idea about the true population reaction times from the wider world that we want to capture. No known population mean, no known population standard deviation, and no ability to simulate a thousand more classes. So how can we use our sample to say anything about the population we care about? How do our sample mean and sample standard deviation relate to the underlying and unobservable sampling distribution?

Here is where things get cool.1 There are two ways forward: a formula, or a computational trick called the bootstrap. Let's start with the formula.

Here's a quietly satisfying fact: by the end of Part Six, you'd actually already calculated a standard error of the sampling distribution three times over, without it having a name yet. The standard deviation of your own 10, 100, and 1,000 sample-means is the standard error – a different quantity from the plain standard deviation of one sample's raw data.

  • Standard deviationThe spread of individual observations within one sample – the 24 students in our own room.
  • Standard errorThe spread of the sampling distribution itself – how much the sample mean would bounce around if you reran the whole experiment many times.

The useful part: you don't need a thousand simulated classes to get it. That formula we mentioned builds it directly from the one sample you actually have.

$$SE(\bar{x}) = \frac{SD[x]}{\sqrt{n}}$$

larger when the data is more spread out, smaller when the sample is bigger

Plugged in with our own numbers – SD = 888.6ms, n = 24 – that gives SE ≈ 181.4ms. Checked against the real spread of the 1,000-sample simulation from Part Six (188.5ms), the two land close together – simulation always carries some noise, so they were never going to match to the decimal, but still close. As I said: Cool!

From the workshop

“We went round the houses a lot on this one – remember, you got that standard deviation from made-up, simulated data. And then this simple formula comes along, and it's only about five milliseconds off. How does that work?”

Root-n convergence

As the number of observations you have – the sample size – increases the standard error decreases and so the sampling distribution you are drawing from is narrower. Therefore your sample mean is likely to be near to the true mean as the number of observations increase. Bigger samples buy precision – but not for free, and not in a straight line. To halve the standard error, you need to quadruple the sample size, because the formula divides by the square root of n, not n itself.

Figure

The standard error shrinks – but not in a straight line

Going from 24 students to 100 would help a reasonable amount; going from 1,000 to 4,000 would barely move the needle. Precision is genuinely expensive, and it gets more expensive the more of it you already have.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 5
Part Nine

The Bootstrap

The formula above needs a known SD and a sample size – both of which we happen to have. But there's a second route to exactly the same destination, one that works even when no formula exists: the bootstrap.

Take the original 24 student-means, and draw a fresh sample of 24 from that same set, with replacement: each draw can be picked more than once, and some of the original 24 might not get picked at all. Compute the mean of that resample. Repeat a few thousand times, and look at the spread of the resulting means – that spread is the bootstrap's own estimate of the standard error, built without ever assuming a DGP at all.

Figure

How a bootstrap sample is built

Three resamples drawn with replacement from the same original sample of 6 – item 4 appears three times in Sample 3, while items 3 and 5 never get drawn there at all. The spread across the resulting statistics is the bootstrap's estimate of the sampling distribution.

Figure · interactive

Bootstrapping our own 24 students

Resamples (B)10

Drag B up from 10 towards 2,000 and watch the bootstrap standard error settle down rather than jump around – once it's settled, it should land close to the 181.4ms the formula already gave. Under the conditions both methods need (a large enough sample, independent observations), the formula and the bootstrap converge on the same answer. The formula is faster to compute; the bootstrap keeps working in situations – more complex statistics, no known formula – where the formula simply doesn't exist.

Part Ten

Confidence Intervals

With a mean and a standard error in hand – by formula or by bootstrap, it no longer matters which – the last step is turning them into a statement about the general pattern our data represents, not just the specific number we happened to measure.

A confidence interval (CI) is a range built symmetrically around the estimate:

$$CI = \text{statistic} \; \pm \; (\text{critical value} \times SE)$$
Confidence levelCritical value
90%1.6 × SE
95%2 × SE (1.96, precisely)
99%2.6 × SE

Plugged in with our own numbers from our sample, 1,070.1 ± (1.96 × 181.4) gives a 95% confidence interval of [714.6, 1425.6] ms. In words: in the general pattern our data represents, we can be 95% confident the true average reaction time falls somewhere in that range.

Wider interval → more confident it contains the truth, but less precise about where. There's no free lunch: more confidence costs precision, every time.

Where the CI actually sits on the curve

The confidence interval isn't a separate idea from the Normal distribution – it's a direct slice of it. Here is the sampling distribution of our mean (Normal, centred on 1,070.1ms, spread by the standard error of 181.4ms), with the 95% CI shaded as the middle region it actually is.

Figure

The 95% CI, shaded on its own sampling distribution

The shaded region holds 95% of the area under the curve; the two unshaded tails hold 2.5% each. The critical value, 1.96, is simply how many standard errors out from the centre you have to go before you've cut off exactly those tails.

Does the 95% promise actually hold?

Simulate 100 samples from the same DGP, build a 95% CI from each one the way just described, and count how many actually contain the true mean. As you'll see below, the orange confidence intervals are the ones that miss the true mean.

Part Eleven

Quick Recap – and What's Next

  • We want to generalise from our data – that's statistical inference, and the gap between what we can claim and what we actually measured is external validity.
  • Any statistic computed from a sample has its own sampling distribution – a different object from the distribution of the raw variable.
  • Two ways to estimate the uncertainty in a statistic: a formula (\(SE = SD/\sqrt{n}\)) or the bootstrap (resample with replacement, look at the spread). Under the right conditions, they agree.
  • A 95% CI is the estimate ± (critical value × SE) – a range we expect to contain the true value.
  • Using that CI, we can finally make a statement about the population – not just the room.

Nearly three hours of workshop led up to one sentence: we can be 95% confident the true average reaction time lies between roughly 715 and 1,426 milliseconds. It doesn't sound like much – but it is huge. It's the first time in this module anything has been said about a population we never directly measured – and everything from here on builds on exactly that move.

Testing a Hypothesis

The workshop ran out of road before reaching this part – so the online session that follows starts here from scratch, then moves on to how a hypothesis test gets misused.

Part Twelve

Fisher's Tea Test

In 1920s Cambridge, a woman at an afternoon tea party made a claim: she could tell, just by taste, whether the milk or the tea had gone into the cup first. Ronald Fisher – a founding figure of a great deal of modern statistics, for good and for bad – was there, and didn't believe her. Neither did his companions. But being statisticians, they didn't just dismiss her either. They designed a test.

Several cups were made, some milk-first, some tea-first, and delivered to her in a random order she didn't know. She had to identify all of them correctly, from taste alone.

From the online session

“If she was just guessing, then we can calculate how likely she would get them all right just by chance. And the point is – if she actually does far better than chance predicted, then this is evidence for her claim.”

That's the entire logic of hypothesis testing, built from one experiment in 1920s Cambridge: work out what pure chance would look like, then ask whether what actually happened is far enough from that to count as evidence against it.

Putting the logic into symbols

Formalising this needs three pieces of notation, and they'll recur for the rest of the module. Define the statistic you want to test – call it \(s\). It could be a mean, a difference of two means (our own device question), or, in later weeks, a regression coefficient. What you actually care about is its true value, \(s_{\text{true}}\) – the value in the population, or general pattern, your data represents. This is the same idea Part Five called \(\mu\), just given its fully general name: not every statistic you'll ever test is a mean. What your own data gives you is only an estimate of that true value, written with a hat: \(\hat{s}\).

Testing a hypothesis means stating the question as two competing possibilities about \(s_{\text{true}}\), only one of which can be true: a null hypothesis \(H_0\) and an alternative hypothesis \(H_A\). Together they have to cover every possibility you're interested in. The most common shape, and the one almost every test in this module will almost implicitly use, sets the null at zero:

$$H_0: \; s_{\text{true}} = 0 \qquad H_A: \; s_{\text{true}} \neq 0$$

The null is protected

Designing a hypothesis test means thinking about the null as something protected – you have to gather real evidence to overturn it, not just a hunch. Békés & Kézdi's own textbook uses an analogy worth keeping close: a criminal court. Everyone goes in presumed innocent, and is only convicted if the evidence is strong enough to overcome that presumption.

Mind the wording. A verdict of "not guilty" never means "proven innocent" – it means there wasn't enough evidence to overcome the presumption. Hypothesis testing uses exactly the same asymmetry. If we don't have enough evidence against \(H_0\), we say we fail to reject it – not that we've proven it true. If we do have enough evidence, we reject \(H_0\) – not that we've proven \(H_A\). Testing can only ever tell you whether the evidence clears a bar you set in advance.
Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 6
Part Thirteen

Stating Our Own Hypothesis

Back to the question Part One opened with: does device – phone versus laptop – affect reaction time? The statistic is the difference in average reaction time between the two groups. The null hypothesis makes the strongest possible default claim: there is no difference at all.

$$H_0: \; \bar{x}_{\text{phone,true}} - \bar{x}_{\text{laptop,true}} = 0$$ $$H_A: \; \bar{x}_{\text{phone,true}} - \bar{x}_{\text{laptop,true}} \neq 0$$

In words: there's no true difference in reaction time by device, in the wider population of JMU students this class represents – versus there is one. The test's whole job is to determine which of these two we actually have enough evidence for.

The t-statistic

The tool for doing that is the t-statistic – a test statistic, which measures how far our estimate sits from what \(H_0\) predicts, in units of standard error.

$$t = \frac{\hat{s}}{SE(\hat{s})}$$

\(\hat{s}\) stands for any estimated statistic – a mean, a difference in means, or, in later weeks, a regression coefficient

For a difference between two averages – our own case – the standard error needs a little more work than Part Eight's single-sample formula:

$$t = \frac{\bar{x}_A - \bar{x}_B}{SE(\bar{x}_A - \bar{x}_B)}$$
$$SE(\bar{x}_A - \bar{x}_B) = \sqrt{\frac{SD_A^2}{n_A} + \frac{SD_B^2}{n_B}}$$

This needs the sampling distribution of \(\bar{x}_A - \bar{x}_B\) to be approximately Normal – not reaction time itself, which Chapter 1 already showed is sharply right-skewed. With dozens of trials in each group, Part Seven's Central Limit Theorem makes that a safe bet regardless of the raw variable's shape.

A simplifying assumption, named honestly

Here's where it gets a little uncomfortable, deliberately. To use that formula at all, every trial has to count as its own independent observation – which means treating the device data not as 24 students, but as 132 independent laptop observations and 67 independent phone observations.

From the online session

“Now, we know, because we created this data ourselves, that this is not true. We had 24 students, and a lot of these observations are repeated observations from students in the classroom. The reason I'm making this assumption is to demonstrate something that relates to the Central Limit Theorem – by the central limit theorem, we need n to be getting up to about 30 to be confident of a broadly Normal sampling distribution. If we only had 24 students and were looking at each student's own average, we'd have something like 16 for laptops and about 8 for phones. Those are low numbers, and a lot of the statistical theory underlying these tests breaks down when you have very low numbers. So for the means of demonstration here, I'm just assuming our data is independent.”

It's a genuine trade-off, not a mistake quietly swept under the rug: student-level counts (16 and 8) are the honest unit of independence, but far too small for the CLT to kick in reliably; trial-level counts (132 and 67) are large enough for the CLT, but overstate how much genuinely independent evidence the data contains. The module makes the second choice here, on purpose, to let the rest of the machinery run – and says so plainly, rather than pretending the problem isn't there.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 6
Part Fourteen

Calculating It, and Making a Decision

With the formula settled, the only thing left is arithmetic – done live, in Excel, against our own real numbers.

The difference in means is \(1{,}078.94 - 772.69 = 306.25\) ms – a sizeable-looking gap, though we still can't say for certain it's real until the test is actually run. The standard error, from the formula above, comes to \(221.64\) ms. Put the two together:

$$t = \frac{306.25}{221.64} \approx 1.38$$

A number on its own means nothing yet – it's a ratio of milliseconds to milliseconds, so the units cancel. What matters is where it falls against a critical value, the same idea Part Ten used to build a confidence interval, now flipped around to make a reject/don't-reject call.

Figure

Where our own t-statistic lands

For a 5% test, the critical value is \(\pm 1.96\) – the same 1.96 Part Ten already used to build a 95% CI, since the two ideas are two sides of one curve. Reject \(H_0\) if \(t\) falls beyond that line in either direction; don't reject it if \(t\) falls inside. Our own \(t = 1.38\) sits comfortably inside – nowhere near either tail.

Two ways to be wrong

Before reading off a verdict, it's worth being precise about what could go wrong with it. There are exactly two kinds of mistake a test can make, and they are not equally serious by design.

\(H_0\) is true\(H_0\) is false
Don't rejectTrue negativeFalse negative – Type II error
RejectFalse positive – Type I errorTrue positive

Think of it as the criminal court again: convicting an innocent person is a false positive, a Type I error – and it's treated as the far worse mistake. A test saying "not pregnant" is pregnant is the same shape of error. The null is protected precisely to keep this kind of mistake rare – deliberately rarer than its counterpart, a false negative.

The p-value

Everything above can also be read off a single number: the p-value – the smallest level of significance at which we could still reject \(H_0\), or equivalently, the probability of seeing a test statistic this extreme (or more extreme) if \(H_0\) were actually true. Small p, strong evidence against \(H_0\).

Show the Python 
from scipy.stats import ttest_ind

laptop = df.loc[df['device'] == 'Laptop', 'reaction_time_ms']
phone  = df.loc[df['device'] == 'Phone',  'reaction_time_ms']

t_stat, p_value = ttest_ind(laptop, phone, equal_var=False)
# t_stat: 1.382, p_value: 0.1687

Same t we calculated by hand above (allowing for rounding) – Python's ttest_ind is doing exactly the same unpooled-variance formula, in one line instead of several.

A p-value of 0.1687 means: if there truly were no device effect, we'd see a gap this large – or larger – by chance with probability 16.87%. That's not low. For a 95% test we'd need it below 5%; for 99%, below 1%.

So: fail to reject. We're not saying straight up that there's definitely no effect – we're saying we haven't got the evidence, from this data, to say a phone-versus-laptop difference is real. That's a genuinely different claim, and the distinction is the whole point of Part Twelve's criminal-court analogy.
Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 6
Part Fifteen

One-Sided Tests, and Picking a Threshold

Before moving on to how tests get misused, one honest footnote on the test we just ran: everything above depends on sample size. A lot of the formulas – standard error, standard deviation from the sample – are driven by how many observations you have. With 24 students in the room, almost any effect has to be fairly large to clear the bar. With 50,000, the bar gets trivially easy to clear – the confidence interval narrows to nearly nothing, and even a one-millisecond gap could reject \(H_0\). At that point statistical significance stops being the interesting question: the question shifts from is there a difference to is the difference big enough to matter, and external validity (Part Two) is exactly as important as it ever was.

Not every test is two-sided

Our device test was two-sided: we had no prior reason to expect phones faster than laptops or vice versa, so either direction counted as evidence. Not every question works that way. Ask instead: does caffeine reduce reaction time? Caffeine is a stimulant – there's a specific directional prediction going in, so only a large effect in that one predicted direction counts as evidence.

$$H_0: \; s_{\text{true}} \leq 0 \qquad H_A: \; s_{\text{true}} > 0$$

Reject \(H_0\) now only if \(t\) is large and positive – extreme in the predicted direction only. The 5% critical value shrinks to about 1.64, smaller than the two-sided 1.96, because the whole 5% now sits in one tail instead of being split across both. Same overall false-positive rate either way, but the one-sided test is more powerful for an effect in the predicted direction – at the cost of zero power to detect an effect in the other direction, however large, because the test was never built to look for it there.

What p-value threshold should you actually pick?

There's no universal answer, and the "5% is significant" convention is, frankly, arbitrary – a p-value of 0.051 is barely distinguishable from 0.049, yet only one crosses the textbook line. What should actually guide the choice is what's at stake.

  • Guilty beyond reasonable doubtA costly, hard-to-reverse decision. Pick a conservative threshold – 1% or lower.
  • Proof of conceptDeciding whether to investigate further. 10–15% is often fine – not slam-dunk evidence, but a real signal worth another look, more data, or another experiment before betting the business on it.
Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 6

Size and power

Two numbers summarise everything a test is trying to balance. The size of a test is the probability, under \(H_0\), of a false positive – fixed in advance, usually at 5% or 1%. The power of a test is the probability, under \(H_A\), of avoiding a false negative. Power rises when the sample is larger, the spread is smaller, or the true effect sits further from the null – the bigger the real effect, the easier it is to tell apart from noise. To keep one picture readable, the figure below uses the one-sided 1.645 critical value from a few paragraphs up, rather than redrawing both tails of a two-sided test.

Part Sixteen

When Testing Breaks Down

Everything so far has quietly assumed you're running one test. Run many at once, and the 5%-false-positive promise stops meaning what it sounds like it means.

The 400-patient problem

Here's Békés & Kézdi's own case: a medical dataset of 400 patients, a heart-disease indicator, and a hundred lifestyle features – diet, exercise, income, sleep, and dozens more. Test each feature against the indicator, one at a time, at the usual 5% level, and suppose you find six with a significant link. That feels like a real discovery.

It probably isn't. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that none of the hundred features actually has any link to heart disease at all – every null hypothesis is true. A 5% false-positive rate means that, purely by chance, you'd still expect to reject about 5 of the 100 nulls, just from noise. Picking out "the six that worked" and writing them up as findings has the logic backwards: you started by assuming none of them was real, and chance alone explains roughly that many false alarms.

The same idea, told as a cartoon rather than a case study: scientists test whether jelly beans cause acne and find no link. They test purple jelly beans – no link. Brown, pink, blue, and so on through twenty colours – until green jelly beans turn up a p-value just under 5%. It gets reported as a real discovery: green jelly beans linked to acne. Testing twenty hypotheses at the standard 5% threshold makes finding at least one "significant" result by pure chance alone genuinely likely – not a freak accident, just arithmetic.2

Testing many hypotheses at once needs a stricter bar than testing one. A few dozen tests might call for 0.1–0.5% instead of 1–5%; the strictest common fix, the Bonferroni correction, divides your target p-value by the number of tests – 20 hypotheses aiming for 0.05 becomes a real bar of \(0.05/20 = 0.0025\). It's often criticised as too conservative, but the direction of the correction is the right instinct: be more sceptical, not less, the more chances you give yourself to get lucky.

P-hacking

Related, and more deliberate: p-hacking – running many versions of one test and only reporting the ones that worked. It's the jelly-bean comic's logic, but done on purpose rather than by accident. It happens more often than anyone would like to admit, and avoiding it is less a technical fix than a professional habit: do the analysis from an honest place, and report what you actually found, not what you went looking for.

Testing when the data is huge

One last pitfall, echoing Part Fifteen's opening note. With millions of observations, statistical inference starts to lose relevance – confidence intervals shrink to almost nothing, and almost any difference becomes statistically significant, including ones too small to matter economically. The question shifts, again, from is there a difference to is the difference big enough to matter – look at the size of the effect, not just whether it clears a p-value.

Part Seventeen

Using AI, and What's Next

Since this module sits inside a Business Analytics and AI degree, it's worth asking the obvious question directly: can AI just do this for you? Ask it for the two-sample t-test formula, and – per the textbook's own test of this – it comes back mostly right.

Mostly is the operative word. AI is a genuinely useful companion for explaining concepts, and there's nothing wrong with feeding it this module's own materials to help something click. But you will always have to verify its output – the same way you'd check a classmate's working. A beautifully written, fluently-explained formula can still be the wrong formula, and it will run (or read) just as confidently either way.

With that, the module has its first complete, working test of whether one difference is real – built from a 1920s tea party, our own class's device data, and a healthy list of ways the whole machinery can go wrong.
