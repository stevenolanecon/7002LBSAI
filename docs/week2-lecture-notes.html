Week 1 ended with a real, visible gap in our own data: phone users looked faster than laptop users, by something like 300 milliseconds. That gap is not in dispute – it is sitting right there in the numbers we collected. The question this chapter exists to answer is a different one entirely: if we ran the exact same exercise again next week, with the same twenty-four of us, would that gap look the same? Bigger? Smaller? Could it even flip direction?

Answering it properly is the whole business of statistical inference: using the one dataset we actually have to say something responsible about a world we didn't get to fully observe. Part Two

What Are We Allowed to Claim? (External Validity)

Suppose, for the sake of argument, that the device gap turns out to be real and not chance. What, exactly, would that actually entitle us to say? Only this: that the gap is real for this class, on the day it was collected, using this specific task. Nothing yet about phone and laptop users in general – twenty-four self-selecting business analytics master's students on one Friday afternoon are a long way from "everyone."

That gap – between what a dataset can actually show and what we'd like to claim from it – has a name: external validity. It asks whether the pattern sitting in our data is the same as the general pattern we actually care about. It is a genuinely different question from internal validity (did we run the experiment itself correctly – were we measuring what we thought we were measuring, the way Week 1's finger-length discussion raised).

Three places the threat usually hides

  • TimeWe have data from the past, or from one specific session. We usually care about the future.
  • SpaceOur data is one class, in one room, on one day. Would the same pattern hold somewhere else?
  • Sub-groupsOur data is this cohort. Would it hold for a different group of people?

I set groups loose on exactly this question for our own data, and the real answers the room came back with are worth keeping, because they're sharper than anything I could invent as a textbook example.

From the workshop

“You'd just done three hours of learning Python, then I came in and said, right, do this reaction-time experiment. If you'd done it at nine in the morning, fresh, I'd say more than likely those reaction times would be quicker. Most people in the universe haven't just sat through a three-hour Python class before they react to something.”

That's a real time threat, specific to our own data, not a generic textbook warning. The same groups raised a real space threat (the room itself, the specific laptops and phones on hand) and a real sub-groups threat (this is a notably young, tech-comfortable cohort – gaming experience in particular came up as something that could easily be pushing reaction times faster than a more representative population would show).

There's no tick-box version of this. You run an experiment, and you keep asking how what you measured here might fail to generalise, staying honest about the specific threats rather than pretending you've eliminated them. Every real dataset carries some of these threats somewhere. The job is knowing which ones apply to yours.

Part Three

One Sample Among Many

Our Week 1 data is one dataset – one of many that could have been collected. Run the exercise again next week and the numbers move, partly just by chance. Those many possible datasets are, conceptually, repeated samples from the same underlying data-generating process (DGP): the whole tangle of eyesight, dexterity, finger length, attention, and hundreds of other things that combine to produce a reaction time.

Because the data could have come out differently, any statistic computed from it – the mean, say – has its own distribution too. Today's mean differs from next week's mean, which differs from the week after's. That distribution of a statistic, across repeated samples, is called its sampling distribution, and it is worth being completely explicit about why it keeps tripping people up: it is a genuinely different object from the distribution of the variable itself.

Two distributions, not one. This is the single idea worth slowing down for in this whole chapter.
  • The distribution of the variable – Week 1's own histogram, 217 individual reaction times, each one a single tap.
  • The sampling distribution of the mean – what you'd get if you repeated the whole 24-student exercise many times and plotted each repetition's own mean, once per repetition.
The first is built from individual observations. The second is built from statistics, one per imagined repeat of the entire experiment. Mixing the two up is the most common way to get lost in what follows.

Part Four

Building a Sampling Distribution by Hand

Before touching a computer, it's worth building one sampling distribution by hand, on a population small enough to see completely. Take five real students from our own Week 1 data and pretend – just for this exercise – that these five are the entire population we care about. Their true average, across all five, is the one number we actually want.

Now impose an artificial restriction: you're only allowed to sample two of the five at a time. Write out every possible pair, and compute each pair's own average.

Figure

Ten pairs, ten different averages

Ten possible pairs from five people, and ten different averages – some close to the true mean of all five, some a long way off. That spread, from a sample of just two, is the sampling distribution in miniature. Scale this exact idea up to repeated classes of twenty-four, sampled from a much larger population, and you have the real thing.

Part Five

Our Assumed Data-Generating Process

Collapse last week's raw trials down to one average reaction time per student, and the 24 resulting numbers have their own shape – a mean, a spread, a distribution. Fit a Normal curve to that shape (two parameters, mean and standard deviation, exactly as Week 1's Online Session covered) and you get something you can actually simulate from.

Figure

The distribution we're assuming

You may notice this curve allows for negative reaction times – a real side effect of fitting a Normal distribution whose standard deviation (888.6ms) is nearly as large as its own mean (1,070.1ms). Obviously, that can't happen in the real world, and we haven't allowed it in any of the simulated data used in this chapter – every simulated value is floor-clipped at a small positive minimum before use. The underlying statistical ideas we're exploring are unaffected either way.
A quick footnote: this is also where the n−1 correction from Week 1's standard-deviation formula actually earns its keep – the real reason it exists is covered properly in Chapter One, "Measuring the Spread", rather than repeated here.

This Normal(μ, σ) curve is our assumed data-generating process: an assumption we're choosing to make, because we have no way to observe the real one directly. We wouldn't normally treat the result of a one-off data-collection exercise like our reaction-time test this way – but doing so here helps us understand some genuinely powerful underlying statistics, which is exactly why it's worth the slightly artificial setup. Everything else in this chapter follows from taking that assumption seriously enough to compute with it.

Part Six

A Thousand Simulated Classes

Here is the payoff of assuming a DGP: a computer can pretend to rerun last week's experiment. Draw 24 values from Normal(1070.1, 888.6) and average them, and you have one simulated class's mean – a single fresh draw from the sampling distribution, standing in for "what if we did this again next week?"

Show the Python behind the simulation 
import numpy as np

# mu_pop, sd_pop, n_students all come from our own Week 1 data
def simulate_means(n, reps):
    draws = np.random.normal(mu_pop, sd_pop, (reps, n))
    return draws.mean(axis=1)

sample_means = simulate_means(n_students, 1000)  # 1,000 simulated class means

This is the core idea, simplified for reading. The real deck's version adds a floor-clip and a small calibration step, because our class's standard deviation (888.6ms) is almost as large as its mean (1,070.1ms) – left unclipped, an occasional simulated reaction time would come out negative, which isn't physically possible. That correction doesn't change the concept above, just keeps every simulated value plausible.

Same n both times – our real class of 24, and one simulated class of 24 – so the two panels are directly comparable. The simulated class is different in its specific numbers, as it should be, but similar in its overall shape: that's the DGP assumption doing its job.

Two different numbers, both called "n" – don't conflate them. A question from the room, almost word for word: "why does the mean get closer to the truth the more we have?" It's worth being precise about what is growing.
  • Sample size (24 here) – how many students are averaged together inside one simulated class.
  • Number of samples (10, 100, 1,000 here) – how many separate simulated classes you've run, each with its own mean.
Growing the number of samples is what smooths the histogram above and pulls the mean-of-means toward the truth. Growing the sample size does something related but different – it's covered on its own in Part Eight, "The Standard Error."
Part Seven

Why This Works: Unbiasedness and the Central Limit Theorem

The two things Part Six just showed happening both have names, and both are doing real, load-bearing work for the rest of this module.

Unbiasedness

The mean of the sampling distribution equals the true population mean. Track the running average of the simulated class-means as more and more of them accumulate, and watch it settle toward the true 1,070.1ms.

Figure

Converging on the truth

This is the Law of Large Numbers: as the number of independent draws increases, the sample mean converges to the true mean. An estimator whose expected value equals the true parameter is called unbiased.

Asymptotic normality (the Central Limit Theorem)

The sampling distribution of the mean approaches Normal as sample size grows – regardless of the shape of the underlying population. This is the genuinely surprising part. Our own class's reaction times are not Normal at all – Week 1's own histogram was sharply right-skewed, with a long tail. It doesn't matter. Simulate samples from a right-skewed distribution shaped like our own data (same mean, same spread), at three different sample sizes, and compare each histogram against the theoretical Normal curve the CLT predicts.

Figure

From lumpy to Normal, regardless of the starting shape

At n = 5 the histogram is genuinely uneven, and only loosely follows the dashed Normal curve. By n = 30 the two are hard to tell apart, even though every draw still comes from the same right-skewed population. This is the Central Limit Theorem, and it is one of the most useful facts in all of statistics: it's the reason the Normal distribution underpins so much of frequentist statistical testing, even when the raw data it's built from looks nothing like a bell curve.

Neither of these results needed the DGP to actually be Normal – only that it has a finite mean and variance, and that enough independent draws get averaged together. That's a far weaker requirement than it might feel like on first encounter, which is exactly why it shows up everywhere.
Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 5
Part Eight

The Standard Error

Everything in Parts Six and Seven assumed a known population – Normal(1070.1, 888.6) – so we could watch a thousand simulated samples land around the true value. In reality, we have one dataset: the reaction-time data collected last week. No known population mean, no known population standard deviation, and no ability to simulate a thousand more classes. From here there are two ways forward: a formula, or a computational trick called the bootstrap. This part covers the formula; Part Nine covers the bootstrap.

Here's a quietly satisfying fact: by the end of Part Six, you'd already calculated a standard error three times over, without it having a name yet. The standard deviation of your own 10, 100, and 1,000 sample-means is the standard error – a genuinely different quantity from the plain standard deviation of one sample's raw data.

  • Standard deviationThe spread of individual observations within one sample – the 24 students in our own room.
  • Standard errorThe spread of the sampling distribution itself – how much the sample mean would bounce around if you reran the whole experiment many times.

The useful part: you don't need a thousand simulated classes to get it. There's a formula, built directly from the one sample you actually have.

$$SE(\bar{x}) = \frac{SD[x]}{\sqrt{n}}$$

larger when the data is more spread out, smaller when the sample is bigger

Plugged in with our own numbers – SD = 888.6ms, n = 24 – that gives SE ≈ 181.4ms. Checked against the real spread of the 1,000-sample simulation from Part Six (188.5ms), the two land close together – simulation always carries some noise, so they were never going to match to the decimal, but close enough that the agreement itself is the real teaching point.

Root-n convergence

Bigger samples buy precision – but not for free, and not in a straight line. To halve the standard error, you need to quadruple the sample size, because the formula divides by the square root of n, not n itself.

Figure

The standard error shrinks – but not in a straight line

Going from 24 students to 100 would help a reasonable amount; going from 1,000 to 4,000 would barely move the needle. Precision is genuinely expensive, and it gets more expensive the more of it you already have.

Part Nine

The Bootstrap

The formula above needs a known SD and a sample size – both of which we happen to have. But there's a second route to exactly the same destination, one that works even when no formula exists: the bootstrap.

Take the original 24 student-means, and draw a fresh sample of 24 from that same set, with replacement: each draw can be picked more than once, and some of the original 24 might not get picked at all. Compute the mean of that resample. Repeat a few thousand times, and look at the spread of the resulting means – that spread is the bootstrap's own estimate of the standard error, built without ever assuming a DGP at all.

Figure

How a bootstrap sample is built

Three resamples drawn with replacement from the same original sample of 6 – item 4 appears three times in Sample 3, while items 3 and 5 never get drawn there at all. The spread across the resulting statistics is the bootstrap's estimate of the sampling distribution.

Figure · interactive

Bootstrapping our own 24 students

Resamples (B)10

Drag B up from 10 towards 2,000 and watch the bootstrap standard error settle down rather than jump around – once it's settled, it should land close to the 181.4ms the formula already gave. Under the conditions both methods need (a large enough sample, independent observations), the formula and the bootstrap converge on the same answer. The formula is faster to compute; the bootstrap keeps working in situations – more complex statistics, no known formula – where the formula simply doesn't exist.

Part Ten

Confidence Intervals

With a mean and a standard error in hand – by formula or by bootstrap, it no longer matters which – the last step is turning them into a statement about the general pattern our data represents, not just the specific number we happened to measure.

A confidence interval (CI) is a range built symmetrically around the estimate:

$$CI = \text{statistic} \; \pm \; (\text{critical value} \times SE)$$
Confidence levelCritical value
90%1.6 × SE
95%2 × SE (1.96, precisely)
99%2.6 × SE
Task, in the workshop. Using our own Week 1 data: collapse the raw trials down to one average reaction time per student, find the mean, SD, and n of those student averages, and use them to calculate SE(x̄) and the 95% CI for mean reaction time – by hand or in Excel, before reading on.

You should get roughly this: plugged in with our own numbers, 1,070.1 ± (1.96 × 181.4) gives a 95% confidence interval of [714.6, 1425.6] ms. In words: in the general pattern our data represents, we can be 95% confident the true average reaction time falls somewhere in that range.

Wider interval → more confident it contains the truth, but less precise about where. There's no free lunch: more confidence costs precision, every time.

Where the CI actually sits on the curve

The confidence interval isn't a separate idea from the Normal distribution – it's a direct slice of it. Here is the sampling distribution of our mean (Normal, centred on 1,070.1ms, spread by the standard error of 181.4ms – not the raw DGP from Part Five, the narrower distribution of the mean itself), with the 95% CI shaded as the middle region it actually is.

Figure

The 95% CI, shaded on its own sampling distribution

The shaded region holds 95% of the area under the curve; the two unshaded tails hold 2.5% each. The critical value, 1.96, is simply how many standard errors out from the centre you have to go before you've cut off exactly those tails.

Part Eleven

Quick Recap – and What's Next

  • We want to generalise from our data – that's statistical inference, and the gap between what we can claim and what we actually measured is external validity.
  • Any statistic computed from a sample has its own sampling distribution – a genuinely different object from the distribution of the raw variable.
  • Two ways to estimate the uncertainty in a statistic: a formula (\(SE = SD/\sqrt{n}\)) or the bootstrap (resample with replacement, look at the spread). Under the right conditions, they agree.
  • A 95% CI is the estimate ± (critical value × SE) – a range we expect to contain the true value.
  • Using that CI, we can finally make a statement about the population – not just the room.

Nearly three hours of workshop led up to one sentence: we can be 95% confident the true average reaction time lies between roughly 715 and 1,426 milliseconds. It doesn't sound like much. It's the first time in this module anything has been said about a population we never directly measured – and everything from here on builds on exactly that move.
