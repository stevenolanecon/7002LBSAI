Count outcomes

The workshop covered a 0/1 outcome. What about an outcome that can be 0, 1, 2, 3… with no natural ceiling?

How many support tickets?

Business question: a software company wants to plan its support team. Which customer accounts raise the most support tickets, and does onboarding training cut them?

  • Data on 600 customer accounts over the last six months (generated for this module)
  • tickets: support tickets the account raised over the period
  • users: seats on the account (5 to 150)
  • plan: Basic, Pro or Enterprise
  • trained: 1 if the account had onboarding training
  • months_active: how many of the six months the account was active. 182 accounts joined partway through

  • Whole numbers, piled up near zero, with a long right tail. That’s what count data usually looks like

Why not just use OLS?

ols_model = smf.ols("tickets ~ users + trained + plan", data=df).fit()
(ols_model.fittedvalues < 0).sum()
  • OLS predicts a negative number of tickets for 12 accounts (the lowest is -0.38). No account can raise fewer than zero tickets
  • OLS also assumes each extra seat adds the same number of tickets, whatever the account. With counts, effects are usually proportional: training cuts tickets by a share, not by a fixed number
  • The same two problems as the LPM in the workshop: predictions outside the possible range, and a straight line where the real relationship isn’t one

Same logic, new distribution

Everything from the workshop still applies: a link function connects a linear index to the outcome, and raw coefficients aren’t read directly.

  • Binary y: the link keeps predictions between 0 and 1 (logistic or normal CDF)
  • Count y: the link needs to keep predictions non-negative. No upper bound is needed, but never below zero
  • The natural choice: \(E[y|x] = \exp(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots)\), the log link

Poisson regression

\[\ln(E[y|x]) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots\]

  • The Poisson distribution is the standard choice for count data. It plays the same role the logistic or normal assumption played for binary y
  • Fit by maximum likelihood, like logit and probit
  • Because of the log link, coefficients are additive on the log scale and multiplicative on the count scale. The same idea as a log–level model from Week 5

Fitting it, in code

import statsmodels.api as sm
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

poisson_model = smf.glm("tickets ~ users + trained + plan", data=df,
                        family=sm.families.Poisson(),
                        exposure=df["months_active"]).fit()
  • smf.glm fits any GLM; family= says which one
  • plan is text, so Basic (first alphabetically) is the reference category
  • exposure= tells the model how long each account was observed. More on that in a moment

Why the exposure?

Some accounts were only active for part of the six months. An account that joined last month has had one month to raise tickets, not six.

So the thing we really want to model is the rate: tickets per month. With the log link that’s easy:

\[\ln(E[\text{tickets}]) = \ln(\text{months}) + \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{users} + \dots\]

\(\ln(\text{months})\) goes in with its coefficient fixed at 1. That term is called an offset. exposure=df["months_active"] adds it for you.

Every coefficient is now about tickets per month, whatever length of time each account was observed.

What if we left it out?

Exposure included Exposure left out
trained (IRR) 0.642 0.507
  • Training was introduced recently, so newer accounts are much more likely to have had it: 72% of the 182 newer accounts against 28% of the rest
  • Newer accounts have had fewer months to raise tickets. Leave the exposure out and training takes the credit for that
  • This is omitted variable bias again (Week 6). Time observed is the omitted variable

From coefficients to incidence rate ratios

Exponentiating a coefficient gives an incidence rate ratio (IRR):

\[IRR = \exp(\beta_1)\]

A one-unit increase in \(x_1\) multiplies the expected count by the IRR. It doesn’t add to it.

  • \(IRR > 1\): the expected count goes up. 1.25 means 25% more
  • \(IRR < 1\): the expected count goes down. 0.80 means 20% fewer
  • \(IRR = 1\): no difference

The IRRs

trained 0.642 0.570 to 0.724

Baseline rate (the exponentiated intercept): 0.191 tickets a month for a Basic, untrained account with zero seats.

Reading a continuous IRR: seats

  • The IRR on users is 1.0093: each extra seat multiplies expected tickets by 1.0093, so about 0.9% more per seat
  • For 10 more seats, raise the IRR to the power 10: 1.009310 = 1.097, about 9.7% more tickets
  • Not quite 10 × 0.93%, because the increases compound. In code: np.exp(10 * poisson_model.params["users"])

Reading a dummy IRR: training

  • The IRR on trained is 0.642
  • Trained accounts raise 36% fewer tickets a month than untrained accounts on the same plan with the same number of seats
  • The 95% CI runs from 0.570 to 0.724: somewhere between 28% and 43% fewer
  • Is it causal? Only if nothing else differs between trained and untrained accounts. The firm chose who got training, so be careful (Week 9 takes this on properly)

Reading a categorical IRR: the plan

  • Basic is the reference category. Each plan IRR compares that plan with Basic
  • Pro: 1.321. Pro accounts raise about 32% more tickets a month than Basic accounts with the same seats and training
  • Enterprise: 1.705. About 71% more than Basic
  • Enterprise against Pro? Divide the two IRRs: 1.705 / 1.321 = 1.29, about 29% more

A predicted count for one account

account = pd.DataFrame({"users": [50], "trained": [0], "plan": ["Pro"]})
poisson_model.predict(account, exposure=[6])
  • A Pro account with 50 seats and no training, active for all six months: 2.40 tickets expected, about 0.40 a month
  • The same account with training: 2.40 × 0.642 = 1.54
  • Predictions are never negative, whatever values we plug in

A closer look

Is Poisson actually a good fit here?

Poisson’s hidden assumption

Poisson assumes equidispersion: the variance of \(y\) equals its mean, given the x’s. \(Var(y|x) = E(y|x)\).

poisson_model.pearson_chi2 / poisson_model.df_resid
  • This dispersion statistic should be close to 1 if Poisson’s assumption holds. Here it’s 2.29
  • The variance is about twice what Poisson allows, after the x’s have done their work. That’s overdispersion
  • Don’t just compare the raw mean and variance (2.30 and 8.03). Some of that spread is explained by the x’s

You can see it in the zeros

Why this matters

Overdispersion doesn’t usually bias the IRRs much. It makes the standard errors too small, so every result looks more certain than it is.

We want a model with the same log link and the same IRRs, that lets the variance be bigger than the mean.

Negative binomial — the fix

  • Same log link, same IRRs, same exposure. Reading the model doesn’t change
  • Adds one extra parameter, α, that lets the variance exceed the mean: \(Var(y|x) = \mu + \alpha\mu^2\)
  • α = 0 takes you back to Poisson
  • The practical rule: check the dispersion statistic; if it’s well above 1, use negative binomial

Fitting it, in code

nb_model = smf.negativebinomial("tickets ~ users + trained + plan", data=df,
                                exposure=df["months_active"]).fit()
  • α is estimated from the data: 0.543 (SE 0.066), clearly above zero
  • The red-versus-green lines on the zeros chart: negative binomial predicts 29% zeros, against 30% in the data

The real consequence: standard errors

Poisson SE Neg. binomial SE Ratio
users 0.0007 0.0010 1.5×
trained 0.0613 0.0901 1.5×
plan: Pro 0.0610 0.0939 1.5×
plan: Enterprise 0.0717 0.1207 1.7×

Negative binomial standard errors are 1.5 to 1.7 times larger. The Poisson model was overconfident about every coefficient. Every effect here is still clearly significant, but on other data a “significant” Poisson result can disappear.

And the IRRs?

Poisson IRR Neg. binomial IRR
users 1.009 1.009
trained 0.642 0.641
plan: Pro 1.321 1.281
plan: Enterprise 1.705 1.698

They barely move. Here negative binomial changes how sure we are, not the answer. With other data the estimates can shift too, so it’s worth comparing.

Other kinds of y, briefly

  • Ordinal or nominal outcomes (satisfaction ratings, product categories) can often be recast as binary, such as “top box or not”. You lose some information but get a model you already know how to run
  • Durations (time until an event) are often censored: you don’t see the full duration for everyone. OLS on censored durations gets the direction right but not the size. Converting to a binary “did the event happen by time T” is often the practical choice

Summary

  • A count outcome needs predictions that are never negative: the log link, giving Poisson regression
  • When units are observed for different lengths of time, add the exposure (an offset) so you model the rate. Leaving it out can bias the estimates
  • Read coefficients as incidence rate ratios, exp(β): they multiply the expected count
  • Poisson assumes the variance equals the mean. Check the dispersion statistic; if it’s well above 1, use negative binomial, which mainly widens the standard errors
