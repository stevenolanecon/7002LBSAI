The question

A company has seen rising staff turnover and wants to understand why employees are leaving – and whether there’s anything the organisation could realistically do about it.

Produce a statistical analysis of attrition and advise leadership on how much confidence they should place in your conclusions.

There is no single right model. You choose which variables to explore, which to include and which to set aside – and you justify those choices.

Two of you can reach different conclusions and both score well, if the reasoning behind each is sound.

What we’re marking

You are marked on your statistical judgement: what you chose to model, why, what you checked, and how well you communicate to a decision-maker what your analysis can and can’t support.

We’re not marking:

  • Your code. Inelegant code that works loses nothing – but clean code is still worth aiming for.
  • Whether you find “the” answer. There isn’t just one. A different, well-argued model scores as well as mine would.
  • Data cleaning. The data is already clean. You don’t need a cleaning narrative.

The data

  • HR records for 1,470 employees at one organisation: role, pay, working conditions, career history
  • 31 variables, no missing values – the data dictionary is in the brief
  • The outcome is Attrition: did the employee leave? Yes or No – a binary dependent variable.

You don’t need every variable

There are 30 possible explanatory variables. You are not expected to explore or model all of them in your notebook.

Strong submissions pick a focused set – guided by a clear question about why people might leave – explore those properly, and explain why the rest were set aside.

A smaller model you can justify and stress-test is better than a large one you can’t explain.

Good reasons to set a variable aside:

  • it overlaps heavily with another variable you’ve kept
  • it isn’t something the business can change, and you don’t need it as a control
  • it might be a result of someone deciding to leave, rather than a cause
  • it barely varies

Two things the rubric is looking for

Your exploration should drive your model. Top marks for EDA go to work where the patterns you find directly and explicitly justify your modelling choices. A page of summary statistics that the model never refers back to won’t get there.

Synthesis, not just analysis. This is the biggest criterion, at 35%. A correct model, correctly reported, with no alternative explanation considered, matches the Needs Improvement descriptor: “little or no engagement with alternative explanations”.

To go higher, weigh a competing explanation against your own – a confounder you might have missed, a control that might be a bad one, a different specification – and argue for the position you take.

Worked example

Used cars

The question: what does mileage cost a seller?

281 used Toyota Camrys for sale in Chicago, from Week 6’s data exercises. odometer is in units of 10,000 miles.

smf.ols('price ~ odometer', data=cars).fit(cov_type='HC1')

Each extra 10,000 miles goes with a price 734 dollars lower (robust SE 31). R² = 0.72.

A basic write-up would stop here: “mileage is significant, so lower your mileage to get a better price.”

What story is this regression telling, and what else could explain the same pattern?

The competing explanation: age

Older cars have done more miles: the correlation between age and mileage is 0.74. Older cars are also cheaper for reasons that have nothing to do with miles – newer models, wear, fashion.

So some of that mileage effect may really be an age effect.

smf.ols('price ~ odometer + age', data=cars).fit(cov_type='HC1')

Holding age fixed, 10,000 more miles goes with a price 385 dollars lower – about half the 734 we started with. Age was a confounder: leaving it out roughly doubled the mileage effect.

But should age be controlled for at all?

It depends on the question you’re answering – explaining this is the synthesis.

  • Seller’s question: “what do my extra miles cost me?” Age is a confounder. Control for it.
  • Buyer’s question: “how much cheaper is a car that’s two years older?” Older cars are cheaper partly because they’ve done more miles. Mileage is one route by which age lowers price, so controlling for it is a bad control – it takes away part of the effect you want.

On age alone, each extra year goes with a price 664 dollars lower. Holding mileage fixed, it’s 412. Neither number is wrong – they answer different questions.

Once age is in, the mileage effect stays between about 300 and 390 dollars whatever else we add. The big change comes from age; the rest barely moves it. That’s what makes the answer worth trusting.

What we can and can’t claim

We can say: holding age fixed, cars with 10,000 more miles sell for roughly 300–400 dollars less, and that range survives every specification we tried.

We can’t say:

  • that this is the causal cost of driving 10,000 miles – CAUSAL KLAXON! Accident history and servicing aren’t in the data, and high-mileage cars may differ in both
  • that it holds beyond Camrys listed in Chicago – the external validity of the model
  • much about condition: sellers rate it themselves, so it’s measured with noise

Each of these limitations is specific to this analysis. “Correlation is not causation” on its own earns very little – say which missing variable worries you and why.

Writing it up

Weaker: “We ran a regression of price on mileage. The coefficient is −734 and significant at 1%, so mileage reduces price. We then added age and the R² went up, so the second model is better.”

Stronger: “Mileage alone suggests 10,000 extra miles cost a seller about 730 dollars. But older cars have done more miles (r = 0.74) and are cheaper for other reasons, so this mixes the two. Holding age fixed, the marginal cost of 10,000 extra miles falls to about 390 dollars on average. This stays between 300 and 390 when we allow age to have a curved effect or add dealer and condition as explanatory variables. We control for age because the question is the seller’s; for a buyer comparing car ages, mileage would be a bad control, because it’s one of the ways age lowers price. Unrecorded accident history could still bias this, so we treat it as an association, not a price the seller is guaranteed.”

The stronger version names the alternative, shows what changed, explains the choice, and is honest about what’s left.

Advising leadership

The conclusion is for a manager, not a statistician.

Weaker: “Mileage has a statistically significant negative effect on price (p < 0.001).”

Stronger: “Expect each 10,000 miles to knock roughly 300–400 dollars off a Camry’s price. We’re fairly confident in that range, because it holds across every model we tried. We can’t rule out that high-mileage cars also differ in ways our data doesn’t record, so use it as a guide when pricing, not a guarantee.”

State the size of the effect, how sure you are, why, and what would change your mind.

Common mistakes

  • Code without narrative. Output with no sentence saying what it shows earns nothing
  • Exploration that goes nowhere. Every chart in your EDA should feed a modelling decision
  • Everything in the model, nothing explained. Each variable you include or drop needs a reason
  • Causal language you can’t back up. “Drives”, “causes”, “leads to” need an argument, not just a p-value
  • One model, no alternatives. On your biggest criterion, that’s the Needs Improvement band
  • Generic limitations. Name the specific variable or problem that worries you
  • A conclusion that says “significant” and stops. Tell leadership how big, how sure, and what to do
