Distributions and Summarising Data

Part One

Why Explore Before You Model

Ten years ago, I was sitting more or less where you are. Starting a master's in economics, grey hairs (mostly) not yet arrived, mildly terrified, with only the vaguest idea what the next twelve months held. What got me through it was a handful of tutors who were willing to explain things properly rather than just wave at the textbook. That's the model for this module: not a shortcut past the statistics, but a proper explanation of it, built from data you can actually see.

This module goes by the name Research Methods in Analytics, which is really a slightly formal way of asking one question: what can we do with data to help us make better decisions? Over eleven weeks that question runs from the simplest possible description of a single variable through to more complex machine learning models. It starts here, with the least glamorous and most consequential step in the whole pipeline – looking properly at your data before you do anything else with it.

The formal name for that step is exploratory data analysis, or EDA. Békés & Kézdi define it plainly: it is the process of describing your variables – the values they take, how often they take them, and whether anything about them is unusual. That sounds almost too simple to spend a whole chapter on. It isn't. Before you fit a model, run a test, or make a claim, you have to know what your data actually looks like, and that turns out to be a much deeper task than opening a spreadsheet and skimming a few rows.

We can frame the whole exercise as three questions you should be able to answer before any modelling begins:

  • How should you start the analysis itself?
  • How do you describe the data and present its key features?
  • How do you explore the data and check whether it's clean enough for further analysis?

This module takes data cleaning as a given – by the time a dataset reaches you here, assume someone has already done the unglamorous but essential work of fixing malformed entries and missing fields. (Your Programming for Business Analytics module is where that skill actually gets built.) But even a perfectly clean dataset still needs exploring, and there are five distinct reasons it's worth the time:

  1. It sense-checks your cleaning. EDA isn't a one-off step before cleaning starts – it's part of an iterative loop. Exploring the data can reveal that it needs more cleaning than you thought.
  2. It guides what comes next. What you see shapes what you do. A skewed variable, a suspicious cluster, an unexpected gap – each one changes the questions worth asking.
  3. It gives you context. Statistical and econometric modelling are tools in service of a question, not the point of the exercise. Understanding the data's context makes your eventual insights richer and more relevant to whoever has to act on them.
  4. It can refine the research question itself. Research is rarely a single moment of inspiration followed by a straight line to an answer – it's iterative, and exploration is often what sharpens a vague question into a useful one.
  5. Occasionally, it just answers the question. Not often. But sometimes a good look at the data is all it takes.
From the workshop

“One of the things you're going to be learning to be is an analyst – and one thing you have to understand is the limits of what we can understand. When you're presenting your results, in a workshop or a conference, it's important not to overplay the confidence you have in them.”

Everything that follows in this chapter is really an extended answer to that first, deceptively simple instruction: look at your data properly, before you do anything else with it.

Part Two

The Business Question

The scenario. A regional bank's customer service centre measures agent response time – how quickly an agent reacts to an incoming alert. It's treated as a key operational metric. HR's current process records one test score per agent, taken once at onboarding, and uses that single number to rank and place staff.

The question this workshop exists to answer is whether that's remotely sensible: is a single measurement an adequate way to capture this metric? Rather than take the question on faith, the class spent the first twenty minutes standing in for the bank's agents, and generating real evidence rather than debating the point in the abstract.

This is a deliberately hackneyed scenario – a fairly ordinary problem, chosen precisely because ordinary problems are where most business analytics work actually happens. Nobody is curing anything. But the reasoning that follows from it – about single measurements, noise, and what a distribution can tell you that one number can't – generalises far beyond reaction times. It's the same logic behind why a single sales figure doesn't tell you a rep's true performance, why one satisfaction survey response doesn't tell you how a customer really feels, and why a single A/B test result on its own rarely settles anything.

Part Three

Generating Our Own Data

Each student worked through a short reaction-time tool on their own phone or laptop: enter your name, pick your device, answer whether you'd had caffeine in the last two hours, measure your own index finger in millimetres, then complete ten trials – tap as soon as the screen turns from red to green. Results saved automatically to a shared, live dataset the moment each student finished.

It was, by design, a slightly rough first attempt – and that turned out to be its own useful lesson. The instructions didn't specify exactly where to measure a finger from, or make it obvious the test had to run all ten times before results counted. A few students improvised. That's not really a flaw in the exercise; it's what a pilot is for. Running something for the first time reliably surfaces the ambiguities you didn't know you'd left in, and the honest response – in this room and in your own future projects – is not to pretend everything went perfectly, but to notice what went wrong, say so, and account for it in what you do next.

What are we actually measuring?

This cuts straight to the heart of a genuinely important idea: given that the instructions weren't perfectly precise, might the class be measuring the wrong thing? Finger length is a real, physical, measurable quantity – it isn't drifting up and down over the course of the workshop – but the tool used to capture it (a ruler, in the hands of a slightly rushed workshop partner) introduces the possibility of measurement error: a gap between the true value of the thing you care about and the number you actually record.

From the workshop

“The bank isn't really interested in your index finger. It's a proxy for your speed, your ability, the physical limits on how fast you can react. That's something we can measure directly in a classroom – but sometimes the thing you actually want to know is one you can't observe at all, and you're stuck relying on a proxy for it, like using someone's qualifications as a proxy for their ability. Those proxies are sometimes useful and sometimes not. Knowing which is part of the job.”

Keep that distinction in mind for the rest of the module: the variable in a spreadsheet is often not quite the same thing as the real-world quantity it's standing in for, and the gap between the two – how it opened up, how large it might be – is something every analyst has to reason about explicitly, not assume away.

From ten numbers to a table

Ten reaction times from one student, on their own, are a limited way to tell a story. They can hint at something – a slow first attempt as someone gets a feel for the task, then a settling-in – but they don't summarise well, and multiplied by twenty-four students they're unmanageable. The fix is a data format every business analyst ends up living in: tidy data, where every row is one observation and every column is one variable.

Here, every row is one trial: one tap, by one student, with that trial's own reaction time, and that student's device, caffeine status, and finger length copied alongside it. Ten trials per student means ten rows per student. Filtering, grouping, and plotting all become a matter of picking a column, once the data is shaped this way.

Part Four

Types of Variable

The reaction-time tool captured five variables per trial, and each one behaves differently – which matters, because the type of a variable determines which summaries and visualisations are even meaningful for it.

VariableTypeScaleWhy
NameCategoricalNominalLabels with no inherent order
Reaction timeContinuousRatioAny value down to the millisecond; zero has a true meaning
DeviceCategoricalNominalPhone vs. laptop – describes, doesn't rank
CaffeineBinaryNominalExactly two possible states
Finger lengthContinuousRatioSame reasoning as reaction time

The distinction worth dwelling on is between a plain nominal categorical variable, like device, and an ordinal one, where the order genuinely carries information – the classic example is a Likert-scale survey response (strongly disagree … strongly agree), where reordering the categories would make the variable meaningless. Trial number, in this dataset, is a quiet example of an ordinal variable that isn't obviously one at first glance: it isn't just a label, because later trials plausibly mean something different from earlier ones, as a student settles into the task.

Why this matters in practice: it is entirely possible to ask statistical software to compute the mean of device, once it's coded numerically (laptop = 1, phone = 2, say). Python will not stop you unless you code correctly. In such cases, it will hand back a number like 1.75. That number describes nothing real – there is no such thing as "a bit more phone than laptop." Knowing a variable's type isn't academic box-ticking; it's what stops you from reporting nonsense with a straight face.

One genuinely useful special case that we return to properly later in this chapter: a binary variable coded 0/1 behaves unusually well under a mean. Averaging a column of 0s and 1s doesn't produce nonsense the way averaging device does – it produces the proportion of the sample coded 1. That single fact is going to do a surprising amount of work later on.

Part Six

Frequency, Probability, and Distribution

With the data assembled, our first real analytical act was almost absurdly simple: count how many students used a laptop, and how many used a phone. Simple, but worth doing carefully, because the vocabulary built here – absolute frequency, relative frequency, probability – is going to reappear constantly for the rest of the module.

  • Absolute frequency – the raw count of observations with a particular value.
  • Relative frequency – that count expressed as a proportion or percentage of all observations.
  • Probability generalises relative frequency: a measure of likelihood, always between 0 and 1, that – unlike relative frequency – can describe an event without needing a dataset in hand at all.
  • The distribution of a variable is the frequency of every value it takes, independent of what any other variable in the data shows.

Getting there from the raw, ten-rows-per-student data took an unglamorous detour through Excel – select the data, insert a pivot table, drag Device into rows, and (after a couple of false starts on exactly which aggregation to use, since "sum" and "average" both make no sense for a category) settle on a count. It's not elegant. It's also exactly the kind of rough, get-an-answer-now tool every analyst reaches for constantly, right alongside the more sophisticated code you'll build up over the rest of this module.

Figure

The device split works out to sixteen laptops against eight phones – a relative frequency of 66.7% laptop, 33.3% phone. Caffeine is more one-sided still: only six of twenty-four students, a flat 25%, had had any in the two hours before the workshop.

From the workshop

“Relative frequency is often far more powerful than the raw number. I can tell you UK national debt is about £1.4 trillion, and that sounds enormous – but on its own it doesn't mean much unless you already live in these figures. What people actually report, almost always, is debt as a percentage of GDP: how much the country owes relative to what it produces in a year. The relative version is what gives you context.”

Probability slots in naturally once a variable is binary. If caffeine is coded 0/1, then the relative frequency of the "1" category – 25% here – is exactly what you'd call the probability that a student picked at random from this room had had caffeine recently. Same number, two different names, depending on whether you're describing a sample you've already collected or reasoning about a future draw from it.

Part Seven

Seeing a Distribution: Histograms

Statistics condense; they don't replace looking. However good your summary numbers are, it is always worth actually looking at the data – and the single most useful way to look at one continuous variable's distribution is the histogram: a bar chart where bars show how many observations (or what percentage) fall into each of a series of equal-width bins.

Reading one well means looking for four things at once:

FeatureWhat to look for
ModesPeaks that stand out from their neighbourhood
Centre & tailsWhere most of the mass sits, and how it thins out
SymmetryRoughly balanced, or a long tail to one side?
Extreme valuesPoints sitting far out from everything else

Even with the long tail trimmed off for readability, the shape is unmistakably right-skewed: most trials cluster in a tight band at the low end, and a thinning tail extends out toward slower and slower times. That shape isn't an accident of this particular class – it's close to universal for reaction-time data, and worth understanding why. There's a hard physical floor (nobody can react in zero milliseconds; brain-to-finger signalling takes a minimum amount of time), but no equivalent ceiling – distraction, hesitation, a missed tap, a conversation with a neighbour can all stretch a trial arbitrarily far to the right – to infinity in theory, but realistically up until the end of the workshop. Floors without ceilings produce right skew almost by construction.

Bin width is a choice, not a default

Drag the bin-width slider from 10ms up to 80ms and the same 217 numbers tell different versions of the story. Too narrow, and the bars become spiky and hard to read as one coherent shape. Too wide, and real structure – a genuine second cluster, a gap – gets smoothed away into something that looks more gradual and more boring than the data actually is. There's no universally correct bin width; there's only a choice, made deliberately, with an eye on what it does to the story the chart tells.

Toggling on the KDE (kernel density estimate) shows an alternative to binning entirely: instead of sorting values into fixed buckets, it places a small smooth "bump" over every single observation and sums them, producing a continuous curve rather than a bar chart. It avoids the arbitrariness of a bin-width choice, and is useful for comparing two distributions side by side where two overlapping sets of bars would turn into visual noise. But it comes at a cost: a KDE can visually smooth away real, sharp structure – a hard floor at 259ms, for instance, doesn't come through as sharply in the smoothed curve as it does in the bars. Treat the histogram as the primary source of truth, and the KDE as a secondary, headline view of its shape.

What Do We Do With an Outlier?

Turn the exclusion threshold on the histogram above all the way up, and one trial sits dramatically apart from everything else – not a little slower than the rest, but on a completely different scale. Where the bulk of the class's reaction times cluster under a second, this single trial clocked in north of fifty-six seconds.

Figure

Spotting the outlier

From the workshop

“This is about as slam-dunk a case as you'll ever get. Whoever produced that trial almost certainly got distracted, or forgot to complete it, or simply hadn't understood the task properly – and the fact that it sits sixty times further out than the next-slowest trial tells you that on its own. It's as if I set out to measure everyone's height in this room, was doing perfectly well, and then a fifteen-metre person walked in. That one observation would wreck every statistic I tried to compute afterwards.”

The instinct to just delete anything that looks strange is understandable, but one to be resisted. Every extreme value deserves the same three questions, asked in this order:

QuestionIf yes…Reaction-time example
Is it an error?Drop it, or fix itA 4ms trial – almost certainly a mis-click before the screen actually turned green
Is it real, but not what we're studying?Drop it, with a noteA slow trial because the student was mid-conversation with a neighbour
Is it a genuine feature of the process?Keep itA slow trial from real hesitation – that is operational variability worth measuring

The 56-second trial clears the first bar easily – it is, by any reasonable account, an error, not a genuine reaction time. But the second question is where the real judgement lives, and it can't be reduced to an algorithm. Suppose a trial came in unusually slow not because of a technical glitch, but because the student was genuinely talking to a neighbour mid-experiment. That response is real – nobody mis-clicked – but it's measuring something the study was never designed to capture (distraction), not the quantity of actual interest (reaction speed). Dropping it is defensible, but only with a stated reason, not as an unexplained deletion. In reality, this kind of data cleaning will be done in python and so every decision will be noted in your script.

The default, stated plainly: always work from the position that you keep as much of your data as you possibly can. Treat every exclusion as something that needs a justification strong enough to survive someone else reading your working, not something you wave through by default. Different analysts, working from the same raw data, can reasonably reach different decisions about the same outlier – which is precisely why documenting why you dropped or kept something matters as much as the decision itself.

And the context genuinely changes the right call. A cluster of ten trials all sitting around the one-minute mark for a single student wouldn't look like ten separate errors – it would look like one student who takes their time, which might be exactly the operational information a business wants to know about their staff. The decision isn't a fixed rule waiting to be looked up; it depends on domain knowledge, on the shape of the rest of the distribution, and on what the eventual answer is actually going to be used for.

Part Nine

Summary Statistics I: Measuring the Centre

A statistic is any meaningful number you can compute from data. Summary statistics are the ones that describe the most important features of a distribution – its centre, its spread, its shape – collapsing a whole column of numbers into a handful worth reporting.

The mean, and the idea behind it

The workshop's one real concession to notation: writing a variable as \(x\), its mean (or average) is

$$\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x_i}{n}$$

sum every observation, divide by how many there are

That little line above the \(x\) is known as the bar and it is the standard notation to signal the mean of a variable. In words rather than symbols: add up every single reaction time in the dataset, and divide by the number of trials that went into that sum. Two small algebraic properties fall straight out of this and are worth keeping in your back pocket for when regression arrives in a few weeks – if \(a\) and \(b\) are fixed while \(x\) varies, then \(\overline{x+a} = \bar{x} + a\) and \(\overline{x \cdot b} = \bar{x} \cdot b\).

The deeper idea sitting underneath the mean is the expected value, \(E[x]\): the value you'd expect from one randomly chosen observation. The class's own mean reaction time is mildly interesting in its own right, but it only becomes properly useful once you treat it as an estimate of something bigger than the room – the expected response time of a wider population this class is standing in for.

That's the payoff promised back in the variable-types section: for a quantitative variable, \(E[x]\) is just the mean. For a binary variable coded 0/1, the mean stops being a meaningless average of labels and becomes something genuinely useful – the probability, or relative frequency, of the category coded 1.

From the workshop

“If I grab one of you at random and ask whether you've had coffee in the last two hours, you'll give me a yes or a no – not a percentage. But the expected value of that binary variable, across the room, is a probability: it's asking, if I reached into this room and pulled out one person at random, what's the chance they'd say yes? Based on what we actually collected, that's 25%.”

Median, quantiles, mode

  • MedianThe middle value once sorted – exactly half the observations lie below it, half above.
  • QuantilesAny division of sorted data into proportions. Quartiles cut it into fourths (the median is the 2nd quartile, the 50th percentile); deciles into tenths; percentiles into hundredths.
  • ModeThe single most frequent value. Some distributions genuinely have more than one. This can be a sign of different distributions for different subgroups of the population.

Pick a student below and watch where the mean and median land on their own ten trials – then try a student whose data includes a noticeably slow outlier, and watch the gap between the two open up.

Mean – sum ÷ \(n\). The balance point of the data; sensitive to every single value, including the extreme ones. Median – the middle value; robust to extremes almost by construction, since moving the largest value further out doesn't move the middle at all. Mode – most useful for discrete or categorical data, where "the average category" wouldn't mean anything but "the most common one" clearly does.

When mean and median pull apart, this provides important information. It is, almost always, a direct symptom of the shape of the underlying distribution, and specifically of skew.

Part Ten

Summary Statistics II: Measuring the Spread

Two datasets can share an identical mean and still look nothing alike – one tightly clustered, one scattered wide. Centre alone never tells you that; you need a separate measure of spread.

$$Var[x] = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}{n-1}$$
$$SD[x] = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}{n-1}}$$

variance, and its square root, standard deviation

Variance is the average squared deviation from the mean – squared partly so positive and negative deviations don't just cancel out to zero, and partly because it makes the algebra behind everything from regression to hypothesis testing far more tractable later in the module. It's harder to interpret directly, precisely because squaring the deviations also squares their units. Standard deviation undoes that by taking the square root, landing back in the original units – roughly, the typical distance between a randomly chosen observation and the mean.

Why \(n-1\), not \(n\)? This module divides by \(n-1\) – the standard convention, and what Python's pandas library uses by default. Békés & Kézdi's own textbook divides by \(n\) instead, which is harmless at the scale of their case studies but can matter when working with smaller scale samples. The reason is called Bessel's correction: an unbiased estimate of the population variance divides squared deviations by \(n-1\), not \(n\), because the sample mean \(\bar{x}\) already fits this exact sample as closely as possible – squared deviations measured from it slightly understate the true spread. Dividing by \(n-1\) corrects for spending one number's worth of information calculating \(\bar{x}\) from these same values in the first place. It's not a huge deal in practice – just worth knowing why the formula looks the way it does, rather than taking \(n-1\) on faith.

In finance, standard deviation is volatility, quoted directly: a higher SD on an asset's price literally means it jumps around more relative to its own average. The same idea, unchanged, describes a call centre's response times, a factory's output, or a portfolio's returns.

SD and variance aren't the only way to describe spread, and each of the alternatives makes a different trade-off against sensitivity to extreme values. Step through the four below on one student's own ten trials, and notice how the highlighted band changes – and how much that outlier-sensitive stripe of "±1 SD" shifts compared to the sturdier IQR band once a slow trial is in the mix.

Standardising, and a first word on skew

Expressing a value in units of standard deviation – standardising it – makes values from entirely different scales directly comparable:

$$x_{\text{standardised}} = \frac{x - \bar{x}}{SD[x]}$$

A standardised value of 1 reads as "one SD above the mean"; −1 as "one SD below." This idea returns properly once the module starts comparing variables measured on genuinely different scales.

Skew gets a simple, working definition here (a fuller statistical treatment can wait): a distribution is skewed if it isn't symmetric, and a serviceable measure of how skewed is

$$\text{Skewness} \approx \frac{\bar{x} - \text{median}(x)}{SD[x]}$$

When a long right tail drags the mean up above the median, this comes out positive – exactly the story the pooled reaction-time histogram already told, before it had a number attached to it.

Part Eleven

Box Plots and Group Comparisons

A box plot packages several of the statistics from the last two sections into one compact, comparable picture – genuinely useful the moment you want to line up more than one group side by side, which a single histogram does badly.

The whiskers reach to the most extreme point still within 1.5×IQR of the box – not the fence itself, and not the true min/max. Anything further out is drawn as its own separate point, an outside value: the same fence, in other words, that flagged the 56-second trial two sections ago. A violin plot carries the same median and IQR, but replaces the box with the full smoothed shape of the distribution – the same kernel-density idea from the histogram section, mirrored either side of a centre line.

Applied to the class's actual data, grouped by device or by whether a student had had caffeine, the box plot becomes the tool for answering this chapter's real running question.

Part Twelve

So – Was One Test Score Enough?

Back to HR's onboarding test: one trial, one number, one ranking. Every one of you just produced ten. When you look back at your own numbers – how does the spread between your own fastest and slowest trial compare with the differences visible between different students entirely?

From the workshop

“A single measurement doesn't tell you someone's true, typical response time. It tells you where one random draw from their own distribution happened to land. What we're really after is the signal – a person's genuine, underlying reaction time – and every individual trial carries some amount of noise on top of it: sometimes a little faster, sometimes a little slower, and every so often, a lot slower. One of the central jobs of an analyst is learning not to mistake the noise for the signal.”

Ranking twenty-four agents on a single tap each would have meant ranking them substantially on who happened to get a lucky or unlucky draw that morning, not on any real, stable difference in ability. Ten trials, and a distribution built from them, is a materially better estimate of the signal than any one of those ten numbers taken alone.

But... it is important to remember this room is not the population anyone actually cares about. Twenty-four master's students on a Friday afternoon are not the bank's real agent workforce, and even within this room, the phone-versus-laptop gap and the caffeine gap, real as the numbers are, are being read off a sample, not the full population of interest. Any sample will show differences between groups purely by chance – run this exact experiment again next week with the same twenty-four people, and the numbers would move even if nothing about them had genuinely changed.

Deciding which differences are worth taking seriously, and which are just the ordinary noise of sampling, is precisely the inference question – and it's where Chapter 2 picks up: sampling distributions, confidence intervals, and the beginnings of a systematic answer to "how do we know?"

The six steps, in one place

  1. Start with the variables that matter most.
  2. Qualitative variables – list their frequencies.
  3. Quantitative variables – look at the histogram.
  4. Check for extreme values, and decide deliberately what to do with each one.
  5. Look at the summary statistics – let them guide the questions you ask next.
  6. Explore further if needed – by group, over time, or otherwise.

Every one of those six steps happened in this chapter, in order, on data the class generated for itself.

Part Thirteen

From One Histogram to a Family of Shapes

The workshop looked at one histogram – the class's own 217 reaction-time trials, bunched up at the low end with a long tail stretching out to the right. That shape told a story on its own, but a single shape, on its own, is also just one dataset. To actually interpret it – to know whether it is ordinary or unusual, and what that implies for the statistical tests this module builds toward – the shape needs something to be compared against. That something is a family of theoretical distributions: not frequencies counted from one real dataset, but idealised, abstract descriptions of how likely different values are across a whole population, fully specified by a small handful of parameters.

Békés & Kézdi's own example makes the distinction concrete: the distribution of height across the whole of planet Earth is a theoretical object, and a different one from the distribution of height within the UK alone, or within France, or Spain. Which theoretical distribution a real variable most resembles shapes a great deal of what comes later in this module – which statistical tests make sense, which assumptions are safe to lean on, and which transformations (logging a variable, for instance) are worth trying before modelling it at all.

Part Fourteen

The Normal Distribution

Start with the shape you've probably already met, even if not by name: the normal distribution, bell-shaped and symmetric, one half a mirror of the other. That symmetry has a direct consequence for the three measures of centre from earlier in this chapter – mean, median, and mode all land in exactly the same place. The mean sits in the middle because half the distribution's mass lies to its left and half to its right (which makes it the median too), and because the bell peaks at its own centre, that same point is also the single most likely value – the mode.

Two parameters fully describe it: the mean, \(\mu\) (mu), fixing where the centre sits, and the standard deviation, \(\sigma\) (sigma), fixing how wide the bell is. Drag the mean below and the whole shape slides left or right without changing width; drag the standard deviation and the shape stretches or compresses around a fixed centre.

From the online session

“A lot of why the normal distribution is so common comes from the fact that it arises from adding up lots of small impacts, each of which might not be normally distributed on its own – but adding them together, the additive nature of it leads to an outcome that is normally distributed. The influences on height – genetics, diet, hundreds of other things – add up together, and the outcome, height, ends up distributed normally.”

Height, IQ, and measurement error are the three examples that recur most often, and all three share that additive structure: lots of small, mostly independent nudges, summed together. Keep that word – summed – in mind. It's exactly what the next distribution does differently.

Part Fifteen

The Log-Normal Distribution – and Why We Log Reaction Time

If the normal distribution comes from summing lots of small effects, the log-normal distribution comes from multiplying them instead – and the shape changes dramatically as a result. Where the normal is symmetric, the log-normal is pushed hard toward the low end, with a long right tail of larger, rarer values.

The two are related by a small, elegant piece of mathematics: the log of a product is the sum of the logs, \(\log(a \times b) = \log a + \log b\). So if a variable is itself the product of many small multiplicative factors, its log is a sum of many small factors – exactly the additive structure that produces a normal shape. Take a normally distributed variable \(x\) and transform it with \(e^x\) (the exponential function) and the result is, by construction, log-normal: always positive, with a long right tail.

A common mix-up, worth guarding against. The quickest way to misunderstand a log-normal distribution is to try to build one by logging a normal variable – the wrong direction entirely. Building a log-normal distribution means exponentiating a normal variable (\(e^x\)), not logging it. Reversing that afterwards – taking the natural log, LN(), of an already log-normal variable – is what pushes it back toward normal, and is exactly the transformation this chapter reaches for on reaction time below.

Income, firm size, and – this chapter's own running example – reaction time all tend toward this shape, for the same underlying reason: each is the outcome of several factors that compound rather than simply add.

From the online session

“Think about the factors feeding into reaction time. It isn't that one factor just adds on to another – attention, the noise in the room, distraction, your own motor speed – those effects compound. All those factors multiplied together produce a distribution something like this.”

The practical payoff arrives once a variable shaped like this needs to go into a statistical model: log it first, and a distribution dragging a long tail of extreme values behind it moves toward something closer to the well-behaved, symmetric shape most classical statistical tests are built to assume. It won't always get there completely – but moving in that direction is frequently worth the modest cost of having to interpret logged, rather than raw, units. Part Sixteen

Four More Distributions Worth Knowing

Power-law (Pareto) – when extremes stay plausible at any scale

The power-law, or Pareto, distribution is similar in silhouette to the log-normal – strongly right-skewed, most of the mass down at the low end – but its tail behaviour differs. In a log-normal distribution, extreme values get rarer at an accelerating rate the further out you look. In a power-law distribution, they don't: a very large value stays plausible at any scale, not just rare-but-possible near the body of the distribution.

From the online session

“Think about a distribution of wealth. Most people are down at the bottom – but you've got a very long tail, where you're bringing in your billionaires, much further out, and you find Musk and people like that. That's the Pareto distribution: similar in shape to log-normal, but those extreme values are even more common, and they're common at any scale.”

Our own reaction-time data, with its one truly extreme trial, has something of this shape too.

Uniform – when nothing is more typical than anything else

The uniform distribution is flat: every value between some minimum and maximum is exactly as likely as every other. It's fully described by just those two numbers.

From the online session

“If I go into a bag that's got numbers 0 to 10 in it – eleven balls – I want the probability I pick out a five to be exactly the same as the probability I pick out any other number. That's what the uniform distribution shows: no peak, nothing more typical than anything else.”

Figure

The uniform distribution

4,000 simulated draws between 0 and 10, bars roughly the same height across the whole range – the flat silhouette that gives this distribution its name.

Mean and median both sit at the midpoint of the range, but – unlike every other distribution in this chapter – there's no mode worth naming, because nothing stands out as more common. The clearest real-world use is a random number generator, where you specifically don't want any number favoured over another; it also turns up describing waiting time within a fixed interval, or rounding error.

Bernoulli – the building block everything else is made from

The Bernoulli distribution is about as simple as a distribution gets: one trial, two possible outcomes, described by a single parameter – the probability of success, \(p\). Think of an unfair coin, heads counted as success: with \(p = 0.3\), heads comes up on average 30% of the time, tails the other 70%. A single coin flip, one pass/fail test, one yes/no survey question – each of these is Bernoulli-distributed.

On its own, a Bernoulli trial is almost too simple to be interesting. It becomes interesting the moment you stop doing it once.

Binomial – counting successes across many trials

The binomial distribution is the number of successes across \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials – the same unfair coin as before, now flipped \(n\) times instead of once. It's described by two parameters: \(n\) (the number of trials) and \(p\) (the probability of success on each one, still 0.3 here).

With just \(n = 2\) trials, the full arithmetic is small enough to do by hand – and worth doing once, because it's the same logic that scales up to any \(n\):

SequenceProbabilitySuccesses
Fail, fail0.7 × 0.7 = 0.490
Success, fail0.3 × 0.7 = 0.211
Fail, success0.7 × 0.3 = 0.211
Success, success0.3 × 0.3 = 0.092

Two different orders – success-then-fail, and fail-then-success – both land exactly one success, so their probabilities get added, not counted once: \(0.21 + 0.21 = 0.42\).

Part Seventeen

The Poisson Distribution

The Poisson distribution answers a different kind of question: not successes out of a fixed number of trials, but the number of events in a fixed interval, given some constant average rate – customers arriving at a coffee shop per hour, emails per day, typos per page. It's described by a single parameter, \(\lambda\) (lambda), the average rate itself – and, distinctively, \(\lambda\) is both the mean and the variance of the distribution at once.

From the online session

“Say there's a coffee shop with an average rate of four people arriving per hour. Simulate that over 4,000 days and this is the distribution you get – most of the time it's about four, some days low, a high percentage of days around one. As we go into the extreme, the most ever seen out of 4,000 simulated days is some small number – nowhere close to what you'd see with a log-normal or a power-law.”

The Poisson distribution

Average rate (λ)4

Dragging \(\lambda\) up changes two things at once: the whole distribution shifts right (a higher average rate means more events are expected, so the peak moves to wherever \(\lambda\) now sits), and it spreads wider too (since the variance, also equal to \(\lambda\), grows right along with the mean). By around \(\lambda \approx 15\)–20, the shape is already hard to tell apart from a normal distribution – the same underlying pattern just seen with the binomial as \(n\) grew. Two unrelated-looking distributions converging on the same shape, for related reasons, is not a coincidence; it's a big part of why the normal distribution shows up everywhere once you start looking for it.

At small \(\lambda\), though, the Poisson has a distinctive signature: a genuinely thin tail. Values far from the average don't just get rare – they get rare fast. The live readout above shows the most extreme count actually seen in this run's own 4,000 simulated days; the true probability of seeing something like 100 arrivals, against an average of 4, works out to roughly 3 × 10−100 – a decimal point, 99 zeros, then a digit. That's smaller than one divided by the number of atoms in the observable universe. Pretty small.

This is the direct opposite of the power-law distribution from earlier in this chapter. There, big outliers stayed plausible at every scale. Here, they become all but impossible, fast, once you're a handful of standard deviations out. Not every right-skewed shape behaves the same way in its tail – worth checking which kind you actually have before trusting how far a value "could" plausibly go.
