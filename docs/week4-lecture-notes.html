Regression I: Fitting the Line

Part One

From Correlation to a Formula

Last chapter ended with a number: the correlation between finger length and reaction time in our class is about 0.18. Weak, positive, and on its own not much use to anyone. HR, though, hasn't given up. They now want to know whether that relationship can be turned into an actual formula – a screening tool. Measure a candidate's finger, plug the length into the formula, read off a predicted reaction time, and decide whether to shortlist them. No ten-trial test needed.

From the workshop

“Now the real fun begins – where you start applying all these statistics to a model. We're going to look at the simplest kind of model, but the workhorse for so much analytics: regression.”

The naive answer is to draw a line through the scatter by eye and read off the equation. It isn't that simple. Which line? Chosen by what rule? What does its slope actually mean? And a weak correlation doesn't become a good predictor just because you've drawn a line through it – how good the line is depends on how strong the relationship was in the first place. This chapter answers all of those questions. The tool is regression, and the particular way of fitting it is ordinary least squares (OLS).

Part Two

What Regression Is For: Two Goals

Regression can serve two quite different purposes, and it pays to keep them apart from the start.

  • Predictive analysisWhat should we expect y to be, for a given value of x? The focus is the predicted value. HR's screening tool is a prediction problem.
  • Causal analysisWhat is the effect of x on y – what would happen to y if we changed x? The focus is a parameter, a number describing that effect.
From the workshop

“You could think of predictive analysis as us trying to look at what might be, and causal analysis as what is – what happens. The model is written out exactly the same way. Regression as a tool doesn't know which goal you have. That's down to you.”

Either way, regression is a method of comparison: it compares the average of y across observations with different values of x. What changes between the two goals is the interpretation you're allowed to make, and that depends on how the data were produced. The goal comes first, before any analysis. Businesses often want prediction – a forecast of next quarter's sales – but decision-makers also want to know what will happen if they pull a particular lever, and that is a causal question. We come back to causality properly in Week 9.

Part Three

Regression Compares Conditional Means

None of this is a gear change from last chapter. Regression uncovers patterns of association between y, the outcome we care about, and x, by comparing the average value of y across different values of x – the conditional mean from Chapter 3:

$$E[y \mid x] = f(x) \qquad \text{shorthand: } y^E = f(x)$$

Here \(f\) is a function: a rule that turns each value of x into a value of y. It could be anything. The rule \(y = 10x\) says that y is always ten times x: when x is 0, y is 0; when x is 1, y is 10; when x is a billion, y is ten billion. Plotted, that's a straight line, because we've defined it to be one. But in the real world relationships rarely move in perfectly straight lines, and \(f\) could just as easily be a curve, or a squiggle.

That raises a choice. Do we let the data decide the shape, or do we impose one?

  • Non-parametric regressionNo shape is imposed; the data tell the story. Flexible, but data-hungry – you need many observations at each value of x. The bin-scatter from Chapter 3 is a simple example.
  • Parametric regressionWe impose a shape – for example, a straight line – described by a few numbers called parameters. More restrictive, but interpretable, and it works with far less data.
From the workshop

“Imagine your projector's broken, you haven't got your slides, and you have to present to your director. What's the relationship like? ‘It kind of goes up and then down and around a bit.’ Whereas if you can say, ‘on average, when x increases, y increases by a factor of ten’ – that's much easier to explain to people.”

A parametric model is always a reduction of a messy world. Deciding how much complexity to throw away, and justifying it, is a large part of the analyst's job.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 7

Part Four

Non-Parametric Regression: LOWESS

The standard non-parametric tool is LOWESS: locally weighted scatterplot smoothing. Like a bin-scatter it estimates the average of y at each x, but instead of chopping x into fixed bins it uses a moving, weighted local average. To estimate y at a finger length of 70mm, it averages the reaction times of students near 70mm – those at 69mm or 72mm count a lot, those at 90mm hardly at all. Doing that at every x gives a smooth curve rather than a handful of dots.

How “local” the average is gets set by the bandwidth. A wide bandwidth gives a smooth curve but can iron out real bends; a narrow one tracks the data closely but goes jagged. Try it on our own class:

One dot per student (mean reaction time). The red curve is a kernel-weighted local average; the bandwidth is the distance, in millimetres, over which neighbours carry real weight.

Narrow the bandwidth and the curve chases individual students; widen it and it flattens towards a gentle slope. LOWESS is excellent for spotting shape before you commit to a model. What it can't give you is a single interpretable number – you're back to describing a squiggle to the director.

LOWESS in Python 
from statsmodels.nonparametric.smoothers_lowess import lowess

smoothed = lowess(endog=df['reaction_time_ms'], exog=df['finger_mm'], frac=0.3)
# endog is y, exog is x. smoothed is an array of (x, y-hat) pairs, sorted by x

In statsmodels the bandwidth is set by frac: the share of all observations used in each local average, rather than a distance in millimetres. A larger frac gives a smoother curve.

Part Five

Linear Regression: y = α + βx + ε

Now we impose some structure. Linear regression says that every individual observation's y is made of three parts:

$$y = \alpha + \beta x + \varepsilon$$
  • \(\alpha\) (alpha)A constant: the intercept.
  • \(\beta\) (beta)The slope: how much y differs, on average, per unit of x.
  • \(\varepsilon\) (epsilon)The error term: everything else affecting y that the model doesn't capture – other factors we know about but have no data on, factors we don't know about, ability, luck, plain noise. We never observe it directly.

The key assumption is that the error is unrelated to x – on average it is zero whatever the value of x, so knowing x tells us nothing about which way \(\varepsilon\) leans:

$$E[\varepsilon \mid x] = 0$$

Why the error averages away

Take the expectation of both sides, conditional on x. The average of a sum is the sum of the averages, so we can work term by term:

$$E[y \mid x] = E[\alpha \mid x] + E[\beta x \mid x] + E[\varepsilon \mid x] = \alpha + \beta x + 0$$
  • \(E[\alpha \mid x] = \alpha\)\(\alpha\) is a fixed number, the same for every observation, and the average of something that never changes is just itself.
  • \(E[\beta x \mid x] = \beta x\)Conditioning on x means looking only at observations that share one value of x. Within that group \(\beta x\) is the same number for everyone, so its average is \(\beta x\).
  • \(E[\varepsilon \mid x] = 0\)The key assumption above.
From the workshop

“If I gave everybody in this room £10, and there are 20 people in the room, on average I've given you £10. Take the average of something that's the same for every observation and it's just its own value.”

So the model collapses to \(y^E = \alpha + \beta x\). The error hasn't vanished from reality – individual students will still sit above or below the line – but on average it cancels out. What's left is the part of y that x explains.

Part Six

Reading α and β

Linear regression imposes linearity in the coefficients, the simplest parametric form there is. That's an assumption, or an approximation: even when the true pattern curves, a straight line approximates its average slope.

The intercept, \(\alpha\), is the average value of y when x is zero. Put \(x = 0\) into the model and \(\beta\) drops out:

$$E[y \mid x = 0] = \alpha + \beta \times 0 = \alpha$$

The slope, \(\beta\), is the expected difference in y between observations one unit apart in x. Compare \(x_0 + 1\) with \(x_0\): the two \(\alpha\)s cancel, the two \(\beta x_0\)s cancel, and only \(\beta\) is left.

$$E[y \mid x_0 + 1] - E[y \mid x_0] = \big(\alpha + \beta(x_0 + 1)\big) - \big(\alpha + \beta x_0\big) = \beta$$

In the \(y = 10x\) example, \(\beta = 10\) and \(\alpha = 0\). In words: on average, y is ten times x.

Precise language matters

The safe way to describe a slope is: “y is higher, on average, by \(\beta\), for observations with a one-unit higher x.” Two words to avoid:

  • “Increases”Implies something changing over time. A regression compares different observations; it doesn't watch one observation change.
  • “Effect”Implies causation, which a regression on observational data can't justify on its own.
From the workshop

“Avoid ‘effect’. It's something I still have to go through and take out of my own work, after ten years of doing this. I think the human brain wants to imply causality.”

Estimates wear hats

The true \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) describe the whole population, and we never get to see them. What we compute from a sample is an estimate, marked with a hat: \(\hat\alpha\) and \(\hat\beta\). The rule we use to get from data to an estimate is an estimator – this chapter's estimator is OLS.

From the workshop

“The true values – you're never going to find out what they are. We already have the most complex model in the world. It's called the world. You're simplifying the world to understand it. Someone decided at some point that estimates wear a hat.”

Part Seven

Your First Regression

Time to fit the model to our own class. The data are the Week 1 reaction times, with the finger-length typos corrected in Chapter 3, collapsed to one row per student: each student's mean reaction time and their finger length (Part Fourteen explains why one row per student, not one per trial).

import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

data_path = "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/stevenolanecon/7002LBSAI/main/data/week3_reaction_times.csv"
df = pd.read_csv(data_path)
df['finger_mm'] = df['finger_length_corrected']

# one row per student: average the trials, keep the (fixed) finger length and device
student_summary = (
    df.groupby('name')
      .agg(mean_rt=('reaction_time_ms', 'mean'),
           finger_mm=('finger_mm', 'first'),
           device=('device', 'first'))
      .reset_index()
)

model = smf.ols('mean_rt ~ finger_mm', data=student_summary).fit()
model.params
Where the syntax usually trips people up.
  • Point at the right data frame. The raw data are in df, one row per trial. The regression needs student_summary, the collapsed version. data=df will either fail (no mean_rt column) or fit the wrong data.
  • The formula is a string: 'y ~ x', with the outcome on the left of the tilde and the explanatory variable on the right, both inside one pair of quotes.
  • Always end with .fit(). smf.ols(...) only sets the model up; .fit() does the estimating.
  • .params pulls out just the two estimates. model.params.round(1) tidies them to one decimal place.
  • 'first' in the aggregation means “take the value on the student's first row”. Finger length and device are the same on all of a student's rows, so the first one will do.

The result:

$$\widehat{\text{mean reaction time}} = -404.9 + 18.6 \times \text{finger length (mm)}$$

\(\hat\beta = 18.6\): students whose index finger is one millimetre longer have, on average, a mean reaction time about 18.6ms slower. Positive, matching the positive correlation from Chapter 3.

\(\hat\alpha = -404.9\): by definition, the predicted average reaction time for someone whose finger is zero millimetres long. A negative reaction time is impossible.

From the workshop

“People with no index finger are so fast they're going back in time to do the test. Obviously that's nonsense – but we didn't have anyone in our data with no index finger.”

The intercept is where the fitted line crosses the y-axis, and our data start at 68mm, a long way from zero. So \(\hat\alpha\) here is an anchor that positions the line, not a description of anyone we observed. Part Sixteen comes back to why predicting that far from the data is a bad idea.

Part Nine

Regression on a Binary x

x doesn't have to be continuous. Device is a dummy variable: Laptop = 0, Phone = 1. With only two possible values of x, plug each into \(y^E = \alpha + \beta x\):

  • \(\alpha\)The average y when x = 0: the average reaction time on a laptop.
  • \(\alpha + \beta\)The average y when x = 1: the average reaction time on a phone.
  • \(\beta\)The difference between the two: phone minus laptop.

The “line” is only ever evaluated at 0 and 1, so it's really just two group averages and their difference, written in regression notation. It's exactly the conditional means from Chapter 3, now produced by one model. Part Eighteen fits it to our data.

Part Ten

The OLS Estimator

So far Python has produced \(\hat\alpha\) and \(\hat\beta\) for us. Here's what it computes. The slope is built from two things you already know:

$$\hat\beta = \frac{Cov[x,y]}{Var[x]} \qquad\qquad \hat\alpha = \bar y - \hat\beta \bar x$$

The slope is the covariance between x and y – how they move together – divided by the variance of x, how much x moves on its own. That's why it reads as “the difference in average y per unit difference in x”.

The intercept follows from the slope. Averaging the fitted line over the whole sample gives \(\bar y = \hat\alpha + \hat\beta \bar x\); subtract \(\hat\beta \bar x\) from both sides and you have the formula. A direct consequence is that the fitted line always passes through the point of means, \((\bar x, \bar y)\). Once \(\hat\beta\) is fixed, \(\hat\alpha\) isn't a second, separate choice – it's whatever pins the line through that point. The software estimates \(\hat\beta\) first, then \(\hat\alpha\) from it.

Worked example: a whole regression by hand

Back to Chapter 3's five students, hours spent revising (x) against a quiz score out of 12 (y). We already know \(\bar x = 6\), \(\bar y = 7\), \(Cov[x,y] = 8\) and \(Var[x] = 40/4 = 10\).

Part Eleven

Why Divide by Var[x]?

Chapter 3 drew covariance as a set of rectangles from the point of means to each observation, positive when x and y are on the same side of their averages and negative otherwise; the covariance is their net area. Variance is the same idea applied to x on its own – pair x with itself and every rectangle becomes a square, \((x_i - \bar x)^2\). A number times itself can't be negative, so Var[x] is always positive. That means the sign of \(\hat\beta\) comes entirely from the covariance: whether y tends to be higher or lower when x is higher.

So what is the division for? Units. Covariance is measured in x-units times y-units – millimetre-milliseconds for our data – which isn't a slope. Dividing by Var[x], in millimetres squared, leaves milliseconds per millimetre: a rate.

Check it by measuring finger length in centimetres instead (multiply every x by 0.1). A slope in ms per cm should be exactly ten times the slope in ms per mm.

Covariance, variance and slope for finger length in millimetres and centimetres.
Cov[x,y]Var[x]β̂
Millimetres1,359.673.0918.60 ms per mm
Centimetres135.96 (×0.1)0.7309 (×0.01)186.0 ms per cm (×10)

Covariance alone moves the wrong way (smaller, when the slope per centimetre should be bigger). Covariance divided by variance moves by exactly the right amount. That's the whole point of the division: it turns a measure of co-movement into a rate per unit of x.

Part Twelve

Fitted Values and Residuals

Once the line is estimated, we can put each observation's own x back into it. That gives the fitted value, \(\hat y_i\) – what the model predicts for observation \(i\) using only its x. The gap between what we actually observed and what the model predicted is the residual, \(e_i\):

$$\hat y_i = \hat\alpha + \hat\beta x_i \qquad\qquad e_i = y_i - \hat y_i$$

The little \(i\) just labels the observation: row 1, row 2, row 3 of the data. A positive residual means the point sits above the line (y was higher than predicted); a negative one, below it. On a chart, the residual is the vertical distance between a point and the line.

Residual or error? They're easy to mix up. The error \(\varepsilon_i\) is the gap between y and the true line \(\alpha + \beta x\), which we never see. The residual \(e_i\) is the gap between y and our estimated line \(\hat\alpha + \hat\beta x\), which we can compute for every observation. The residual is our best stand-in for the error.

Worked example: the five students

Use \(\widehat{\text{score}} = 2.2 + 0.8 \times \text{hours}\) on each student in turn. Student 1 revised for 2 hours, so \(\hat y_1 = 2.2 + 0.8 \times 2 = 3.8\). They actually scored 3, so \(e_1 = 3 - 3.8 = -0.8\): slightly below the line.

Part Thirteen

Why This Particular Line?

Any candidate line, with any \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), leaves a residual for every observation. OLS picks the line that makes the residuals as small as possible overall. Specifically, it minimises the sum of squared residuals:

$$\min_{\alpha,\beta} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \alpha - \beta x_i)^2$$

Why square them? Some residuals are positive and some negative, so adding them up raw would let them cancel. Squaring makes every one count as a positive distance, and it penalises big misses more heavily than small ones. Solving this minimisation with calculus gives exactly the Cov/Var formula from Part Ten. You don't need the calculus; you do need the idea, which you can watch happen:

It is smallest exactly at \(\hat\beta = 18.60\), the dashed line – the Cov/Var answer. Notice the scale, though: even a flat line (slope 0) does only a few per cent worse. That's a weak relationship showing itself.

Part Fourteen

OLS Is BLUE – If…

OLS is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator: among every linear, unbiased way of estimating \(\beta\), it has the smallest variance. “Best” means most precise, not most correct. That guarantee is the Gauss-Markov theorem, and it holds only if a handful of assumptions are true:

  • LinearityA straight line is a reasonable approximation of the relationship.
  • Independent observationsOne observation's error doesn't leak into another's.
  • HomoskedasticityThe spread of the residuals is roughly constant across the range of x – not fanning out or narrowing.
  • No extreme influenceNo single point is silently dragging the whole line.
  • No perfect multicollinearityNo explanatory variable is an exact linear combination of the others. This only bites once a model has more than one x; Week 6 covers it.

Independence, concretely. This is why our regression uses one row per student (24 rows), not one row per trial (199 rows). A student's ten trials aren't independent of each other: they all share whatever made that student fast or slow that day. Treating every trial as a separate observation would pretend each attempt came from a different person, overstating how much genuinely independent information we have.

Homoskedasticity, concretely. If slower reactors are also more erratic, the residuals would fan out at higher predicted values. Here's what the two cases look like, using a generic simulated example (our 24 points are too few to show the pattern convincingly):

Figure

Homoskedastic vs. heteroskedastic (simulated data)

Top: the data and the fitted line. Bottom: each point's residual. On the left the residuals form an even band around zero; on the right the band fans out as x grows.

None of these violations makes OLS impossible to compute, except perfect multicollinearity. What they change is how far you should trust the line and the precision claimed for it – which matters a great deal once we start building confidence intervals next week. A sixth assumption doesn't make this list at all: that the residuals are normally distributed. Gauss-Markov never needs it, so it can't bias \(\hat\beta\), but it does matter for the t-tests and confidence intervals coming in Week 5.

Most of the time, with a reasonable sample size and sensible data handling, these assumptions can be met, and where they can't there are standard fixes. But you have to know what they are to know whether your model is the right one.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 7

Part Fifteen

Two Things That Are Always True

The five-student table showed two facts. They hold for every OLS regression with an intercept, by construction:

  • The residuals sum to zeroThe line balances through the data: the misses above it exactly offset the misses below it.
  • Average \(\hat y\) = average \(y\)A direct consequence: if the residuals average zero, the fitted values must average the same as the observed values.

Both follow from the line passing through \((\bar x, \bar y)\). Check it on our class in Python, using the model you fitted in Part Seven:

model.resid.sum()                                        # about 0 (e.g. -1e-11, rounding)
model.fittedvalues.mean(), student_summary['mean_rt'].mean()   # 1070.1 and 1070.1

The sum comes out as something like \(-1 \times 10^{-11}\): zero, apart from the computer's rounding. If your residuals don't sum to (almost) zero, the model has been fitted without an intercept or on different data.

Part Sixteen

Interpolation vs. Extrapolation

Fitted values aren't only for the students we have. Put any finger length into the line and you get a prediction – which is exactly what HR wants. In Python, give model.predict a small data frame of new x values, using the same column name as in the model:

new = pd.DataFrame({'finger_mm': [80, 40, 20]})
model.predict(new)
# 80mm: 1,083ms   40mm: 339ms   20mm: -33ms

Our data run from 68mm to 100mm. At 80mm we're inside that range: interpolation, reasonably safe, because the line was fitted on data like that. At 40mm and 20mm we're well outside it: extrapolation, which assumes the straight-line pattern carries on somewhere we never checked. At 20mm the line predicts a negative reaction time; it crosses zero at about 21.8mm. Drag the slider to see where the line stops being trustworthy:

Part Seventeen

How Good Is the Fit? R²

If you're going to put your name to a prediction, you want to know how well the model fits. The standard measure is R²: the share of the variation in y that the fitted values account for.

$$R^2 = \frac{Var[\hat y]}{Var[y]} = 1 - \frac{Var[e]}{Var[y]} \qquad\qquad Var[y] = Var[\hat y] + Var[e]$$
  • R² = 0The fitted values are just \(\bar y\) for everyone; the regression explains nothing.
  • R² = 1Every point sits exactly on the line.
  • Simple regressionWith one x, \(R^2 = (Corr[x,y])^2\) – a direct link back to Chapter 3.

Worked example: the five students

The fitted values 3.8, 5.4, 7.0, 8.6, 10.2 deviate from their mean of 7 by \(-3.2, -1.6, 0, 1.6, 3.2\). Square and add: \(10.24 + 2.56 + 0 + 2.56 + 10.24 = 25.6\). The real scores deviate from 7 by \(-4, 0, -2, 4, 2\), and their squares add to 40. Dividing each by \(n - 1 = 4\):

$$R^2 = \frac{Var[\hat y]}{Var[y]} = \frac{25.6/4}{40/4} = \frac{6.4}{10} = 0.64 = 0.8^2$$

Revising hours account for 64% of the variation in quiz scores, and 0.64 is the square of the correlation of 0.8 from Chapter 3.

The same thing in sums of squares

You'll often see R² written with sums of squares instead of variances – the same identity without the division by \(n - 1\). Excel's regression table, for example, reports them:

$$\underbrace{\textstyle\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2}_{TSS} = \underbrace{\textstyle\sum(\hat y_i-\bar y)^2}_{ESS} + \underbrace{\textstyle\sum e_i^2}_{RSS} \qquad R^2 = \frac{ESS}{TSS} = 1 - \frac{RSS}{TSS}$$

TSS is the total sum of squares, ESS the explained part, RSS the residual part. For the five students: TSS = 40, ESS = 25.6, and the squared residuals \(0.64 + 2.56 + 4 + 5.76 + 1.44 = 14.4\) give RSS. Check: \(25.6 + 14.4 = 40\), and \(R^2 = 25.6/40 = 0.64\).

A labelling trap. Some textbooks swap the names and call the residual part SSE. statsmodels doesn't print the sums of squares in its summary, but a fitted model stores them as model.centered_tss (TSS), model.ess (ESS) and model.ssr (RSS). Note that statsmodels' ssr is the residual sum of squares, while some textbooks use SSR for the explained part. Check what a source means before trusting the letters.

Our class: R² from the fitted values

Compute it yourself rather than reading it off: pull out the fitted values, take the two variances, and divide.

fitted = model.fittedvalues
r2 = fitted.var() / student_summary['mean_rt'].var()
r2                     # 25,291 / 789,561 = 0.032
model.rsquared         # the same number, directly

Finger length accounts for about 3.2% of the variation in mean reaction time. That's the answer to HR. The formula exists; it just isn't useful. (Check: the correlation was 0.179, and \(0.179^2 = 0.032\).)

Part Eighteen

A Second Regression: Device

Part Nine showed that with a 0/1 x, \(\hat\alpha\) is the laptop average and \(\hat\beta\) is phone minus laptop. Try it on our class, still with one row per student:

student_summary.groupby('device')['mean_rt'].mean()
# Laptop 1136.9   (16 students)
# Phone   936.5   (8 students)

So \(\hat\alpha = 1{,}136.9\)ms and \(\hat\beta = 936.5 - 1{,}136.9 = -200.4\)ms: phone users averaged about 200ms quicker than laptop users in our class. You can get the same numbers from the Cov/Var formula, coding device as 0/1 yourself:

import numpy as np

x = (student_summary['device'] == 'Phone').astype(int)
y = student_summary['mean_rt']
beta = np.cov(x, y)[0, 1] / x.var(ddof=1)     # -200.4
alpha = y.mean() - beta * x.mean()             # 1136.9

And from statsmodels in one line. It converts the text column to 0/1 itself, labelling the coefficient device[T.Phone] to show which category is x = 1:

model2 = smf.ols('mean_rt ~ device', data=student_summary).fit()
model2.params      # Intercept 1136.89, device[T.Phone] -200.41

Part Nineteen


Is It Causal?



Suppose the finger-length slope had been large and the fit tight. What could we conclude? A positive slope is consistent with at least three explanations, and they aren't mutually exclusive:



    
  
  • x causes yLonger fingers lead to slower reactions.
    • 
  
  • y causes xReaction speed changes finger length. Implausible here, but always worth ruling out explicitly.
    • 
  
  • A third variable causes bothHand size, age or general dexterity could move finger length and reaction time together.
    • 




“Correlation doesn't imply causation” undersells the point. Better: we should not infer cause and effect from a comparison in observational data. The method isn't broken; nothing about how this data was collected was designed to rule out the third explanation. Week 9 looks at data that is designed that way.
