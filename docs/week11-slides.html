Time series regression

Every dataset so far has been a snapshot — many units, one point in time. What changes when it’s one unit, followed through time?

How risky is Microsoft stock?

Business question: you’re considering investing in Microsoft stock. How much does it move when the overall market moves — and how would you even estimate that?

We have real daily data on Microsoft’s share price and the S&P 500 index, from December 1997 to December 2018.

The naive answer

Naive answer: regress the Microsoft share price directly on the S&P 500 index level. Read off the slope.

Why it’s not that simple: both series have climbed for two decades. Two things that both trend upward will look related whether or not they actually are — and that’s before we’ve even asked whether “same day” is even the right comparison.

What we need

  • An understanding of what makes time series data different from the cross-sections we’ve used all module
  • A way to tell a genuine relationship apart from two variables that just happen to drift the same direction
  • The tools to fix it when regression assumptions specific to time series data break down

What’s special about time series data

New notation, new opportunities

  • Time series data follows one unit across many time periods: \(y_t,\ t = 1, 2, \dots, T\)
  • Ordering matters — this is the key difference from every cross-section this module has used
  • New opportunities: we can ask how \(y\) responds to a change in \(x\), immediately or with a delay
  • New complications: frequency, gaps, and — the big one — values that aren’t independent of their own past

Trend

\[\Delta y_t = y_t - y_{t-1}\]

  • Positive trend: \(E[\Delta y_t] > 0\) on average. Negative trend is the mirror image.
  • Linear trend: the change itself is stable on average
  • Exponential trend: the relative change is stable on average — common for prices, income, anything that compounds

Seasonality

  • The expected value changes periodically — by day of week, month, or hour
  • Can be linear (constant seasonal gaps) or exponential (constant relative gaps)
  • Both trend and seasonality make time series regression genuinely harder than cross-sectional regression

Stationarity

A stationary time series has the same expected value and distribution at every point in time. Stability, full stop.

Non-stationary series break this in one of three ways:

  • Has a trend
  • Has seasonality
  • Has an unstable, ever-widening spread — a random walk

Random walk

\[y_t = y_{t-1} + e_t\]

  • Today’s value is yesterday’s value, plus a completely random, unpredictable shock
  • Whatever happened last period doesn’t influence the size of today’s shock — but \(y_t\) still carries forward everything that came before
  • The spread of possible values grows the further out you go — genuinely impossible to predict beyond a drift

Spurious regression, and fixing it

When correlation is just two trends

Per-capita cheese consumption correlates at 0.947 with the number of people who died getting tangled in their bedsheets (Tyler Vigen, Spurious Correlations). Neither causes the other — both simply drifted upward over the same years.

Trend and seasonality act like confounders — omitted variables that create an association where none exists. The regression can’t tell the difference between “these move together because they’re related” and “these move together because it’s 2015 for both of them.”

The fix: differencing

  • Replace variables in the regression with their first differences — this removes the trend and is often enough to make a series stationary
  • Same idea in relative terms: percent changes, or their close approximation, log differences

\[\Delta y_t^E = \alpha + \beta \Delta x_t\]

\(\alpha\): how much \(y\) changes on average when \(x\) doesn’t change. \(\beta\): how much more \(y\) changes when \(x\) changes by one more unit — additional to \(\alpha\), not instead of it.

The fix: seasonal dummies

  • Differencing handles trend. Seasonality needs its own fix — include a dummy for each season (month, day of week, whatever the cycle is)

\[y_t^E = \alpha + \beta x_t + \delta_{Jan} + \delta_{Feb} + \dots + \delta_{Nov}\]

  • One category held out as reference, exactly like any other categorical variable since Week 6

Serial correlation

Correlated with its own past

\[\rho_1 = Corr[y_t, y_{t-1}]\]

  • Serial correlation: a variable correlated with its own lagged values
  • \(\rho_1 = 0\) means no serial correlation — “white noise,” order doesn’t matter, just like cross-sectional data
  • Positive serial correlation: if \(y_t\) is above average, \(y_{t+1}\) is more likely to be above average too

Why this breaks the standard errors

  • Serial correlation doesn’t bias the coefficient — but it makes the usual standard errors wrong, often badly so
  • Even heteroskedasticity-robust SEs (the HC1 we’ve used since Week 5) don’t fix this — a different problem needs a different tool

Two fixes:

  1. Newey-West standard errors — built to handle serial correlation directly (and heteroskedasticity too), needs a choice of how many lags to account for. In statsmodels, this is cov_type="HAC" (Heteroskedasticity- and Autocorrelation-Consistent) with cov_kwds={"maxlags": ...} — “HAC” is the library’s name for the Newey-West family, not a different thing.
  2. Include a lagged dependent variable in the regression itself — usually one lag is enough

Case study: electricity and temperature

  • Monthly residential electricity consumption in Arizona, and temperature — measured as cooling degree days and heating degree days — 2001–2018

Lags of x

When effects take time to arrive

  • A lagged association: \(x\) affects \(y\) not immediately, but one or more periods later
  • Useful whenever there’s a real-world delay — people don’t necessarily change behaviour the moment conditions change

\[\Delta y_t^E = \alpha + \beta_0 \Delta x_t + \beta_1 \Delta x_{t-1} + \beta_2 \Delta x_{t-2}\]

\(\beta_0\): same-period effect. \(\beta_1\): how much more \(y\) changes next period after \(x\) moved, holding \(x\) fixed elsewhere. The cumulative effect is \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 + \beta_2\).

Back to the question

So, how risky is Microsoft?

The full workflow, start to finish — levels to returns to beta:

import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

df = pd.read_csv(data_path)
df["pct_msft"] = df["p_MSFT"].pct_change() * 100
df["pct_sp500"] = df["p_SP500"].pct_change() * 100

model = smf.ols("pct_msft ~ pct_sp500", data=df).fit(cov_type="HC1")
print(model.params)

Intercept      0.54
pct_sp500     1.26

  • Once returns replace levels, the spurious-trend problem disappears — the real relationship is a beta of : Microsoft moves noticeably more than the market, in the same direction
  • The Arizona case study shows the same discipline pays off differently — differencing and Newey-West SEs don’t change the story much here, but checking mattered

Summary

  • Time series data brings genuine opportunities — same-unit comparisons over time — and genuine hazards: trend, seasonality, and random walk all threaten spurious results
  • First differences (or percent/log changes) are the standard fix for trend; seasonal dummies handle seasonality
  • Serial correlation doesn’t bias coefficients, but breaks standard errors — Newey-West SEs or a lagged DV both fix it
  • Effects can arrive with a delay — lags let a regression capture that directly
