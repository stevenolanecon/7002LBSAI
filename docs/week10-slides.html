Prediction

Every model so far has answered “what’s the relationship?” This week asks a different question: “what’s my best guess for this new observation?”

Selling your car

Business question: You want to sell your Toyota Camry through an online ad. What price do you put in the listing?

You have data on hundreds of other Camrys already advertised — their age, mileage, condition, and asking price. Somewhere in that data is the answer.

The naive answer

Naive answer: run a regression of price on the car’s features, read off the coefficients, plug in your car’s numbers.

Why it’s not that simple: which features? How many? A model with more variables always fits the data in front of you better — but does it give a better price for your car, or just a better description of the cars you already have data on?

Explanation and prediction are different goals

Every regression so far has asked: what is the relationship, and can we trust the coefficients?

This week asks something else: given a new car we haven’t seen, what’s our best guess for its price?

  • We care much less about individual coefficients, or multicollinearity between them
  • We care much more about whether \(\hat y\) is close to the true \(y\) for new observations
  • The best model for explaining is not always the best model for predicting

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 13

What we need

  • A way to measure how wrong a prediction is, and how much that should matter
  • A way to tell whether a model that fits well is actually a good model, or just an overfit one
  • A rule for choosing between models that doesn’t just reward complexity

Setting up the problem

Original data and live data

  • Original data — what we have: cars with known prices, used to build the model
  • Live data — the observations the model will actually be applied to once it’s built: your car, or the next ad posted tomorrow. We know their \(x\)’s, but not yet their \(y\) — that’s the whole point of predicting them. The model is only useful if the relationship between \(x\) and \(y\) in the live data matches what it learned from the original data.
  • Target variable \(y\) — what we want to predict (price)
  • Predictor variables \(x\) — what we use to predict it (age, mileage, condition…)

\[\hat y_j = \hat f(x_j)\]

We need a predicted value for target observation \(j\) — we know its \(x\)’s, we don’t know its \(y\).

The prediction error

The prediction error is the gap between what the model says and what actually happens — the single number that tells you how wrong a specific prediction was, once you find out:

\[e_j = \hat y_j - y_j\]

  • Direction of miss: positive if we overpredict, negative if we underpredict — whether that matters depends on the decision
  • Size of miss: larger in absolute value the further the prediction is from the truth
  • The prediction error is unknown until the actual value is realised — that’s the whole problem

Decomposing the prediction error

Three sources, stacked together:

  1. Estimation error — we don’t know the true \(\beta\)’s, only estimates. Shrinks with a larger sample.
  2. Model error — we may not have the best model: wrong functional form, missing an interaction, wrong variables entirely.
  3. Irreducible (genuine) error — the part no model could ever capture, even with the truth known exactly.

Estimation and model error are the two parts an analyst can actually improve.

Point and interval prediction

  • A point prediction is a single number — the price to put in the ad
  • An interval prediction captures the uncertainty around it
  • The 95% prediction interval covers estimation error and irreducible error — but not model error. It assumes the model itself is right.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 13

The loss function

Turning errors into a decision

A loss function attaches a value to a prediction error — how bad is being wrong by this much, in this direction?

Two questions decide its shape:

  • Symmetric? Are errors in opposite directions equally bad?
  • Convex? Does an error twice as large cost more than twice as much?

Mean squared error and RMSE

For \(K\) target observations, \(\hat y_k\) is the model’s prediction for observation \(k\) and \(y_k\) its actual value:

\[MSE = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\hat y_k - y_k)^2 \qquad RMSE = \sqrt{MSE}\]

  • Squared loss is symmetric and convex — the most widely used loss function
  • \(MSE\) minimisation is exactly what OLS already does — that’s why the average is the best predictor under squared loss
  • \(RMSE\) is easier to interpret: it’s back in the original units (dollars, not dollars²)

MSE decomposes into bias and variance

\(\overline{\hat y} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_k \hat y_k\) — the average prediction across all \(K\) target observations, not any one \(\hat y_k\) on its own. That lets us write the bias compactly: \(\bar e = \overline{\hat y} - \bar y\), the average of the individual prediction errors \(e_k = \hat y_k - y_k\).

\[MSE = \underbrace{\bar e^{\,2}}_{\text{Bias}^2} \; + \; \underbrace{\frac{1}{K}\sum_k (e_k - \bar e)^2}_{\text{Prediction variance}}\]

  • Bias (\(\bar e\)): the average prediction error across all \(K\) observations. Zero for an unbiased predictor.
  • Variance: how much each error \(e_k\) varies around that average — not how much \(y\) itself varies.
  • OLS is unbiased. Some other methods (LASSO, later this week’s online session) trade a little bias for a lot less variance.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 13

Overfitting

Underfit, overfit

Comparing two models on the same data:

  • Underfitting: Model A fits worse than Model B, in both the original data and the live data. Simple — build a better model.
  • Overfitting: Model A actually fits better in the original data, but worse in the live data. This is the dangerous one — it looks like the better model until it’s too late to tell.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 13

Why overfitting happens

Typically: a model too complex for the data — too many variables, too many interactions, too flexible a functional form (high-degree polynomials, splines with too many knots).

It fits patterns that are:

  • specific to this sample, not the general pattern behind it (an internal validity problem), or
  • specific to the world of the original data, not the world the live data comes from (an external validity problem)

How would we actually know?

In-sample RMSE can’t catch overfitting — it’s measured on the same data the model was fit to, so it rewards complexity by construction.

We don’t have live data yet, but we can pretend: hold back some of the original data, fit the model without it, then see how well the model predicts the part it never saw.

Do that a few times and average the results — that’s what the chart’s cross-validated RMSE line means. The full mechanics come shortly.

Finding the best model

Two ways to penalise complexity

Approach 1 — indirectly. Fit on all the data, use a fit measure with a built-in penalty for the number of parameters.

Approach 2 — directly. Actually hold data back, and see how the model does on data it never saw.

The BIC (and AIC)

Both take the same starting point — the model’s log-likelihood — and add a penalty for the number of parameters, \(k\):

\[AIC = -2 \times \ln(likelihood) + 2 \times k \qquad\qquad BIC = -2 \times \ln(likelihood) + \ln(N) \times k\]

  • Same idea, different penalty: BIC’s penalty (\(\ln N\) per parameter) is heavier than AIC’s (a flat 2 per parameter) once \(N > 7\)
  • No sample splitting needed — useful when data is limited
  • The likelihood requires a distributional assumption — for OLS, that the residuals are normally distributed. Neither AIC nor BIC is free: this is the price for not having to hold data back.
  • Don’t use plain \(R^2\) or MSE for model choice — they never penalise complexity, so they always favour the most complex model
  • In practice, this course uses BIC, not AIC — Gabor & Kézdi’s own recommendation

Training and test samples

  • Approach 2, directly: split the data — say 80% training, 20% test
  • Estimate every candidate model on the training sample only
  • Evaluate each one’s RMSE on the test sample — data it never touched while fitting
  • No distributional assumptions needed, unlike BIC

k-fold cross-validation

Rather than one split, do it \(k\) times:

  1. Divide the data into \(k\) roughly equal folds
  2. For each fold: train on the other \(k-1\) folds, test on this one, record the RMSE
  3. Average the \(k\) RMSEs — this is the cross-validated RMSE

Every observation gets used for both training and testing, just never both at once. Less sensitive to which particular split you happened to draw.

get_prediction() gives both the point forecast (mean) and — via summary_frame(alpha=0.20) — the bounds of an 80% prediction interval, all from the model already fitted.

The price

An 80% prediction interval for this specific car: 80% of cars just like it should have their true price land somewhere in this range. It’s wider than a confidence interval for the average price at these characteristics — it has to cover this one car’s entire prediction error, irreducible noise included, not just the uncertainty in a mean.

Stationarity — the assumption underneath everything

  • All of this assumes the relationship between \(x\) and \(y\) in the live data is the same as in the original data
  • If the used-car market shifts — a fuel price shock, a new model year, a different city — the fitted relationship may simply not transfer
  • No amount of BIC or cross-validation on the original data can detect this. It has to be reasoned about, not computed.

Summary

  • Prediction is a different goal from explanation — coefficients matter less, \(\hat y\) for new observations matters more
  • A loss function turns a prediction error into a number a business decision can use; squared loss (MSE/RMSE) is the default, but not the only option
  • More complex models always fit the original data better — that’s exactly why in-sample fit alone is a trap
  • BIC and cross-validation both penalise complexity; used properly, they agree
