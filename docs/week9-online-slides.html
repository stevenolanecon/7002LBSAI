When You Can’t Randomise

7002LBSAI Research Methods in Analytics — Week 9 Online Session

When you can’t randomise

The workshop showed the gold standard. Most of the time, you won’t have it — the portfolio project almost certainly won’t. This session: the reasoning tools for observational data, and their honest limits.

Recap: why experiments are the gold standard

  • Random assignment makes variation in the causal variable exogenous — independent of potential outcomes — which is exactly what makes the simple group comparison trustworthy
  • Most business questions can’t be experimented on: it’s too slow, too expensive, unethical, or the decision has already been made and you only have the data left over
  • This is the normal situation, not the exception. The rest of this module’s regression toolkit exists because of it

The honest fallback

Without random assignment, we can’t create exogenous variation — we can only try to condition it into existence, by controlling for everything endogenous we can measure.

Week 6 called this the difference between omitted variable bias and multicollinearity. This session gives it a proper diagram.

Naming the confounders

Three ways a variable can confound

  • Common-cause confounder: one variable drives both \(x\) and \(y\) — the classic case, and the one Week 6’s OVB formula was built for
  • Reverse-causality confounder: what looks like \(x \to y\) is partly \(y \to x\) — the direction of the arrow itself is in question
  • Unwanted-mechanism confounder: \(x\) affects \(y\) through a route that has nothing to do with the causal story you actually care about

Is this causal?

  • People who eat more fruit and veg aren’t a random sample of the population — they may also earn more, exercise more, smoke less
  • Gabor’s own version of this question: household income is a plausible common-cause confounder — richer households can afford better diets, and income affects blood pressure through completely different channels (stress, healthcare access, housing)
html`<p>In this data, fruit-and-veg intake correlates with log household income at <strong>r = ${inc_corr}</strong> — modest, but real. Exactly the kind of endogenous source of variation Section C warns about.</p>`

Task — should we condition on crisps?

Business question: should we control for how many crisps someone eats, when estimating the effect of fruit-and-veg intake on blood pressure?

Think about it as a DAG question before running anything: does crisp-eating sit upstream of fruit-and-veg intake (a confounder), downstream of it (a bad control), or somewhere else entirely? No further syntax hints.

What you should have found

Gabor’s own answer: yes, condition on it — crisp-eating is a signal of an unhealthy diet more broadly, a plausible common cause of both low fruit-and-veg intake and (via other channels) blood pressure.

html`<p>In this reproduction, adding crisps as a control barely moves the fruit-and-veg coefficient (<strong>${food_coef}</strong> → <strong>${food_coef_chips}</strong>) — a real, honest result. Confounders don't always move the number by much; the discipline is in checking, not in expecting a dramatic swing every time.</p>`

Latent variables

The confounder you can’t measure

  • A latent variable is a confounder that lives in your reasoning but not in your data — “ability,” “motivation,” “management quality”
  • If it’s correlated with \(x\), leaving it out is still omitted variable bias — the fact that you can’t measure it doesn’t make the bias go away
  • Sometimes a rough proxy is available (years of education for ability, a past sales figure for motivation) — imperfect, but better than nothing

This is precisely the ceiling Week 6’s worked example (the gender pay gap) hit — multiple regression closes the gap between naive and adjusted estimates, but only for what’s actually in the data.

Bad controls

Not every correlated variable belongs

Week 6 first raised this with the TV-advertising example. Three specific shapes are worth naming:

  • Exogenous-source variable: unaffected by \(x\), unrelated to \(y\) except by chance — controlling for it does no harm, but doesn’t help either
  • Mechanism (mediator) variable: sits on the causal path from \(x\) to \(y\) — controlling for it removes part of the very effect you’re trying to measure
  • Collider variable: caused by both \(x\) and \(y\) — controlling for it can create a spurious association where none existed

The collider, specifically

  • \(C\) is a collision of two arrowheads — both \(X\) and \(Y\) cause it, neither causes the other
  • \(X\) and \(Y\) can be genuinely unrelated. But condition on \(C\) (e.g. restrict to a subgroup defined by it), and a fake association between \(X\) and \(Y\) appears — because within any fixed value of \(C\), knowing \(X\) tells you something about \(Y\)
  • Famous example: talent and looks might be unrelated in the general population, but conditioning on “being a celebrity” (caused by both) makes them look negatively correlated

Telling them apart is genuinely hard

There’s no shortcut that avoids thinking about the DAG. The question to ask of every control variable: does this sit upstream of \(x\) (a legitimate confounder to include), or downstream / on the path (a bad control to exclude)?

If you can’t draw the arrow with confidence, that’s a sign to say so explicitly in your write-up, not to guess.

What you should have found

There’s no single correct list here — the point is the discipline of naming each omitted variable’s type, not just noting “there might be other factors.” A vague acknowledgment doesn’t tell a reader whether you understand the direction or size of the risk; a typed list does.

A middle ground, briefly

Natural experiments

  • Occasionally, real-world events create variation that behaves as if it were randomly assigned — a policy change, a lottery, a sharp eligibility cutoff — without the analyst designing anything
  • These sit between pure observation and a controlled experiment, and support genuinely credible causal claims when the “as-if-random” story holds up
  • This module doesn’t go further into the specific designs (difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity) — worth knowing the name exists, and worth reading about if your own portfolio project stumbles across a setting where one might apply, but it’s out of scope here

Summary

  • Confounders come in three shapes: common cause, reverse causality, unwanted mechanism — and only the first is what Week 6’s OVB formula directly targets
  • Latent variables are confounders you can’t measure — they don’t stop being confounders for being invisible
  • Bad controls (mechanism variables, colliders) can make a model worse even though they’re correlated with \(y\) — more controls isn’t automatically better
  • When you can’t randomise, the honest move is naming what you couldn’t control for, not pretending the model closes every gap
