  const xb = d3.mean(rows, d => d[xk]), yb = d3.mean(rows, d => d[yk])

Hotels revisited: shape and noise

The same 207 Vienna hotels as last week. Is a straight line the right shape, and what happens when x is measured with noise?

Part 1: what shape is the line?

The question

Last week a straight line picked out the hotels that are cheap for their distance from the centre.

But that line assumed every extra mile knocks the same number of euros off the price, whether it’s the first mile or the sixth. Is that believable?

Today we follow the workshop’s roadmap: check the distributions, look at the shape, then choose a functional form.

The R² trap

R² goes up with every row of the table: 0.157, 0.205, 0.280, 0.334. Is log–log simply the best model?

No – you can only compare R² between models with the same y. R² for price measures how much of the variation in euros we explain; R² for ln(price) measures variation in log euros. They’re different things.

Valid comparisons: level–level vs. level–log (both explain price), and log–level vs. log–log (both explain ln(price)). In both pairs, logging distance fits better.

The rule: only compare R² between models that explain the same y. Logging x is fine; logging y changes what R² is measuring. The same goes for Friday’s log model of reaction time: R² of 0.056 for ln(reaction time) against 0.032 for reaction time is not a like-for-like comparison.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 8

Which one would you use?

What you want to know comes first.

  • Looking for a good deal on one night? You care about euros, so keep price in levels
  • Comparing across seasons, when prices rise and fall together? Percentages travel better, so log price
  • For finding a hotel, distance in miles is easier to think about than “10% further out”

Then the statistics: the scatterplots and R² both say logging distance helps. Gabor’s own verdict: “your call”. There’s no single right answer, only a choice you can defend.

Part 2: splines

When the slope changes at one point

LOWESS showed a steep drop within about a mile of the centre, then hardly any change.

  • A log bends the line smoothly, everywhere
  • A quadratic bends it smoothly too, as a parabola

But what if the pattern really is two straight lines: one steep slope near the centre, a flatter one further out? Then we want the slope to change at a point we choose.

Piecewise linear splines

A spline cuts x into intervals at knots and fits a straight line in each one. The lines join at the knots, but each can have its own slope.

\[y^E = \alpha_1 + \beta_1 x\,[x<k_1] + (\alpha_2+\beta_2 x)\,[k_1\le x< k_2] + \dots\]

  • Each \(\beta\) is the slope within its own interval: easy to interpret
  • It can follow any shape, including ones that go up and then down
  • The cost: you choose how many knots, and where

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 8

Gabor’s guideline: if you only want the average association, a straight line (or a log) is fine. Bend it when you care about prediction or residuals – like finding deals. And simpler is usually better.

Part 3: measurement error, using the ratings

Nothing is measured exactly, even in a hotel listing

  • Price is one night, on one date – not the hotel’s “true” price
  • Distance is measured to a city centre someone chose
  • Rating is the average of whatever reviews guests happened to leave

So every variable we’ve used has some error in it. Does that matter for the regression? It depends on which variable has the error, and what kind of error it is.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 8

A new question: ratings and price

Do better-rated hotels charge more? We regress ln(price) on the average customer rating.

But think about where that rating comes from. A hotel with 20 reviews has an average that a few unusual guests can move a lot. A hotel with 500 reviews has an average that’s hard to move.

The rating is measured with more noise for some hotels than others. That’s what we use to see measurement error in real data.

Classical measurement error

The measured value = true value + error, and the error:

  1. is zero on average, so it doesn’t shift the mean, and
  2. is unrelated to everything else, including the true value

For a rating: each review is one guest’s opinion, sometimes above the hotel’s “true” quality, sometimes below. Averaging over more reviews cancels more of it out. That’s close to classical error.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 8

Noise in y doesn’t bias the slope

Price on one night = “true” price + error. Because the error is unrelated to x:

\[\beta = \frac{Cov[y^* + e,\ x]}{Var[x]} = \frac{Cov[y^*, x] + \overbrace{Cov[e, x]}^{=\,0}}{Var[x]} = \beta^*\]

The same idea as Week 4’s \(E[\varepsilon \mid x] = 0\): random noise in y adds scatter around the line, which makes SEs bigger, but it doesn’t tilt the line.

Noise in x does: attenuation bias

Now the rating (x) has the noise:

\[\hat\beta = \beta^* \times \frac{Var[x^*]}{Var[x^*] + Var[e]}\]

  • The fraction is always below 1, so the slope is pulled towards zero
  • More noise, bigger shrinkage

Why: if two hotels’ ratings differ by 0.2, their true quality probably differs by less – some of that 0.2 is noise. So the price difference that goes with each rating point looks smaller than it really is.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 8

Flatter where the rating is noisier, as attenuation predicts. But the two CIs overlap, so this is consistent with attenuation rather than proof of it.

Noise-to-signal ratio

\[\text{noise-to-signal} = \frac{Var[e]}{Var[x^*]}\]

  • Low: careful recording, or a variable averaged over lots of observations. Usually safe to ignore
  • High: the noise is large compared with the real spread in x. Worth taking seriously
html`<p>The hotel ratings are a warning: among well-reviewed hotels the ratings barely vary (SD ${stats.many_sd} on a 5-point scale). With so little real spread, even a little noise is a big share of it.</p>`

When the error isn’t classical

The “always towards zero” result needs the error to be random. Hotel ratings might not be:

  • A hotel could ask its happiest guests to leave reviews
  • A new hotel’s first reviews might come from friends and family

Then the error is linked to the hotel itself, and the bias could go either way. There’s no formula to fix it; the answer is better measurement.
