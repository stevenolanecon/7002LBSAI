Binary Dependent Variables

Part One

Yes or No

Every outcome we have modelled so far has been a number that could take almost any value: a reaction time in milliseconds, a hotel price in euros, an hourly wage. Plenty of the questions a business actually asks are not like that. Did the customer churn? Did the loan default? Did the employee leave? The answer is yes or no, and in the data it is stored as a 1 or a 0.

That is exactly the outcome in the portfolio. Attrition is a yes/no variable, so this chapter is the last piece of the toolkit you need for it. The good news is that almost everything from the regression chapters carries over. The less good news is that a 0/1 outcome changes what the coefficients mean, adds a problem the straight line can't solve on its own, and needs different ways of judging whether the model is any good.

From the workshop

“With attrition it's fairly clear where the boundary is. Have you left? No, I'm still here, I'm literally in the building, my key card is logging in every day – then you're a zero. If you've stopped coming to work, left the job, resigned, you're a one. But sometimes it's not as obvious as that.”

The question

We work through one case from the textbook. Békés and Kézdi use the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE), which follows the same people over time and asks about their health, their habits and their circumstances. The sample is people aged 50 to 60 who were healthy when first surveyed. Four years later, each one either still rates their health as good (stayshealthy = 1) or doesn't (stayshealthy = 0).

The question: among people who are currently healthy, does smoking meaningfully lower the chance they stay that way – once the other differences between smokers and non-smokers are accounted for?

It is worth pausing on what “stays healthy” means. Here it comes from people rating their own health, which is a broad brush. With attrition the line between 0 and 1 is sharp; with health it has been drawn for us. Keep that in mind, because it is the idea behind Part Three.

The naive answer, and why it fails

The naive answer is to compare the share of smokers who stayed healthy with the share of non-smokers who did, and call the gap the effect of smoking. It fails for a reason you already know from Chapter Six: smokers and non-smokers differ in many other ways. They differ in age, in how much they exercise, in income and in education, and each of those is related to health too. A raw comparison mixes all of that into one number.

So we need three things. We need a regression that works when y is only ever 0 or 1, so that we can control for those differences. We need a way to read its coefficients when y is a probability rather than a quantity. And we need a way to judge the fit of a model that can never predict exactly 0 or exactly 1.

Part Two

A 0/1 Outcome Is a Probability

Regression is about the conditional expected value of y: the average of y for people with given values of x. With a wage, that average is a number of pounds. With a 0/1 variable something nicer happens. The average of a set of 0s and 1s is just the share of 1s, and the share of 1s is the probability that someone picked at random has y = 1:

$$E[y] = P[y=1] \qquad\qquad E[y \mid x] = P[y = 1 \mid x]$$

If 25 people in a room of 100 stayed healthy, the average of stayshealthy is 0.25, and the chance that a randomly chosen person stayed healthy is 25%. Frequency and probability become the same statement. So when y is binary, modelling y means modelling a probability, and every coefficient becomes a change in the probability that y = 1.

That probability has a name you will see throughout: we write it as yP, short for P[y = 1 | x1, x2, …]. Changes in it are measured in percentage points. A move from 60% to 54% is 6 percentage points; calling it “6 percent” would mean something else (6% of 60% is 3.6 points), and mixing the two up is one of the most common slips in written reports.

Part Three

The Latent Variable

Health does not really switch between two states. Some people are robustly healthy, some are fragile but not yet ill, some are already declining. A survey that records only “stayed healthy” or “didn't” has collapsed a whole continuum into two boxes.

A useful way to think about any binary outcome is that something continuous sits underneath it. Call it y*, the latent variable – latent because we never observe it. Here y* is each person's underlying health. All we see is which side of a threshold it ended up on:

$$y = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } y^* > 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } y^* \le 0 \end{cases}$$
From the workshop

“Think about measurements like BMI. It's not binary, but you get to a point where they go, you were overweight yesterday, but you've gone down by about 0.1 and you've crossed that threshold. … Just marginally crossing that threshold flips you from one category into the other – so clearly there's a lot of flattening of information in there. But often that's all you have.”

The predicted health score and the error

Now suppose y* follows an ordinary regression, if only we could see it:

$$y^* = \underbrace{\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \dots}_{\text{predicted health score}} \; + \; e$$

Split it into two parts. The first is the part we can predict from what we know about the person – smoking, age, income, exercise and the rest. Call it their predicted health score, or score for short. A higher score means better expected health. The second part, e, is everything we don't observe: genes, luck, stress, an illness nobody has diagnosed yet. For any one person it might help or hurt.

Someone stays healthy when score + e > 0, which is the same as saying e > −score. The score is a head start, and e is luck: you stay healthy unless your bad luck is bigger than your head start. So the probability of staying healthy is the chance that e lands above −score. That is an area under the curve that describes how e is spread across people.

The bell curve shows how luck, e, is spread across people. The dashed line marks how much bad luck it takes to fall ill. The shaded share to its right is everyone whose luck isn't bad enough to tip them over: the probability of staying healthy. A higher score moves the line left and the shaded share grows, but it can never fall below 0 or rise above 1. The logistic curve is drawn stretched to the normal's spread, as explained below.

From the area to a formula

Statisticians call the area to the left of a point the cumulative distribution function, or CDF, and write it F. We want the area to the right of −score, which is 1 minus the CDF there. For a curve that is symmetric around zero, as both of ours are, the area to the right of −score equals the area to the left of +score. So

$$\begin{aligned} P[y = 1 \mid x] &= P[e > -\text{score}] = 1 - F(-\text{score}) \\ &= F(\text{score}) = F(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots) \end{aligned}$$

Everything in the rest of the chapter follows from that line. The probability is the CDF of the error, evaluated at the score. Because a CDF is a share of a curve, it always lies between 0 and 1 – which is exactly what a probability needs.

Probit, logit, and a little history

Which curve should e follow? The natural first assumption is the normal distribution. That gives the probit model, where F is the standard normal CDF, written Φ.

The trouble is that there is no simple formula for an area under the normal curve. Until computers were cheap, those areas came from printed tables, and estimating a probit model took a great deal of work. The logistic distribution has almost exactly the same bell shape, and its CDF has a formula you can write down:

$$\Lambda(z) = \frac{\exp(z)}{1 + \exp(z)}$$

Assume e is logistic and you get the logit model. It was easier to compute, close enough to the probit to give much the same answers, and it became the habit.

From the workshop

“This was long before anyone had powerful computers – computers in the days when they were doing this took up an entire room. So having something you could actually sit down with a piece of paper and estimate yourself was really important. These days it's not at all; Python will run either of these instantaneously. But the habit has stuck. … I like my logit, and I'm going to stay with it.”

One technical detail is worth knowing even though you won't be examined on it. The raw logistic curve is more spread out than the standard normal. To give the same probabilities, a logit model needs bigger coefficients – roughly 1.6 times the probit's. That is why Figure 8.1 draws the logistic curve stretched to match: Λ(1.6z) sits almost on top of Φ(z). It is also why you should never compare a logit coefficient directly with a probit one.

The third option is to ignore the error distribution altogether and fit a straight line to the 0s and 1s. That is the linear probability model, and it is where we start in Part Five.

Part Four

One Family: the Generalised Linear Model

The linear probability model, logit and probit are not three unrelated techniques. They belong to one family, the generalised linear model (GLM), along with ordinary least squares and the Poisson model for counts. Every member has the same three parts:

  • A linear predictorη = β0 + β1x1 + … – the same right-hand side as every regression since Chapter Four. Logs, squares, splines and interactions all still go in here, because the model is still linear in its parameters.
  • A link functionThe function that turns η into the expected value of y. In the latent-variable story, it comes from the assumption about the error.
  • A distribution for yFor a 0/1 outcome, each observation is a single yes/no draw with probability yP.

OLS is the special case where the link function does nothing at all: put the linear predictor in, and out comes the conditional mean. The other members put it through a transformation first. Statistical software does that part for you; your job is to choose the model and read what comes out.

Part Five

The Linear Probability Model

The simplest option is to treat the 0/1 outcome like any other y and run OLS:

$$y^P = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \dots$$

This is the linear probability model (LPM). It is plain OLS, so everything from Chapters Four to Six still applies: logs, polynomials, splines, dummies and interactions all work the same way. The only change is the reading. β1 is now the difference in the probability that y = 1, in percentage points, for a one-unit difference in x1, holding the other variables fixed.

Why robust standard errors are essential

One assumption behind OLS's usual standard errors is homoskedasticity: the spread of the residuals is the same everywhere along x. With a 0/1 outcome that assumption fails every time. At any value of x, y can only be 0 or 1, so the residual can only take two values: 1 − ŷP if y = 1, or −ŷP if y = 0. The variance of a yes/no outcome with probability p is p(1 − p), so

$$Var[y \mid x] = y^P\,(1 - y^P)$$

That depends on the predicted probability, and the predicted probability changes with x. So the spread can't be constant. It is largest at yP = 0.5 (variance 0.25) and shrinks towards zero near 0 and 1. This isn't a feature of any particular dataset; it is built into the arithmetic of a 0/1 variable. The fix is the one you already use: heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors, cov_type="HC1", on every LPM, without exception.

Part Seven

Adding the Controls

The raw gap compares smokers with non-smokers who differ in many other ways. Multiple regression lets us compare people who are otherwise alike. Following the textbook, we add whether someone ever smoked, gender, age, years of education, an income group (deciles, 1 to 10), BMI, whether they exercise regularly, and country.

df["country"] = df["country"].astype(str).astype("category")   # one dummy per country

model = smf.ols("stayshealthy ~ smoking + ever_smoked + female + age + eduyears"
                " + income10 + bmi + exerc + country", data=df).fit(cov_type="HC1")
VariableCoefficientRobust SEp

Each coefficient now reads “holding the others fixed”. Country enters as a set of dummies, one for each of the countries minus a reference country. Countries differ in health systems, diets and base levels of health, so it matters to compare like with like within a country. The country coefficients themselves are hard to read and usually left out of a report unless the question is about countries.

Two reading points. First, the intercept stops meaning anything useful: it is the probability of staying healthy for someone aged zero with a BMI of zero and no education, and nobody in the data looks like that. It still matters to the model, but don't interpret it. Second, statsmodels doesn't print significance stars, but many tables do: *** usually means significant at 1%, ** at 5%, * at 10%.

Is this a kitchen sink?

We have added every control the dataset offers. That deserves a challenge. A kitchen-sink regression throws everything in and hopes; a good specification makes each control earn its place.

  • Likely confoundersAge, education, income, gender and country come before smoking or alongside it. They can drive both smoking and health, so leaving them out would bias the smoking coefficient (Chapter Six's omitted variable bias). Control for them.
  • Possible bad controlsBMI and exercise are less clear. Smoking may itself change weight and how much people exercise. If it does, they sit partly between smoking and health, and holding them fixed soaks up part of smoking's own effect.
From the workshop

“You almost need to think of it as putting each explanatory variable on trial: justify why you should be in this model, and justify what form you should be in it. … This is where, as business analysts, domain knowledge is key. … A variable that could only happen after attrition was recorded would have no place in there.”

We use the textbook's controls, but enter them all as straight lines, where the book uses piecewise splines for education and BMI. So our numbers are close to theirs rather than identical. In your own work, the honest test is to drop the doubtful controls and see whether the answer moves.

Part Eight

The LPM's Range Problem

So far the LPM has done everything we asked. The trouble starts when we use it to predict. Each person gets a fitted value ŷP, and that fitted value is meant to be a probability.

pred = model.predict(df)
pred.min(), pred.max(), (pred > 1).sum(), (pred < 0).sum()

A straight line has no reason to stop at 0 or 1. Push x far enough and it goes past either end. In this dataset only one person crosses the line, and only just. With other data – more extreme values of x, an outcome that is very rare or very common – it can be dozens of people, well past the boundary.

From the workshop

“If it was 1.04, it's saying the predicted probability of staying healthy is 104%, which I would like to be true for me. But we're not footballers after a match saying we gave it 110%. You can't tell a client the chance of this outcome happening is 104%. It would be embarrassing for everyone in the room.”

For learning about relationships the LPM is often perfectly good, and its coefficients are the easiest to read. For prediction, we need a model that keeps every predicted probability strictly between 0 and 1.

Part Nine

Logit and Probit

Part Three already gave us the answer. Put the linear score through a function that only ever returns values between 0 and 1 – a CDF – and the predictions can't escape:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Logit:}\quad y^P &= \Lambda(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots) = \frac{\exp(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots)}{1 + \exp(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots)} \\[6pt] \text{Probit:}\quad y^P &= \Phi(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots) \end{aligned}$$

The function that turns the score into a probability is the link function. Plot the three side by side and the difference is plain.

Figure 8.3

Three link functions

The predicted probability against the score, z = β0 + β1x1 + …. The LPM is a straight line and runs past 0 and 1. The logit (drawn as Λ(1.6z)) and the probit are S-shaped and stay between 0 and 1. On the same scale they are almost indistinguishable; the logit approaches 0 and 1 a little more slowly, so it has slightly thicker tails.

Békés and Kézdi's summary of the picture: both Λ and Φ are increasing S-shaped curves, bounded between 0 and 1. The difference between them is small. And because z is a combination of βs and x's, the estimated βs come out different in logit and probit even when their probabilities agree.

From the workshop

“When you're using logit or probit, essentially what you're doing is putting a bit of curve on your OLS. It's just ensuring you don't have to stand up and say the probability of this is 150%, and get fired.”

Fitting all three

In Python the change is one word. The formula stays exactly the same; only the estimator changes.

f = ("stayshealthy ~ smoking + ever_smoked + female + age + eduyears"
     " + income10 + bmi + exerc + country")

logit  = smf.logit(f, data=df).fit(cov_type="HC1")

Part Ten

Why Raw Coefficients Stop Working

In the LPM the slope is the same everywhere: one more unit of x moves the probability by β, wherever you start. On an S-curve that is no longer true. The curve is steepest in the middle, at yP = 0.5, flatter further away, and very flat near 0 and 1. So the same change in the score moves the probability a lot for someone in the middle and hardly at all for someone near the ends.

Figure 8.5 · Interactive

The same coefficient, different changes in probability

Where the person starts (z)

A made-up logit with β1 = 0.5. The dot shows where a person starts on the curve; the step shows what one more unit of x does to them. Starting at 0.5, the probability rises by about 12 points. Starting near 0.9 or 0.1, the same coefficient gives only a few points.

Formally, the change in probability for a small change in xk is the slope of the curve at that point, which is the coefficient times the height of the error curve there:

$$\frac{\partial\, y^P}{\partial x_k} = \beta_k \, f(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots) \qquad\qquad \text{logit:}\;\; \beta_k\, y^P (1 - y^P)$$

For the logit, yP(1 − yP) is at most 0.25, reached at yP = 0.5. So the most one more unit of xk can ever move the probability in a logit is βk/4, that is 25 × βk percentage points – and for most people it is less.

The consequence is the one Békés and Kézdi stress: a raw logit or probit coefficient has no direct reading as a change in probability. Its sign tells you the direction, and its p-value tells you about significance. Its size, on its own, tells you very little.

Going further. Logit coefficients do have one exact reading: βk is the change in the log-odds, ln(yP/(1 − yP)), and exp(βk) is an odds ratio. You will meet odds ratios in medical and marketing reports. They are correct but notoriously easy to misread as probabilities, which is why this module reports marginal effects instead.

Part Eleven

Marginal Differences

The fix is to turn the coefficients back into changes in probability. For each person, work out how much their predicted probability changes with one more unit of x, given where they sit on the curve. Then average those changes over everyone in the data. Békés and Kézdi call the result the average marginal difference. Software calls it the average marginal effect (AME), or sometimes the average partial effect – they are the same thing.

For a dummy like smoking, “one more unit” means switching from 0 to 1. So for every person in the data we can ask: what would their predicted probability be as a non-smoker, and as a smoker, with everything else about them unchanged? The difference is their individual effect. Figure 8.6 shows all of them.

Figure 8.6

Everyone's own effect of smoking

From the full logit model: for each person, predicted probability of staying healthy as a smoker minus as a non-smoker, with everything else held at their own values. The dashed line is the average of all of them: the average marginal effect.

An AME has exactly the same interpretation as an LPM coefficient: the average difference in the probability that y = 1 for a one-unit difference in x. In Python it is one more line after the logit:

import marginaleffects as me

ame = me.avg_slopes(logit)
print(ame)

The output has one row per variable (and one for each country other than the reference country). estimate is the AME, with its own standard error, p-value and confidence interval. For a dummy, contrast reads 1 - 0; for a continuous variable it reads dY/dX, the slope.

You can compare their size. But you wouldn't want to say, if you smoke and you do some exercise, you knock all of that out – there are lots of other correlations going on in there as well.”

Does probit agree with logit?

That is the general lesson. Logit and probit almost always give the same predicted probabilities and the same marginal effects; pick one and stick with it. And the LPM's coefficients are often close to the AMEs as well. When they are, the LPM was already good enough for interpreting the relationship – but the logit or probit is the safer choice the moment you want to predict.

Part Twelve

How Logit and Probit Are Estimated

From the workshop

“Logit and probit are done with a process called maximum likelihood, which you don't need to know about. I'll put it in the lecture notes for those that are curious – I can't imagine there's many of you.”

For the curious, then. OLS chooses the coefficients that make the squared residuals as small as possible, and there is a formula for the answer. Logit and probit can't work that way: the S-curve gets in the way, and there is no formula. Instead they use maximum likelihood. It isn't examined, but it explains three things you will see in every logit output: the log-likelihood, the pseudo-R², and the line that says how many iterations it took.

The idea

Pick some values for the coefficients. For each person, the model then gives a predicted probability of staying healthy. Now ask: if these coefficients were right, how likely is it that we would have seen exactly the 0s and 1s in our data? A person who stayed healthy contributes their predicted probability, yP; a person who didn't contributes 1 − yP. Multiply these across everyone and you have the likelihood. In practice we add up their logs instead, which gives the log-likelihood:

$$\ln L(\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Big[\, y_i \ln \hat y^P_i + (1 - y_i) \ln\big(1 - \hat y^P_i\big) \Big]$$

Every term is the log of a probability, so every term is negative, and the log-likelihood is always below zero. Coefficients that predict high probabilities for the people who stayed healthy and low ones for those who didn't make it less negative. Maximum likelihood chooses the coefficients that make it as large (as close to zero) as possible.

There is no formula for that maximum, so the software searches for it: start somewhere, see which way the log-likelihood rises, step that way, and repeat until the steps make no difference. That is what Iterations and converged: True mean in the statsmodels output. Search algorithms like this come back in a big way in machine learning.

Try it yourself

With one dummy variable, the log-likelihood only needs four counts: how many non-smokers and smokers there are, and how many of each stayed healthy. So we can search by hand. Figure 8.7 does the logit of staying healthy on smoking alone.

Why this explains the goodness-of-fit numbers

Because logit and probit have a log-likelihood, we can compare it with the log-likelihood of a model with no explanatory variables at all – one that predicts the same overall share for everyone. McFadden's pseudo-R² is

$$\text{pseudo-}R^2 = 1 - \frac{\ln L_{\text{model}}}{\ln L_{\text{no } x}}$$

The LPM is estimated by OLS, not maximum likelihood, so it has no log-likelihood of its own and no pseudo-R². That is why the LPM row of the next table shows “n.a.” for pseudo-R². Its log-loss can still be worked out from its predictions, as Part Thirteen shows.

Part Thirteen

Is the Model Any Good?

The logit output has no R². That is deliberate. A probability model never predicts exactly 0 or exactly 1, but every actual outcome is 0 or 1. So even a very good model leaves a residual for almost everyone, and R²'s reading as “the share of variation explained” stops being a useful yardstick. You can still compute it – it is in the table below – but we need measures built for probabilities.

  • Brier scoreThe average squared distance between the predicted probability and the actual 0 or 1. Smaller is better; a perfect model scores 0. It is the same squared-error idea behind R² in Chapter Four, applied to a probability.
  • Pseudo-R²McFadden's measure from Part Twelve. Bigger is better. Logit and probit only.
  • Log-lossThe average log-likelihood per person. Always negative; closer to zero is better. (scikit-learn and most machine learning texts report it with the sign flipped, as a positive number where smaller is better. Same measure.)

You don't need to work the Brier score out step by step. Once the model is fitted, it is one line:

import numpy as np

np.mean((logit.predict(df) - df["stayshealthy"]) ** 2)   # Brier score

Swap in probit or lpm for the other models. scikit-learn has the same calculation built in, brier_score_loss(df["stayshealthy"], logit.predict(df)), but it refuses predictions outside 0 and 1, so it fails on an LPM that predicts above 1; the NumPy line works for every model. The other two measures come straight from a fitted logit or probit:

logit.prsquared          # pseudo-R-squared (also in .summary())
logit.llf / logit.nobs   # log-loss

The LPM has no log-likelihood to read off, but its log-loss can be computed from its predictions. One prediction is above 1, so clip them just inside 0 and 1 first:

y = df["stayshealthy"]
p = lpm.predict(df).clip(1e-6, 1 - 1e-6)
np.mean(y * np.log(p) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - p))   # LPM log-loss
ModelR²Brier scorePseudo-R²Log-loss

These numbers mean little on their own. A Brier score of 0.215 isn't good or bad in any absolute sense. They are for comparing – this model against that one, this set of controls against another, a squared age term against none – on the same data and the same outcome. That is how you will use them in the portfolio. Read across a row to see what each model achieves, and down a column to compare models; never compare a Brier score with an R².

From the workshop

“Because you're only ever comparing against different models – you'd try one with a different functional form on age, say, and then compare. In terms of interpreting it, the actual number is less important. You just need to know whether low or high is good.”

Part Fourteen

Calibration

A model can be right on average and still be wrong in places – too optimistic for people it thinks are at low risk, too pessimistic at the top. Calibration asks the stricter question: is it right at every level of predicted probability?

Take everyone the model gives about a 30% chance of staying healthy. If roughly 30% of them actually did, the model is well calibrated there. Do that across the whole range: sort people by their predicted probability, cut them into ten equal groups (deciles), and plot each group's average predicted probability against the share who actually stayed healthy. That is a calibration curve.

import pandas as pd

df["pred"]   = logit.predict(df)
df["decile"] = pd.qcut(df["pred"], 10, labels=False)
calib = df.groupby("decile").agg(pred=("pred", "mean"), actual=("stayshealthy", "mean"))

Part Fifteen

So, Does Smoking Matter?

What we have not shown is that smoking causes worse health. Every number in this chapter is an association, however carefully controlled. People choose whether to smoke, and the things that drive that choice may also drive their health in ways our controls can't see. The textbook's own conclusion is careful in the same way.

The chapter in four points

  • It's a probabilityWith a 0/1 outcome you are modelling the probability that y = 1, and coefficients are changes in that probability, in percentage points. The latent-variable story explains why a link function is needed.
  • The LPMOLS with a 0/1 y. Simple, easy to read, always with robust standard errors – but its predictions can fall outside 0 and 1.
  • Logit and probitFix the range problem with an S-shaped link. Their raw coefficients can't be read as probabilities, so report average marginal effects. Pick one; they almost always agree.
  • Judging fitBrier score, pseudo-R², log-loss and calibration, used to compare models rather than read in isolation.
