Forecasting from time series data

The workshop found genuine relationships in time series data. This session: using that same data to predict what hasn’t happened yet.

Planning next year’s staffing

Business question: a swimming pool operator needs to know roughly how many tickets will sell each day next year, to plan staffing and opening hours.

Forecasting is a special case of prediction — using only what’s happened up to today to say something about tomorrow. Same discipline as Week 10’s prediction content, applied to data where “the future” has a very literal meaning.

Forecast horizon and cross-validation

Forecast horizon

  • The length of the live time series data still to come — the forecast horizon, \(H\)
  • Short-horizon: a few periods ahead relative to the data (5 years of monthly data, forecast 3–12 months)
  • Long-horizon: many periods ahead (5 years of daily data, forecast a year out)

Why you can’t just shuffle and split

Cross-validation with time series is necessary — and genuinely tricky, because a random shuffle destroys the one thing that makes it a time series: order.

Two approaches, matched to the horizon:

  • Insert test sets — for long-horizon forecasts, where serial correlation and trend matter less. Use all remaining observations, including those after the test set, as training data.
  • Rolling window — for short-horizon forecasts, where serial correlation matters. Training data always precedes the test set, so nothing “sees the future.”

Long-horizon: trend and seasonality

Two ways to model a trend

  • Average change: \(\hat y_{T+H} = y_T + H \times \widehat{\Delta y}\) — carries forward from the last observation
  • Trend line: \(\hat y_t = \hat\alpha + \hat\delta t\) — assumes \(y\) stays close to a fitted line, so one unusual last observation matters less
  • Neither is inherently better — depends whether you trust the series to keep going from where it is, or to revert to its long-run pattern

Short-horizon: AR and ARIMA

Autoregressive models

\[y_t^E = \beta_0 + \beta_1 y_{t-1}\]

  • The simplest AR(1) model: today’s value predicted from yesterday’s alone
  • \(\beta_1\) is just the first-order serial correlation coefficient from the workshop, wearing a forecasting hat
  • \(\beta_1 = 1\) is a random walk; \(\beta_1 = 0\) is white noise — nothing left to forecast from the series itself

The innovation

\[e_t = y_t - y_t^E \qquad y_t = y_t^E + e_t\]

The innovation: what’s genuinely new in \(y_t\), on top of everything predictable from its own past. Formally the same idea as a prediction error — but now it’s past innovations doing the predicting.

ARIMA, built from three letters

  • AR(p): predict \(y_t\) from up to \(p\) of its own lags
  • I(d): whether the model is written in \(y\) itself (\(d=0\)) or its \(d\)-th difference
  • MA(q): a linear combination of past innovations, \(y_t = e_t + \theta_1 e_{t-1} + \dots + \theta_q e_{t-q}\) — captures serial correlation that drops to zero sharply after a few lags, a different pattern than AR
  • ARIMA(p,d,q) combines all three, flexible enough to approximate almost any serial correlation pattern

VAR — bringing in other variables

Forecasting with help from x

  • Short-horizon forecasts often improve with other variables — but now you need forecasts of those too
  • Vector autoregression (VAR): a set of time series regressions, one per variable, where every equation’s right-hand side is lags of every variable in the system

\[y_t^E = \beta_{10} + \beta_{11}y_{t-1} + \beta_{12}x_{t-1} \qquad x_t^E = \beta_{20} + \beta_{21}y_{t-1} + \beta_{22}x_{t-1}\]

  • More coefficients to estimate means more estimation error — the hope is that \(x\) still nets out an improvement

Does it help here?

Adding California’s unemployment rate to a VAR(2), forecasting the same 2017 holdout:

from statsmodels.tsa.api import VAR

# Both series first-differenced — VAR needs stationary inputs, same logic as Section C
var_train = df[["price_index_sa", "unemployment_sa"]].diff().dropna()

var_model = VAR(var_train).fit(2)
var_forecast_diffs = var_model.forecast(var_train.values[-2:], steps=len(test))

# Undo the differencing: cumulative sum of forecast differences, added back onto
# the last known level
var_levels = train.iloc[-1] + np.cumsum(var_forecast_diffs[:, 0])
var_rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean((test.values - var_levels) ** 2))

External validity in time series

The one assumption everything rests on

  • External validity in forecasting means stationarity: the patterns you learned from the training data — trend, seasonality, correlation structure — have to still hold in the live data
  • This is a much bigger risk in time series than in cross-sectional prediction, because forecasting is by design about a period you’ve never observed
  • No amount of cross-validation on historical data can fully rule this out — a genuine structural break (a pandemic, a housing crash) breaks every model here equally

Summary

  • Forecasting is prediction with a time index — but cross-validation has to respect order: inserted test sets for long horizons, rolling windows for short ones
  • Long-horizon forecasts lean on trend and seasonality — often seasonality alone does most of the work
  • Short-horizon forecasts lean on serial correlation — AR and ARIMA models capture it directly, chosen by holdout RMSE like any other prediction model
  • VAR can improve a forecast by bringing in other variables, at the cost of needing to forecast those too — and whether it actually helps is itself an empirical, not automatic, question
