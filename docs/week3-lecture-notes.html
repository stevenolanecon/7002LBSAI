When y Met x: Correlation and Covariance

Part One

A Shortcut for HR

For two chapters we've looked at one variable at a time – its shape, its centre, its spread, and how far we can trust a single number computed from it. That's useful, but it's rarely the question anyone is paying you to answer. Real questions almost always involve two things: does this go with that? When one moves, does the other? And if it looks like they do, how much should we believe it?

From the workshop

“Today we're almost looking at a love story – when y met x. It's basically about the relationship between two variables, and what we can say about them. To a certain extent this will form the basis of so much of your analysis.”

So here's this chapter's scenario. Our reaction-time test works, but it's slow to administer – ten trials per person. HR wants something quicker: “find us a quick physical measurement that predicts reaction time, and we'll screen candidates on that instead.” That is why, back in Week 1, everyone measured the length of their index finger.

From the workshop

“We have to imagine we're in a scenario where it would be ethical to take physical measurements of your staff when you're onboarding them. Clearly illegal – not a very realistic scenario – but one that we're working with today.”

Ethics aside, the question is a genuinely good one: is finger length a fast, reliable stand-in for the real test? This chapter gives us the tools to answer it properly – and, just as importantly, the discipline not to answer it too quickly.

Part Two

The y and the x

A huge amount of data analysis comes down to the same move: comparing values of one variable, y, across values of another, x, looking for patterns of association. By convention, y is the variable we're interested in – the outcome variable – and x is the one we compare across. When we start writing models down as equations, from next chapter, y sits on the left-hand side and x on the right, which is why you'll also hear them called the left-hand-side and right-hand-side variables (or, in plenty of textbooks, the dependent and independent variables).

There are two quite different reasons we might want to compare y across x:

  • PredictionUse x to predict y. If the prediction is good, we don't strictly need to know why it works. Can finger length predict someone's average reaction time?
  • Causal effectLearn what would happen to y if we changed x. If someone's finger grew by a centimetre, what would happen to their reaction time?

Put that way, the causal version sounds faintly absurd for finger length – nobody is proposing to stretch anyone's fingers. HR's proxy-test idea is a prediction goal: they just want a cheap measurement that lines up with the expensive one. Keep the two goals separate in your head, though, because in practice they blur constantly, and a lot of this module is about noticing when they have.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Data Analysis for Business, Economics, and Policy, Ch. 4
Part Three

Association Is Not Causation

Two variables are associated when the values of y tend to differ across the values of x – when knowing x tells you something about y. That association comes in three broad flavours:

  • PositiveHigher values of x go with higher values of y, on average. The two move in the same direction.
  • NegativeHigher values of x go with lower values of y, on average. They move in opposite directions.
  • Noney looks the same whatever the value of x. The variables are independent.

Note the “on average”. A positive association doesn't mean every single person with a longer finger is slower; it means that, across the sample, the pattern leans that way.

Everything in this chapter – conditional probabilities, conditional means, covariance, correlation – is a way of describing how y differs across x. And none of it, on its own, tells you why. Asked in the room what we can't say about an association, the answer came back almost immediately: correlation is not causation. A positive association between x and y is equally consistent with x causing y, y causing x, or – very often – some third thing driving both.

Watch your language. This is the single most common mistake analysts make, at every level of seniority: finding an association and then describing it as if one thing caused the other. The honest phrasing is that you have evidence consistent with a hypothesis – not that you've proved it. Clients sometimes find that hedging frustrating. It's still the job.

We'll come back to what it would actually take to say something causal at the end of the chapter. Until then, everything is association.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 4
Part Four

Joint and Conditional Probability

Start with the simplest case: both y and x are binary – each can only take one of two values. Device (laptop or phone) and caffeine in the last two hours (yes or no) are both binary. You can't take the average of “phone”, so instead we talk about probabilities: if I picked one person at random, how likely is it that they're a phone user who's had caffeine?

Here's a worked example, slightly different from our own data – twenty students, set out in a cross-tab (a table counting every combination of the two variables):

Worked example: 20 students by device and caffeine use.
Device \ CaffeineCaffeineNo caffeineTotal
Phone9312
Laptop268
Total11920

The joint probability is the chance that both things happen together. Nine of the twenty students used a phone and had caffeine, so:

$$P(\text{Caffeine and Phone}) = \frac{9}{20} = 0.45$$

Now a different question. Suppose I split the room in two, send the laptop users away, and pick someone from the phone users who are left. What's the chance they've had caffeine? That's a conditional probability – the chance of A, restricted to the cases where B happened:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(B)} $$ $$P(\text{Caffeine} \mid \text{Phone}) = \frac{9}{12} = 0.75$$

Same nine people on top. What changed is the bottom of the fraction: we're now drawing from a smaller population of twelve phone users rather than all twenty students. That's what conditioning means in practice – partitioning the data and looking only inside one part of it. In the jargon, x (here, device) is the conditioning variable.

That vertical line is a pipe, not a slash. \(P(A \mid B)\) reads “the probability of A, given B” or “conditional on B”. It is not division. On most keyboards it's the shifted key next to the left Shift (Mac) or the backslash key (PC), and you'll be typing it a lot.
From the workshop

“Maths is only a shorthand, because we get bored of writing out sentences. ‘The probability of caffeine use among phone users’ isn't a long sentence, but it's still longer than that. Journalists have their own shorthand that nobody else can read; analysts and mathematicians have theirs. That's all it is. It's not supposed to be alienating.”

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 4
Part Five

Independence

Two events are independent if knowing one tells you nothing about the other. Formally, conditioning on B doesn't change the probability of A at all:

$$P(A \mid B) = P(A)$$

Independence buys you a very convenient shortcut: the joint probability is just the two individual probabilities multiplied together, \(P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B)\). But that shortcut only works under independence. The version that always holds, independent or not, is:

$$P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A \mid B) \times P(B)$$

Check it against the worked example. Overall, 11 of the 20 students had caffeine, so \(P(\text{Caffeine}) = 0.55\). Among phone users it was \(0.75\). Those aren't equal, so device and caffeine are not independent in this table. Use the shortcut anyway and you get \(0.6 \times 0.55 = 0.33\) – a long way from the real joint probability of \(0.45\). The shortcut doesn't fail gracefully; it gives you a confidently wrong number.

Part Six

Device and Caffeine: Our Own Data

Now the real thing – one row per student from our own Week 1 data:

The row and column totals give you the marginal probabilities – the probability of each variable on its own, ignoring the other, using the whole class as the population. Sixteen of 24 used a laptop, so \(P(\text{Laptop}) = 16/24 = 2/3\). Six of 24 had caffeine, so \(P(\text{Caffeine}) = 6/24 = 0.25\). No conditioning has happened yet.

The in-class task was the conditional version: what is \(P(\text{Caffeine} \mid \text{Phone})\)? The trick, which several people found once it clicked, is to read the expression from the right-hand side – start with what you're conditioning on.

From the workshop

“I could say all laptop users get out of the room, and then I've got eight. Imagine I don't know who's a caffeine user, and I pick one of you. I've got three chances out of that eight to get a caffeine user.”

So \(P(\text{Caffeine} \mid \text{Phone}) = 3/8 = 37.5\%\). On its own, that number doesn't answer the follow-up question – does knowing someone's device change your guess about their caffeine use? For that you need the comparison: among laptop users it's \(3/16 \approx 18.8\%\). Phone users in our class were roughly twice as likely to have had a coffee in the two hours before the experiment. That is a real, communicable finding about our data, and exactly the kind of statistic people quote in the real world.

How far would you trust it, though?

From the workshop

“Say I take this study to a newspaper: ‘I have found a near twenty-percentage-point difference in caffeine use between phone and laptop users!’ ‘Wow, amazing – what did you do?’ ‘I did it on a Friday afternoon, amongst 24 students.’ They'd be like, please get out of my office.”

Independence isn't a yes/no question you can answer from a table this small. Any real sample will show some gap between the conditional and marginal probabilities purely by chance – even when the true relationship in the population is independence. Telling a real pattern from sampling noise is exactly what Chapter 2's tools (standard errors, hypothesis tests) are for, and from next chapter regression will hand you those tests in a couple of lines of Python. With three caffeine drinkers in each group, “we can't tell yet” is the honest answer.

Condition on

Switch the control to condition on caffeine instead and the question changes: of the six caffeine drinkers, what share used a phone? Three out of six, or 50% – against 8 of 24, a third, for the class overall. Several people in the room expected flipping the condition to give the same number back, and it's a very natural assumption. It's wrong:

$$P(\text{Phone} \mid \text{Caffeine}) = \frac{3}{6} = 0.50$$ $$P(\text{Caffeine} \mid \text{Phone}) = \frac{3}{8} = 0.375$$

Same numerator, different denominator. Conditioning on caffeine splits the room in a different way – into the “fairly jittery” caffeine drinkers and the “very chilled” non-drinkers, as it was put on the day – so you're working inside a different population, answering a different question. In words it's obvious: “what share of phone users drank coffee?” and “what share of coffee drinkers used a phone?” are not the same question, and there's no reason they should share an answer.

Part Eight

The Conditional Mean

Binary variables only get you so far. Reaction time isn't binary – it's continuous, with a whole distribution, as Chapter 1 spent a long time showing. When y is continuous, the natural thing to compare across x is its average. That's the conditional expectation, or conditional mean:

$$E[y \mid x]$$

In plain English: if we know x takes a certain value, what's the average of y? “The average reaction time, given that we're only looking at phone users” is a conditional mean. It's the mean of y, computed separately for each value (or group) of x – the same split-the-room move as before, followed by an average instead of a count.

When x has only a few values, that's straightforward, and a box plot or histogram per group (Chapter 1) shows it well. When x has many values – finger length in millimetres, say – we have to make decisions about grouping, or binning, x first. Before we can do that with our finger-length data, though, there's a problem to deal with.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 4
Part Nine

Something's Wrong With Our Finger Lengths

Here are all 24 of our finger-length measurements, exactly as they were entered, anonymised and shuffled. Five of them are wrong. Before reading on, find them – and decide what you'd do about them.

The room found them quickly: two implausibly long fingers (700 and 900mm – the better part of a metre) and three implausibly short ones (6.9, 7.0 and 8.2mm). With 24 rows you can eyeball this. With a thousand you'd do what one student suggested straight away: compute summary statistics and look for values far outside the plausible range.

Spotting them is the easy half. The real question is what to do next, and there were good arguments in the room for both main options – drop the five students, or correct the values. Look at the two error patterns, though:

  • Too smallAlmost certainly centimetres entered where millimetres were asked for. A 6.9 is very plausibly a real 69mm finger in the wrong unit.
  • Too largeAlmost certainly an extra typed digit. A 900 is very plausibly a real 90mm finger with a stray zero.

Both have a specific, high-confidence correction – multiply by ten, or divide by ten – that lands the value squarely in the 68–100mm range every other student's measurement falls in. Dropping instead would throw away five of 24 students, over 20% of the class, along with their perfectly good reaction-time data, for a problem that's actually fixable. So we impute: we replace the bad values with our best, justified estimate of the true ones.

Why does it matter? Use the figure below to compare the scatter, the covariance and the correlation (both defined later in this chapter) before and after the fix.

A value that's merely unusual, with no diagnosable cause, is a better candidate for dropping – or for Chapter 1's “keep it, it's a genuine feature of the process” – than for guessing a replacement. That's a big part of why this module teaches you to do things in code: a script is a record.

Part Ten

Binning Finger Length

With the data fixed, back to the conditional mean. The class dataset for this chapter (week3_students.csv, one row per student) already carries the corrected finger lengths and a finger_bin column splitting the class into three roughly equal groups: short, medium and long. The in-class task was to compute the mean reaction time within each bin – by PivotTable (remember to switch the default Sum to Average), by AVERAGEIF, or, for one student racing ahead, by a pandas groupby in a single line.

Three binning decisions are hiding in that column, all of them choices rather than facts: bin students, not individual trials (each student has one finger length); use bins with roughly equal numbers of students rather than equal millimetre widths, so no bin ends up nearly empty; and use three of them. Part Eleven

Scatterplots, Bin-Scatters and Logs

Binning simplifies a relationship by throwing detail away. When both variables are continuous, the alternative is to keep everything and plot it: a scatterplot puts one variable on each axis and draws one dot per observation. It's one of the most powerful tools you have for seeing whether a relationship exists – no formula required, just look. (Unlike a histogram, which shows the distribution of one variable, every dot here is one observation's pair of values.)

Before you build one from our finger-length data, here's a simulated, illustrative case – not our own class: hours of sleep the night before, against reaction time, for 152 made-up trials. Start with the raw scatter.

Sleep and reaction time, three ways (simulated data)

View

On the raw scatter, most people in the room saw very little – the cloud looks fairly flat. There is a real pattern in there, but two extreme “attention lapse” trials (everyone has them, however well-rested) stretch the top of the axis so far that the main cluster gets squashed into a thin band at the bottom.

The bin-scatter

One fix is the bin-scatter: the conditional mean again, like the short/medium/long finger buckets, but with more and narrower bins – here, one-hour bands of sleep. Switch the view to see it. Each bin needs to be wide enough to hold several trials; group by every exact sleep value and most “bins” would contain a single trial, which averages away nothing. Here every bin averages at least a dozen trials, so a lapse can nudge a bin's mean but can no longer dominate the chart, and a clear downward trend appears: more sleep, quicker reactions. It's also a very easy chart to produce in Python once you get there.

Logging the outcome

The other fix goes back to Chapter 1, which found that reaction time looks more log-normal than normal: a floor, no ceiling, a long right tail. Taking the log of a variable like that stretches out the crowded low end and compresses the tail. Switch to the logged view: the same two lapse trials are still there – logging doesn't delete anything – but they barely stretch the axis, and the downward relationship is much easier to see.

The number printed on each chart is R², which you'll meet properly next chapter: roughly, how much of the variation in y the fitted line accounts for – a measure of how well the model fits the data. Logging raises it from about 0.08 to about 0.35, more than four times better. That's a signal that logging is a sensible modelling choice here. It's a tool, not a rule: it helps only because it genuinely improves the fit, and logging can't conjure a relationship that isn't there.

Part Thirteen

Covariance

Scatterplots show a relationship; we'd also like to put a number on it. The first such number is covariance, a fancy name for what we've been looking at all chapter. It asks whether x and y tend to move together: when one is above its own average, is the other usually above its average too?

$$Cov[x,y] = \frac{\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{n-1}$$

Read it from the inside out. For each observation \(i\), work out how far its x is from the mean of x, \(\bar{x}\) (the bar always means “the average”), and how far its y is from \(\bar{y}\). Multiply those two deviations together. Add up all the products, and divide by \(n-1\) – the same small-sample correction Chapter 1 used for the standard deviation, for the same reason.

The sign of each product is where the meaning lives. If a student is above average on both x and y, both deviations are positive and so is their product. Below average on both: two negatives, still a positive product. Above on one and below on the other: a negative product. Covariance is the average of all of these, so it comes out positive when x and y tend to deviate from their means in the same direction, and negative when they tend to go opposite ways.

The most common slip: summing too soon. Add up the \((x_i - \bar{x})\) column on its own and you'll always get zero – deviations from a mean cancel out by definition. Several people hit exactly this in the room. Multiply each row's two deviations first, then add up the products.

This is also, it's fair to say, the last time you should need to compute a covariance by hand. From here it's one cell in Excel or one line of Python. The point of doing it slowly once is to know what that one line means.

Part Fourteen

Correlation

The correlation coefficient asks exactly the same question as covariance – do x and y move together? – but reports the answer on a fixed scale from −1 to 1, so it's comparable whatever units x and y are in. It gets there by standardising the covariance: dividing by the product of the two standard deviations, which strips the units back out.

$$Corr[x,y] = \frac{Cov[x,y]}{SD[x]\,SD[y]}$$ $$-1 \leq Corr[x,y] \leq 1$$
  • +1Perfect positive correlation: on a scatter, every point sits on one upward-sloping straight line.
  • −1Perfect negative correlation: a straight line sloping the other way.
  • 0No linear association at all (that qualifier matters – see Part Sixteen).

A rough guide: below about 0.2 in size is weak, above about 0.7 generally strong, with the same thresholds for negative values. Both covariance and correlation measure the direction and strength of a linear association – how far the two variables move together along a straight line. Strictly, this version is Pearson's correlation coefficient.

Back to the five students. Square each deviation from the last table and add them up: 40 for x, and 40 for y too. Divide by \(n - 1 = 4\) and take the square root, and both standard deviations come out at \(\sqrt{10} \approx 3.16\). So:

$$Corr[x,y] = \frac{8}{\sqrt{10} \times \sqrt{10}} = \frac{8}{10} = 0.8$$

Above 0.7: a strong positive association – with the obvious caveat that five observations wouldn't persuade anyone to bet the farm on it.

Now our class. Divide the covariance of 1,359.6 by the two standard deviations and you get 0.18: weak, and a numerical confirmation of what the scatter already suggested. The simulated hours-awake class, by contrast, has a covariance of 267.7 and standard deviations of 4.43 hours and 68.0ms:

$$Corr = \frac{267.7}{4.43 \times 68.0} \approx 0.89$$

Strong, and matching what your eye saw on the scatter. Which brings us back to HR. Whatever number comes back for our class, is it strong enough, on its own, to justify replacing a ten-trial reaction test with a ruler? At 0.18, from 24 people, clearly not.

Part Sixteen

Dependence, Mean-Dependence, Correlation

Two variables are associated whenever they're not independent. But association comes in different forms, and correlation only sees one of them:

  • IndependentThe whole distribution of y is the same whatever the value of x. Knowing x tells you nothing about y. (For caffeine and reaction time, with a big enough sample, the two histograms would be identical.)
  • Mean-dependentThe average of y changes with x: \(E[y \mid x]\) isn't flat. This is what covariance and correlation pick up – but only its linear part.
  • Dependent, not in the meanThe average of y stays the same, but something else about y changes with x – its spread, for instance.

The in-class task: for each panel below, does knowing x tell you anything about y? And what would you guess the correlation is? Turn on the conditional mean, \(E[y \mid x]\), to help.

Only panel 1 is independent. Panel 2 is the familiar linear case, correlation 0.82. Panels 3 and 4 are the interesting ones: both have a correlation of exactly zero, and yet x clearly tells you something about y. In panel 3 – a U-shape, or as it was put on the day, a “Joker smile” – the average changes with x, just not along a straight line. In panel 4 the average stays flat but the spread changes, narrow in the middle and wide at the edges. That second pattern has a name you'll meet in a few weeks, heteroskedasticity, and it matters a great deal for whether a regression's standard errors can be trusted.

Part Seventeen

Is It Causal?

All chapter we've stayed carefully away from the hot stove: when, if ever, can we say x causes y? The answer depends on where the variation in x comes from.

Every comparison in this chapter needs variation in the conditioning variable. If everyone in the class had used a laptop, there'd be no room to split; if everyone had the same finger length, no scatter to draw. And the source of that variation determines what you're allowed to conclude.

  • Experimental dataThe treatment is assigned, often randomly, in a way designed so that differences in y can only be attributed to the treatment. Randomly assign aspirin or a placebo, and a difference in outcomes is the aspirin's doing.
  • Observational datax varies because of people's own choices, circumstances and histories, and plenty of other things vary alongside it. People who already take aspirin routinely may do so because of an existing health risk.

Almost everything in business analytics is observational: we watch what people and firms do, rather than assigning it. People are messy, they make choices for all sorts of reasons, and lots of other things change at the same time as x. Causality is the gold standard – often an expensive one, requiring a properly designed experiment – and later in the module we'll see how firms run exactly those experiments, as A/B tests. Until then, we work in a world of association, and the job is to describe it in the right language.

Source: Békés & Kézdi (2021), Ch. 4
Part Eighteen

AI, and What's Next

Generative AI is genuinely useful for a first pass over a new dataset – spotting candidate patterns quickly, the way a profiling script would. Where it's weaker is judgement: deciding whether a correlation is meaningfully weak or strong, and knowing which pattern is worth pursuing in the first place. That judgement is the skill this module is building, and it's yours.

From the workshop

“At no point should you be outsourcing your judgement, your understanding or your decision-making to the little robot friend. Do the hard yards. Bang your head against the brick wall. Later, you'll have the confidence to hand off coding and check an agent's work – but you won't be able to hand off your own judgement.”

This chapter's online session is a recorded walkthrough of the textbook's own case study – are bigger firms better managed? – using real data on Mexican firms, done step by step in Python. Everything here, from conditional probabilities to correlation, gets applied to real data, and the half-hour calculations from the workshop turn into single lines of code.

We can now describe a relationship between two variables. Next chapter turns that description into a model we can use to predict and interpret: simple linear regression, fitted by ordinary least squares.
